# My employer auto-enrolled me in a pension — is that okay?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

## Answer

**Being enrolled is not the problem, and opting out is usually the wrong response. What matters is where the money is invested — and in most workplace schemes that is something you can change without giving up the pension.**

A workplace pension is three things stacked together: deferred wages of your own, a contribution your employer adds, and a fund manager who invests the total on your behalf. Those separate cleanly into three questions the books do address.

**First: money that arrives without your asking for it.**

The employer contribution is money you did not request and that appears because of an arrangement you did not design. That is a recognised category, and the guidance is favourable. Ibn Taymiyya cites the instruction given to 'Umar: `ما أتاك من هذا المال وأنت غير سائل ولا مشرف فخذه ومالا فلا تتبعه نفسك` [1] — whatever of this wealth comes to you **while you are neither asking nor looking out for it, take it**; and what does not, do not let yourself go after.

He draws the distinction out: `فتبين بهذين الحديثين أن الانسان إذا كان سائلا بلسانه او مشرفا إلى ما يعطاه فلا ينبغي أن يقبله إلا حيث تباح له المسالة والاستشراف` [2] — one who asks with his tongue or fixes his hopes on what he may be given should not accept except where asking is permitted to him. But: `وأما إذا أتاه من غير مسألة ولا إشراف فله أخذه` [3] — where it comes without asking and without looking for it, **he may take it**; and `فان قبله وكان من غير إشراف له عليه فقد أحسن` [4] — if he accepts it in that state, he has done well.

Auto-enrolment is close to the paradigm case. This does not settle the investment question, but it does dispose of the instinct that money arriving automatically is suspect merely for arriving automatically.

**Second: someone else managing your money.**

That relationship is `وكالة`, and it is not a grudging permission. Al-Sarakhsi describes both the contract and precisely the need it meets: `فالتوكيل تفويض التصرف إلى الغير وتسليم المال إليه ليتصرف فيه ثم للناس إلى هذا العقد حاجة ماسة فقد يعجز الانسان عن حفظ ماله عند خروجه للسفر وقد يعجز عن التصرف في ماله لقلة هدايته وكثره اشتغاله أو لكثرة ماله فيحتاج إلى تفويض التصرف إلى الغير بطريق الوكالة` [5] — agency is entrusting disposition to another and handing over the property so he may act in it; **people have a pressing need for this contract**, since a person may be unable to manage his wealth through lack of expertise, or being much occupied, or having a great deal of it — so he needs to entrust its handling to another by way of agency.

Lack of expertise and being too busy: that is the case for a pension fund manager, stated in the eleventh century. `وقد عرف جواز هذا العقد بالكتاب والسنة` [6] — the permissibility of this contract is established by the Book and the Sunna.

So neither the enrolment nor the delegation is the difficulty.

**Third: what the fund actually holds. This is the real question, and it is where your attention belongs.**

A default pension fund typically holds shares across a whole market, along with government and corporate bonds. Two things follow.

Shares in companies doing lawful business are the straightforward case, and profit from a real venture is exactly what the books call `ربح`: `الربح هو ما يكتسبه المرء زائدا على قيمة معوضه` [7] — what a person gains over and above the value of what he gave in exchange. That is trade, and it is on the permitted side.

Bonds are not that. A bond is a loan on which a fixed return is contracted, and the maxim applies without any need for interpretation: `كل قرض جر منفعة فهو ربا` [8] — every loan drawing a benefit is usury. A default fund's bond allocation is the part of a pension that most directly raises the objection, and it is also the part most people never look at.

The mixed shareholdings raise the same question addressed on the page about stocks — the measure of tainted income is estimated and given away, and the ruling follows the predominant. That principle applies here too, and the practical implication is the same.

**What is genuinely absent from these books.**

Nothing here addresses compulsory enrolment by statute, or a default fund chosen for you that you must actively act to change, or the fact that opting out in most schemes means **forfeiting the employer's contribution entirely** — which is a straightforward financial loss, often the largest single component of the benefit. Nothing weighs that loss against the taint in a mixed fund, because no author faced the choice. Nor is there anything on annuities, on state top-ups, or on what happens to the fund after your death, which is an inheritance question your scheme's rules may not answer in the way the law of inheritance would.

**What to do.**

1. **Do not opt out as a first move.** You would be giving up deferred wages and your employer's contribution to solve a problem that is usually solvable another way.
2. **Check the fund options.** Most workplace schemes now include a sharia-compliant or Islamic equity fund among their choices, and switching your fund is normally a form or a few clicks — it does not affect enrolment or the employer contribution. If your scheme has one, that is the answer to nearly all of this.
3. **If there is no compliant option, ask for one.** Providers add funds when members request them, and you may not be the only person asking.
4. **If you remain in a mixed default fund**, apply the purification approach set out on the stocks page: estimate the impermissible portion of the returns and dispose of it, following the procedure on the page about interest money.
5. **Look specifically at the bond allocation**, since that is the clearest issue and is often adjustable separately from the equity holdings.

If your scheme genuinely offers nothing compliant and opting out would cost you a substantial employer contribution, that is a real weighing of harms and it is the kind of question to put to a scholar with your scheme's actual fund list in front of them — not a reason to decide it alone in either direction.
