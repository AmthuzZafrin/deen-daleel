# Is vanilla extract haram because of the alcohol?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

## Answer

**This site will not rule on vanilla extract, because the answer turns on a
classification step the classical sources never took: whether industrially
produced ethanol used as a solvent is *khamr* at all.** What the sources do give
you is the reasoning on either side of that step, and it is unusually
well-developed.

**What the sources are actually about.** The prohibition attaches to *khamr* — a
drink made to be drunk. Ibn Taymiyya on using it otherwise: **"لا يجوز التداوي
بالخمر وغيرها من الخبائث"** — *it is not permitted to seek treatment with wine or
other foul things* [1], citing the man who told the Prophet ﷺ he made it only as
medicine and was answered: **"انه ليس بدواء ولكنه داء"** — *it is not a medicine;
it is a disease* [2]. And Ibn Mas'ud's line: **"ان الله لم يجعل شفاءكم فيما حرم
عليكم"** — *Allah has not placed your cure in what He has forbidden you* [3].

**So a non-drink use does not automatically escape.** That is the strong side of
the argument and it should not be waved away.

**The other side: the predominance test.** Al-Jassas argues from how the case is
judged: **"بدلالة أن قطرة من خمر لو وقعت في حق ماء فشرب منه إنسان لم يقل إنه شارب
خمر ولا يجب عليه الحد"** — *by the evidence that if a drop of wine fell into a
vessel of water and a person drank from it, he would not be called a wine-drinker,
and no prescribed penalty would be due upon him* [4].

**And *Bada'i al-Sana'i* gives the most precise statement of the problem
anywhere in this material** — a two-level answer that is worth reading twice:

**"ولو شرب خمر ممزوجا بالماء إن كانت الغلبة للخمر يجب الحد وان غلب الماء عليها
حتى زال طعمها وريحها لا يجب لان الغلبة إذا كانت للخمر فقد بقي اسم الخمر ومعناها
وإذا كانت الغلبة للماء فقد زال الاسم والمعنى الا أنه يحرم شرب الماء الممزوج
بالخمر لما فيه من أجزاء الخمر حقيقة"** — *if someone drinks wine mixed with
water: if the wine predominates, the penalty is due; and if the water overcomes it
so that its taste and smell are gone, the penalty is not due — because when the
wine predominates the **name** of wine and its meaning remain, and when the water
predominates the name and the meaning are gone. **However**, drinking water mixed
with wine is forbidden, because of the parts of wine actually in it* [5].

**Two different judgements about one liquid.** It is no longer *khamr* — the name
and the legal meaning have gone, so the penalty does not apply. And it is still
forbidden to drink, because wine parts are physically present. Anyone who quotes
only half of that sentence is misrepresenting the school.

The same passage forecloses one shortcut: **"وكذا يحرم شرب الخمر المطبوخ لان
الطبخ لا يحل حراما"** — *and likewise drinking cooked wine is forbidden, because
cooking does not make the forbidden lawful* [6]. And it shows the naming test
working in another direction: **"ولو شرب دردى الخمر لا حد عليه الا إذا سكر لأنه لا
يسمى خمرا"** — *if he drinks the lees of wine there is no penalty upon him unless
he becomes intoxicated, because it is not called wine* [7].

**Notice the pattern.** The jurists ask two separate questions — *is this thing
called khamr?* and *are wine-parts present?* — and answer them independently.
Modern arguments about vanilla extract usually collapse them.

---

**Why this site will not decide it.**

1. **Is the ethanol in it khamr?** Extract solvent is typically not a fermented
   drink made for drinking; it is a chemical used to carry flavour. Whether that
   falls under the name the prohibition attaches to is precisely the
   classification the sources above did not perform, because the substance in that
   form did not exist for them.
2. **If it is, does the predominance reasoning apply?** *Bada'i*'s answer would be
   that the name has gone but the parts remain — a position that does not resolve
   into a simple yes or no for a food ingredient.
3. **Does the amount matter?** The remnant in a baked good is different again from
   the bottle on the shelf, and no passage above measures it for you.

**What to do.**

- **Ask the body or scholar you follow**, and give them the actual facts: what the
  solvent is, how much survives, and what the product is. That is the form of
  question the *Bada'i* passage is designed to answer.
- **Know that this splits along school lines** for the same reason the gelatin
  question does — see *Is gelatin halal?* — and that both sides are held
  seriously.
- **Alcohol-free extracts and vanilla powder exist**, and using one is the
  simplest way to make the question not arise. That costs you nothing.
- **Do not treat someone else's different conclusion as laxity.** The passages
  above are the reason the disagreement exists.
- **And keep the scale straight.** *Bada'i* itself notes that even the strict
  ruling on wine relaxes under genuine necessity: **"الا أنه رخص شربها عند ضرورة
  العطش أو لاكراه قدر ما تندفع به الضرورة"** — *except that drinking it was
  permitted in the necessity of thirst, or under compulsion, to the extent that
  repels the necessity* [8]. A tradition that makes room for that is not one that
  intends you to be paralysed by a teaspoon of flavouring.

There is no version of this question that this site will settle for you.
