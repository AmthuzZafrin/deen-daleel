# The days are 20 hours where I live — what do I do?

## Answer

Start with what the corpus actually settles, because it is narrower than you might hope. The times of the fast are fixed to the sun where you are, not to a clock. Al-Marghinani: **the time of the fast is from the rising of the second dawn until the setting of the sun** [1], and al-Quduri's text is word for word the same [2]. Both derive it from the verse — **eat and drink until the white thread becomes distinct to you from the black thread** [3]. So a twenty-hour day is, on the face of the texts, a twenty-hour fast. There is no classical rule that caps the fast at some number of hours, or that lets you borrow Mecca's timings, because the authors of these books never encountered a latitude where the question arises.

What the corpus does give you is the concession for harm, and that is the door most people at high latitudes go through. Al-Quduri: **whoever is ill in Ramadan and fears that fasting will increase his illness breaks the fast and makes it up** [4]. Ibn Qudama defines the threshold — **the illness that permits breaking the fast is the severe one that fasting increases, or where a slowing of recovery is feared** [5] — and then extends it past actual illness to anticipated illness: **a healthy person who fears becoming ill through fasting is like the sick person who fears his illness increasing, as regards the permissibility of breaking the fast** [6]. He goes further still, to danger without illness at all: **one who fears destruction for himself from thirst or the like** [7], which is precisely the failure mode of a very long summer day.

That is the honest state of the evidence. It gives you a principle — real, expected harm licenses breaking the fast, and the days are made up later — but it does not give you a rule for the case you are in, because the case is modern. The question of whether a person at 60° north follows their own horizon, or the timings of the nearest place with a normal day, or Mecca's, is a matter contemporary scholars have worked out and disagreed over, and their reasoning is not in this corpus. If that is your situation, this page cannot settle it; a scholar working in your country, who knows your latitude and has ruled on it before, can.

What you can take from here meanwhile: try the fast, watch honestly for real harm rather than mere difficulty — the texts are careful to require severity, not discomfort — and if harm arrives, break the fast, make the day up, and do not treat that as a failure. Al-Quduri pairs the concession with the obligation in one clause for a reason: you break, and you make it up.
