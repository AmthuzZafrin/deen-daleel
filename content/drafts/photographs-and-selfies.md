# Are photographs haram?

> Reviewer note: Contested. Retrieval leans prohibitive on taswir; photography did not exist when any of it was written. Present the range.


## Answer

No source in front of us is about photography. Every ruling below was written centuries before a camera existed, about someone *making* a likeness by hand. Whether a photograph is that thing is the whole question, and it is a question the sources never faced. So here is what they actually said, and then what it does and does not settle.

**The prohibition, stated at its strongest.**

Al-Nawawi: "our companions and other scholars said: making an image of an animate being is forbidden with a severe prohibition, and it is among the major sins, because it is threatened with this severe threat mentioned in the hadiths. And whether one makes it for something that will be degraded or for otherwise, making it is forbidden in every case — **because in it is an imitation of the creation of God Most High**" [1].

That last clause is the reason, and everything turns on it.

The prohibition is limited to living things: "as for making an image of a tree, or camel saddles, or anything else that is not the image of an animate being — that is not forbidden" [2].

**Why the punishment is severe — the sources say so plainly.**

Al-Khattabi: "the punishment of the image-maker was made grave only because **images used to be worshipped besides God**, and because looking at them causes temptation, and some souls incline toward them. He said: and what is meant by images here is statues that have a spirit" [3].

Ibn Hajar makes the same point about the origin of the rule: the prohibition is intensified for what is worshipped besides God, "for it imitates the form of the idols, **which are the origin of the prohibition of image-making**" [4]. And from the church-in-Abyssinia hadith, he concludes: "this indicates that making images of animals is an innovated act, invented by the worshippers of images" [5].

Three separate places in the commentary give the same *'illa*: idolatry, and imitation of the act of creating.

**And that reason is used to grade the sin.**

Ibn Hajar: "some restricted the severe threat to one who makes an image *intending to imitate* — for by that intention he becomes a disbeliever... As for anyone other than him, it is forbidden to him and he sins, **but his sin is below the sin of the one who imitates**" [6].

So even among those who prohibit, the threat texts are read as landing hardest on intention. A person photographing their child is not, on anybody's reading, in the category the threat describes.

**The licence, and how wide it is.**

There is a well-attested exception. Ibn Hajar: "this hadith was used as evidence for the permissibility of keeping images when they cast no shadow and are, along with that, something trodden on and walked over, or degraded by use — like bolsters and cushions" [7]. And: "al-Nawawi said: this is the position of the majority of scholars among the Companions and the Successors, and it is the position of al-Thawri, Malik, Abu Hanifa and al-Shafi'i" — with the qualification that "if it is hung on a wall, or worn, or a turban, or the like of what is not counted as degraded, then it is forbidden" [8].

Al-Nawawi's own summary separates two questions cleanly — the *making* and the *keeping*: "as for keeping something in which an animate image is made: if it is hung on a wall, or a worn garment or a turban and the like of what is not counted as degraded, it is forbidden; and if it is on a rug that is trodden, or a bolster, or a cushion, and the like of what is degraded — it is not forbidden" [9].

And it was practised. Ibn Sirin, Salim b. 'Abdullah, 'Ikrima b. Khalid and Sa'id b. Jubayr "said there is no harm in an image if it is trodden on; and by the route of 'Urwa, that he used to lean on cushions **on which were figures of birds and men**" [10].

'Urwa b. al-Zubayr leaning on a cushion embroidered with men is not a marginal report. It is one of the most careful of the Successors, in Medina, living with images of people in his house.

**How wide is the disagreement about flat images?**

Ibn Hajar quotes Ibn al-'Arabi's summary, and it is the most useful single paragraph in this file: "the sum of what is in keeping images is that if they have bodies, it is forbidden by consensus; and if they are flat markings, there are four positions. **First**, permitted absolutely, on the apparent sense of his words *except a marking in a cloth*. **Second**, forbidden absolutely, even the flat marking. **Third**, if the image remains in its form, standing in shape, it is forbidden, and if the head is cut off or the parts are separated, it is permitted — he said, and this is the soundest. **Fourth**, if it is something degraded it is permitted, and if it is hung it is not" [11].

Four positions. Three-dimensional statues are the agreed case; flat images are not.

**So what about a photograph?**

Nothing above decides it, and anyone who tells you otherwise is filling in a gap. What the sources give you is a reason — imitation of creating, and the road to worship — and a set of distinctions: shadow-casting versus flat, degraded versus honoured, made-to-imitate versus made for a use.

Both of the honest readings are available to you:

- **A photograph is a captured shadow, not a fashioned likeness.** No one has "imitated the creation of God"; a lens has recorded light. On this reading the *'illa* al-Khattabi and Ibn Hajar identified is simply absent, and with it the ruling. This is the reasoning behind the widespread permission of photography for identification, documentation, news and family record.
- **A photograph is an image of an animate being, and the texts are general.** On this reading the flat-image dispute applies to it, and you would land somewhere among Ibn al-'Arabi's four positions — most likely the fourth, which permits what is not hung and honoured.

Notice that even the stricter of the two paths does not reach a passport photo, a phone camera roll, or a picture of your family — those are stored and used, not displayed for admiration. What it reaches is the framed portrait on the wall, and there the classical rule is explicit.

**Practically.**

- **Photographs for a purpose** — identity documents, work, recording your life, keeping your family's faces — are outside the case the sources describe on either reading. Do not let anyone burden you about a driving licence.
- **The one thing everyone in the file agrees on is a statue.** A free-standing three-dimensional figure of a person or animal is forbidden by consensus, per Ibn al-'Arabi. That is not disputed by anybody.
- **Displaying a large portrait for veneration** — of a person, alive or dead, in a place of honour — is what the reasoning is actually about, and it is the one modern case that lands squarely inside the classical prohibition.
- **The prayer space is a separate and simpler matter.** 'A'isha had a cloth with images stretched across an alcove, and the Prophet صلى الله عليه وسلم prayed facing it and said: "put it away from me" [12]. Ibn Hajar draws the point from the chapter itself: the objection is that images "distract the one praying when they face him" [13]. That is about attention, not impurity — so keep images out of where you pray, for the reason he gave.
- **If your school prohibits, follow it.** The majority position that al-Nawawi reports is a real one held by real imams, and following it costs you nothing but a habit.

Nothing in the sources makes taking a photograph a major sin, and nothing in them makes it plainly permitted either. The person telling you it is definitely one or the other is telling you about themselves.
