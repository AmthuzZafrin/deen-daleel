# What do I do with interest my bank already paid me?

## Answer

**Take it out and give it away — to people in need or to public benefit — without counting it as charity you earn reward for. Do not leave it with the bank, and do not destroy it. That is the settled procedure in these books, and they set it out step by step for exactly this situation.**

**The category you are in is named explicitly.** Ibn Taymiyya, on wealth that cannot be returned to its owners: `فهذه الأموال التى تعذر ردها إلى أهلها لعدم العلم بهم مثلا هي مما يصرف في مصالح المسلمين عند أكثر العلماء وكذلك من كان عنده مال لا يعرف صاحبه كالغاصب التائب والخائن التائب والمرابى التائب ونحوهم ممن صار بيده مال لا يملكه ولايعرف صاحبه` [1] — such wealth is spent on the general interests of the Muslims, in the view of most scholars; **and likewise anyone holding money whose owner he does not know — the repentant usurper, the repentant embezzler, and the repentant usurer** — anyone who has come to hold money he does not own and whose owner he does not know. His instruction: `يصرفه إلى ذوى الحاجات ومصالح المسلمين` [2] — he spends it on those in need and on public benefit.

The repentant usurer is in that list by name. You are not improvising a category.

**The full procedure, in order.** Al-Ghazali's steps, as al-Nawawi records them:

`إذا كان معه مال حرام وأراد التوبة والبراءة منه فإن كان له مالك معين وجب صرفه إليه أو إلى وكيله فإن كان ميتا وجب دفعه إلى وارثه` [3] — if there is a **determinate owner**, it must go to him, or his agent, or his heir if he has died.

`وإن كان لمالك لا يعرفه ويئس من معرفته فينبغي أن يصرفه في مصالح المسلمين العامة كالقناطر والربط والمساجد ومصالح طريق مكة ونحو ذلك مما يشترك المسلمون فيه والا فيتصدق به على فقير أو فقراء` [4] — if the owner is unknown and there is no hope of identifying him, it should be spent on **general public benefit** — bridges, hostels, mosques, the upkeep of the road to Mecca, and the like in which people share — or else given to a poor person or poor people.

**And the reason nothing else will do:** `لأنه لا يجوز اتلاف هذا المال ورميه في البحر فلم يبق إلا صرفه في مصالح المسلمين` [5] — **it is not permitted to destroy this money and throw it into the sea; so nothing remains but to spend it on the interests of the Muslims.**

That sentence answers the two wrong instincts at once. Burning it or refusing to withdraw it is not piety — it is wasting wealth, which is itself objectionable. And leaving it with the bank is not neutral: it is leaving it where it will be lent at interest again.

**A point that relieves a common anxiety.** `وإذا دفعه إلى الفقير لا يكون حراما على الفقير بل يكون حلالا طيبا` [6] — when it is handed to the poor person **it is not forbidden to him; it is lawful and wholesome for him.**

So you are not passing a contamination on. The taint is in your acquiring it, not in the coins. This is why the money can properly be used for real needs rather than only for things that feel "safe."

**If you do not know how much it is.** `فان علم أن فيه حراما وشك في قدره اخرج قدر الحرام بالاجتهاد` [7] — if he knows there is something unlawful in it but is uncertain of the amount, he takes out the unlawful measure **by considered estimation**. You are not required to reconstruct a decade of statements to the penny. Estimate carefully and honestly, and err on the generous side.

**On what you have already spent or consumed.** The relevant precedent is 'A'isha's exchange with a woman who had entered a transaction 'A'isha regarded as usurious. When the woman asked what if she took back only her capital, the answer given was the verse: `فمن جاءه موعظة من ربه فانتهى فله ما سلف` [8] — whoever receives an admonition from his Lord and desists, **what has already passed is his**.

Take your principal; stop; and what preceded your stopping is not a debt hanging over you. That is the shape of the remedy throughout this chapter, and it applies to you: the obligation is to divest what you are holding now and stop the flow, not to reconstruct and repay years of past living.

**Two things worth guarding against.**

The first is scrupulosity about everything else you own. Ibn 'Abidin notes that once such money has been mixed into a person's wealth, the wealth becomes his; what remains forbidden is `لا يباح الانتفاع به قبل أداء البدل في الصحيح من المذهب` [9] — benefiting from it **before discharging its equivalent**. Discharge the equivalent and the matter is closed; do not treat your savings as permanently poisoned.

The second is the drift into thinking nothing is clean any more. Ibn Taymiyya answers that directly and calls it an error against consensus: `الحلال هو الغالب على أموال الناس وهو اكثر من الحرام` [10] — **the lawful predominates in people's wealth and is more than the unlawful.** And on inherited money of unknown provenance: `من ورث مالا ولم يعلم من أين كسبه مورثه أمن حلال أم حرام ولم تكن علامة فهو حلال باجماع العلماء` [11] — lawful **by the consensus of the scholars**, where there is no indication otherwise.

**What to do, concretely.**

1. **Find the amount.** Most banks will show interest credited over a period. If the records do not go back far enough, estimate and be generous.
2. **Withdraw it and give it away.** To people in genuine need, or to public benefit of the kind named above — the modern equivalents of bridges and hostels are not hard to identify. Charities providing food, shelter, medical care or debt relief all fit.
3. **Do not count it as your charity.** It is not a donation you are rewarded for; it is disposal of what is not yours. Give your actual charity separately, from clean money. This distinction is the reason people are told not to intend reward by it — the act is divesting, not giving.
4. **It cannot be used for zakat.** Zakat is a purification of wealth you own; this is money you are getting rid of. Keep the two separate.
5. **Stop the flow.** Switch to an account that does not pay interest, or ask the bank to disable it. Disposing of it every year without changing the account leaves the underlying problem in place.

If your situation is unusual — a very large sum, money entangled with a business, or a case where an actual identifiable person was wronged rather than a bank — take it to a scholar. The first step in al-Ghazali's list exists for a reason: where there is a determinate owner, giving the money to charity is not the answer; returning it is.
