# Is car finance allowed?

## Answer

**It depends entirely on which of two things the agreement is: a loan on which you pay a stipulated increase, or a sale of a car to you at a fixed higher price payable over time. The first is the thing the prohibition names. The second is an ordinary deferred sale. Most products called "car finance" are the first, and most called "Islamic car finance" claim to be the second — and the way to tell them apart is stated in these books.**

**The rule for the loan case.** Al-Nawawi puts the criterion in one clause: `لأن المنهي عنه ما كان مشروطا في عقد القرض` [1] — what is prohibited is **what was stipulated in the loan contract**. A personal loan or hire-purchase agreement with an APR is a stipulated increase on money lent. The presence of a car in the arrangement does not change what the money is doing.

**The rule for the sale case — and the tests it has to pass.** If a financier genuinely buys the car and sells it to you at a marked-up price payable in instalments, that is a deferred sale, permitted on the reasoning set out on the pages about instalments and mortgages. But the classical materials impose conditions, and they are not decorative.

`لا تبع ما ليس عندك` [2] — **do not sell what you do not have.** Al-Nawawi's reason: `ولان ما لا يملكه لا يقدر على تسليمه فهو كالطير في الهواء أو السمك في الماء` [3] — what a man does not own he cannot deliver, so it is like a bird in the air or a fish in the water. And ownership is listed among the requirements of a valid sale: `أنه سبق أن شروط المبيع خمسة منها أن يكون مملوكا لمن يقع العقد له فان باشر العقد لنفسه فشرطه كونه مالكا للعين` [4] — among the five conditions of the object of sale is that it be owned by the one the contract is for. Selling another's property without authority: `فلو باع مال غيره بغير إذن ولا ولاية فقولان (الصحيح) أن العقد باطل` [5] — the sound view is that the contract is void.

There is also a possession requirement: `من ابتاع بيعا فلا يبعه حتى يقبضه` [6] — whoever buys something should not sell it until he has taken possession of it.

And the cluster of prohibitions that finance structures most often run into: `ولا شرطان في بيع، ولا ربح ما لم يضمن، ولا بيع ما ليس عندك` [7] — **no two conditions in one sale, no profit on what one has not borne the risk of, and no selling what one does not have.**

`لا ربح ما لم يضمن` is the sharpest of these for your purposes. Profit is earned by bearing risk. A financier who never owns the car, never carries the risk of its loss, and simply collects an increase on money advanced has not sold you anything — it has lent to you.

**So the questions to put to any "Islamic" car finance product.**

- **Does the financier actually buy the car?** Not appear in the paperwork for an instant, but acquire it.
- **Does it bear any risk while it owns it?** If the car were destroyed between its purchase and its sale to you, who bears the loss? If the answer is always you, `لا ربح ما لم يضمن` is the objection.
- **Is the total price fixed and stated?** A deferred sale needs a determinate price; a figure recalculated against a published interest rate is a different animal.
- **What happens if you pay late?** An increase added to the outstanding sum is a stipulated increase on a debt, whatever the contract calls it.
- **Are two transactions bound together** — a sale conditional on a resale, or a third party appearing only to complete a circle? That is the `شرطان في بيع` case and the `عينة` discussion on the mortgage page.

**A note on leasing.** A hire agreement, where you pay for the use of a car that someone else owns and bears the risk of, is a different contract again — hire rather than sale — and is unproblematic in itself. The complications arise where a lease is engineered to transfer ownership at the end for a token sum, since that makes it a sale wearing the clothes of a lease. Whether a given lease-to-own product is genuinely one or the other is a question about that contract.

**What the corpus does not address.** It has nothing on balloon payments, on personal contract purchase, on manufacturer-subsidised zero-percent offers, or on gap arrangements bundled into the finance. A zero-percent offer where the cash price and the credit price are identical is, on the analysis above, simply a deferred sale at a fixed price — but check whether a "cash discount" you are giving up is really the interest reappearing under another name.

**What to do.**

If you can buy the car outright, do. If you cannot, the honest sequence is: look for a genuine deferred-sale or leasing product from a provider that actually takes ownership and risk; failing that, buy a cheaper car you can pay for; and take any product you are seriously considering, with its contract, to a scholar who will read the terms rather than the brochure.

The single question that does most of the work is the one the tradition asks: **is anyone selling you a car, or is someone lending you money?**
