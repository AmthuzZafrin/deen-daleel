# Is buying followers or writing fake reviews haram?

## Answer

**There is a hadith that lands on bought followers so squarely it barely needs an argument, and a separate one that lands on fake reviews.** Neither is a stretch, and the second one names an offence people were already committing in the marketplaces of Madina.

**Buying followers: pretending to have been given what you were not.**

A woman asked the Prophet ﷺ whether she might claim her husband had given her more than he had — a small vanity among co-wives. The answer: `المتشبع بما لم يعط، كلابس ثوبي زور` [1] — the one who affects to be sated with what he was not given is like one who wears two garments of falsehood.

Muslim files it in a chapter on `التزوير في اللباس` — counterfeiting in dress and the rest. The offence is not lying about a fact so much as *dressing yourself in a standing you do not have*. A follower count you paid for is that, exactly and without adaptation. So is an engagement number, a fabricated testimonial, and a badge you bought.

Nothing in the hadith turns on whether anyone was cheated out of money. She was not selling anything. It is about the display.

**Fake reviews: praising goods with what is not in them.**

Ibn 'Umar reports a prohibition that al-Tamhid records with a striking gloss: `نهى عن التحبير والتحبير أن يمدح الرجل سلعته بما ليس فيها` [2] — he forbade *tahbir*, and tahbir is that a man praises his merchandise with what is not in it. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr notes the better-known name for the practice: `وإنما المعروف النجش` [3] — najsh.

That is a five-star review for a product that does not deserve one, written or paid for by the seller. The classical form was a man in the crowd talking a lot up, or bidding on it with no intention to buy. The mechanism has moved online; the description has not had to change at all.

**The rule that covers both, and its case.**

`من غشنا فليس منا` [4] — whoever deceives us is not of us. The occasion is worth knowing because it is so ordinary: the Prophet ﷺ passed a man selling food, asked his price, then `فأومأ بيده أن أدخل يدك فيه فأدخل يده فيه فإذا هو مبلول فقال رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم من غش فليس منا` [5] — he gestured for him to put his hand inside, the man did, and it was wet. Rain had soaked the grain; the dry was on top.

No lie was told. The seller simply arranged what a buyer would see. That is the whole business model of a manufactured review profile, and the ruling attached to it is among the most severe formulae in the literature — not "this is disliked", but *not of us*.

Al-Fatawa al-Hindiyya treats a related deception as ranking with `شهادة الزور` [6], false witness, which is the register the sources reserve for this.

**Where the line falls, honestly.**

Not every kind of promotion is najsh. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr's own concern in that passage is with the seller who `يكتم عليه` — conceals from the buyer — knowledge that the buyer lacks, and Malik's objection to bulk sales was that they let one side know what the other cannot. The offence in all of it is engineering a false impression in someone who cannot check.

So: describing your product well is not the problem. Paying someone to say they bought and loved it is. Asking a real customer for an honest review is not the problem. Asking them for a five-star one, or offering them something for it, moves towards it.

**What these books do not reach.**

Nothing here addresses paid advertising that is disclosed as advertising, which is not a false impression at all. Nothing addresses gifting a product to a reviewer who states that it was gifted and reviews it honestly. Nothing addresses the follower counts of platforms that inflate them by default, nor accounts bought as a business asset and then run genuinely. Nothing addresses buying advertising to reach an audience, which is purchase of attention rather than of a false appearance.

**What to ask, or rather what to test.**

The question that decides nearly all of it: **if the person seeing this knew how it was produced, would it still work?** A disclosed advertisement survives that test. A bought follower count does not. A real customer's honest review survives it; a paid five-star one does not.

If you have already done it, the material above is about the practice and not a verdict on you — and the remedy in every case is the same and unglamorous: take it down, stop paying for it, and let the numbers be what they are.
