# Can Muslims celebrate birthdays?

## Answer

**Nothing in this corpus discusses a birthday.** What the sources rule on is two different things — taking part in *another religion's* festival, and inventing an *act of worship* — and it is worth seeing both, because a birthday is neither of them, and because if you turn it into one of them the rulings do apply.

**What Islam does have by way of festivals.**

Anas: "the Messenger of Allah ﷺ came to Medina and they had two days on which they used to play. He said: **what are these two days?** They said: we used to play on them in the days of ignorance. So the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: **God has replaced them for you with two better than them — the day of Sacrifice and the day of Fitr**" [1].

And 'A'isha's report, from which the phrase comes: "**every people has a festival, and this is ours**" [2].

Two things follow. Islam has designated festivals of its own, and the community is meant to know them as its own. And — this is easy to miss — he did not simply abolish two days of play. He **replaced** them, which means the impulse itself was not the problem.

**What is prohibited: joining another religion's festival.**

Ibn Taymiyya states it at full strength: "**it is not lawful for the Muslims to imitate them in anything that belongs specifically to their festivals** — not in food, nor dress, nor bathing, nor lighting fires, nor suspending a habit of livelihood or worship or anything else. And it is not lawful to hold a banquet, nor to give gifts, nor to sell what assists in that, for the sake of that; nor to let children and the like play the games that belong to the festivals; nor to display adornment. In sum: **it is not for them to single out their festivals with anything of their emblems**" [3]. And: "**rather, the day of their festival should be for the Muslims like any other day — the Muslims singling it out with nothing of their distinguishing marks**" [4].

The evidence he leans on is a striking hadith. A man vowed to slaughter camels at a place called Buwana. The Prophet ﷺ asked: "**was there in it any idol worshipped besides God, of the idols of the days of ignorance?** He said no. He said: **and was there in it any festival of theirs?** He said no. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: **fulfil your vow**" [5].

Two questions, both about another religion's rites, and when the answer to both was no, the act was permitted. That is a clean statement of what the rule is aimed at — and it is also a clean test you can apply.

**What is prohibited: inventing a devotion.**

The other concern is turning a date into an act of worship. Ibn Taymiyya, on what people had added to 'Ashura': "as for the rest of the matters — such as **taking food outside the ordinary**, whether grains or otherwise, or **renewing clothing**, or **enlarging the spending**, or buying the year's needs on that day, or **performing a specific act of worship such as a prayer particular to it**, or intending a slaughter... or applying kohl, or dyeing, or bathing, or shaking hands, or exchanging visits, or visiting the mosques and shrines, and the like — **this is of the reprehensible innovations**" [6]. And, naming them: "**none of the imams of the Muslims recommended it — not Malik, nor al-Thawri, nor al-Layth b. Sa'd, nor Abu Hanifa, nor al-Awza'i, nor al-Shafi'i, nor Ahmad b. Hanbal, nor Ishaq b. Rahawayh**" [7].

Read what is on that list. Special food, new clothes, extra spending, a particular prayer, visits. Every one of them is an ordinary permitted act — and every one is condemned **because it is being done as a devotion tied to a date**. That is what the innovation objection is about. It is not about cake.

**So where does a birthday land?**

Apply the two tests the sources actually give:

- **Is it another religion's festival?** No. There is no rite, no deity, no creed attached to the date of your birth. The Buwana test returns *no* to both of the Prophet's questions.
- **Is it an act of worship?** Not unless you make it one. A meal and a present are not devotions. If someone institutes an annual prayer, or a slaughter, or a religious observance tied to the day, the innovation objection engages exactly as Ibn Taymiyya describes.

That is as far as the evidence carries. This page will not tell you it is forbidden, because nothing here forbids it; and it will not tell you it is recommended, because nothing here recommends it.

**What some scholars say, which you should know about.**

There is a real position among later scholars that any recurring annual celebration is an *'id* by definition, and that Islam's festivals are the two named ones only — so a birthday is an added festival and should be left. That reasoning has the *hadith* of the two replaced days behind it, and it is held by serious people. It is not what these sources say in so many words, and this page cannot present it as their ruling. If it is your school's position, follow it.

**Practically.**

- **A meal, a gift, a card, people you love in one room — none of this is what the prohibitions describe.** If you want to mark your child's birthday, nothing in front of us stops you.
- **Do not attach worship to it.** No special prayer, no vow, no religious observance tied to the date. That is the line Ibn Taymiyya draws and it is a clear one.
- **Do not treat it as a religious festival.** If it is a family occasion, it stays a family occasion.
- **Accept and give birthday presents freely** — that question is separate and easier, and the gift material settles it.
- **If your family are not Muslim and this is their way of showing love, receive it.** Refusing your mother's cake will teach her something about your religion that is not true.
