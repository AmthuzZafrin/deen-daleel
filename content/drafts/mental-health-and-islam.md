# Is it okay to see a therapist or take antidepressants?

## Answer

**Yes — and the reasoning is not a concession, it is the ordinary law of medicine applied to an ordinary illness.** These books have no word for clinical depression, no psychotherapy, no antidepressants. What they do have is a settled position that treating illness is legitimate, and a settled habit of counting distress of the heart *as* illness. Put those together and the question mostly answers itself.

**The books class this as illness, not as a character flaw.** Ibn Hajar, commenting on the report that a Muslim is recompensed for what befalls them, reaches `هم` and `حزن` — anxiety and grief — and explains why they sit in that list alongside physical sickness: `هما من أمراض الباطن ولذلك ساغ عطفهما على الوصب` [1] — **they are diseases of the interior**, which is why they could be set beside bodily ailment. He then distinguishes them with some care: `وهو ما يضيق على القلب وقيل في هذه الأشياء الثلاثة وهي الهم والغم والحزن أن الهم ينشأ عن الفكر فيما يتوقع حصوله مما يتأذى به والغم كرب يحدث للقلب بسبب ما حصل والحزن يحدث لفقد ما يشق على المرء فقده` [2] — `هم` arises from thinking about a harm one expects; `غم` is a constriction of the heart from what has already happened; `حزن` from losing what it is hard to lose. Anticipation, oppression, loss. That is a description of anxiety and depression made by a man who had no other vocabulary for it, and it is not a description of weak faith.

The Prophet himself asked to be relieved of both: `اللهم إني أعوذ بك من الهم والحزن` [3]. You do not seek refuge from a virtue, and you do not seek refuge from something that is simply your own fault. It is named as an affliction, alongside incapacity, miserliness and debt.

**And treating illness is not merely allowed — the position is stronger than that.** Al-Nawawi on the report that every disease has its cure: `وفى هذا الحديث إشارة إلى استحباب الدواء وهو مذهب أصحابنا وجمهور السلف وعامة الخلف` [4] — an indication that taking the remedy is *recommended*, the school's position, the position of most of the early Muslims and of the later ones generally. He then names the objection and rejects it: `وفيها رد على من أنكر التداوي من غلاة الصوفية وقال كل شئ بقضاء وقدر فلا حاجة إلى التداوي` [5] — a refutation of those who denied treatment, saying everything is by decree so treatment is unnecessary. The answer he gives is the one that matters here: `ويعتقدون أن الله تعالى هو الفاعل وأن التداوي هو أيضا من قدر الله` [6] — God is the one who acts, **and treatment is itself part of God's decree**.

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr goes further in his language than almost anyone: `فمن زعم أنه لا معنى للرقى والاستعاذة ومنع من التداوي والمعالجة ونحو ذلك مما يلتمس به العافية من الله فقد خرج من عرف المسلمين وخالف طريقهم` [7] — whoever claims there is no point in seeking cure, and forbids treatment and medical care and the like by which well-being is sought from God, has departed from what Muslims recognise and gone against their way.

**Medicine is treated as a real body of knowledge, with real practitioners.** This part is worth reading closely, because it is the classical answer to "why not just make du'a?" Al-Nawawi reports the definition without embarrassment: `المرض هو خروج الجسم عن المجرى الطبيعي والمداواة رده إليه` [8] — illness is the body's departure from its natural course, and treatment is returning it. When people object that many sick are treated and not cured, his answer is that this is `إنما ذلك لفقد العلم بحقيقة المداواة لا لفقد الدواء` [9] — a gap in knowledge of the true treatment, not the absence of a remedy. And then, remarkably: `والأطباء مجمعون على أن المرض الواحد يختلف علاجه باختلاف السن والزمان والعادة والغذاء المتقدمة والتدبير المألوف وقوة الطباع` [10] — physicians agree that one and the same illness is treated differently according to age, season, habit, prior diet, accustomed regimen, and strength of constitution.

That is an argument for individual clinical judgement, made in a book of ḥadīth commentary. It is precisely why a page cannot tell you which treatment you need and a clinician can.

**Sadness itself is not a sin, and the books say so directly.** Al-Nawawi, on the Prophet weeping: `ان الله لا يعذب بدمع العين ولا بحزن القلب ولكن يعذب بهذا أو يرحم وأشار إلى لسانه وفي الحديث الآخر العين تدمع والقلب يحزن ولا نقول ما يسخط الله` [11] — God does not punish for the eye's tear nor for the heart's grief; He punishes or shows mercy for *this* — and he pointed to his tongue. The eye weeps and the heart grieves, and we say nothing that angers God.

Nor is a prophet exempt from being told not to be crushed. Al-Tabari, on the command to endure: `ولا تحزن على هؤلاء المشركين الذين يكذبونك وينكرون ما جئتهم به في أن ولوا عنك وأعرضوا عما أتيتهم به من النصيحة` [12] — do not grieve over them for turning away. The instruction presupposes the grief was real.

**If your problem is intrusive thoughts, this next part may be the most useful thing on this page.** People with anxiety, and especially with the obsessive form of it that attaches to religion, often arrive convinced that the thoughts crossing their mind are themselves sins recorded against them. The ḥadīth is explicit: `إن الله تجاوز لأمتي ما حدثت به أنفسها، ما لم يتكلموا، أو يعملوا به` [13] — God has passed over, for this community, what their selves say within them, so long as they do not speak it or act on it. Ibn Taymiyya reads the pardon as covering everything that does not amount to abandoning belief: `والعفو عن حديث النفس إنما وقع لأمة محمد المؤمنين بالله وملائكته وكتبه ورسله واليوم الآخر فعلم أن هذا العفو هو فيما يكون من الأمور التى لا تقدح في الإيمان` [14].

He also makes a point that people tormented by such thoughts are rarely told. Discussing the whisperer, he insists the source need not be a devil at all: `وأيضا فالنفس لها وسوسة كما قال تعالى ولقد خلقنا الإنسان ونعلم ما توسوس به نفسه فهذا توسوس به نفسه لنفسه كما يقال حديث النفس` [15] — the self has its own whispering; this is the self whispering to itself, what is called the speech of the soul. And so: `فالذي يوسوس في صدور الناس نفسه وشياطين الجن وشياطين الإنس` [16] — what whispers in people's breasts is their own self, and the devils of jinn, and the devils of men. Your own mind generating unwanted thoughts is a recognised category here, not a sign of possession and not a verdict on you.

**The honest disagreement, because there is one.** Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, after all his severity toward those who forbid treatment, does not agree that it is recommended. His own conclusion is that it is permitted and no more: `وإنما التداوي والله أعلم إباحة على ما قدمنا لميل النفوس إليه وسكونها نحوه ((ولكل أجل كتاب)) لا أنه سنة ولا أنه واجب` [17] — treatment is a permission, not a *sunna* and not an obligation; and he notes that some of the best of this community bore illness patiently with physicians available and were not faulted for it. He still reports that `وعلى إباحة التداوي والاسترقاء جمهور العلماء` [18] — the great majority hold treatment permitted.

So the range in these books runs from *permitted* to *recommended*. It does not include *forbidden*, and it does not include *a sign that your faith has failed*. Whichever end you take, therapy and medication are on the lawful side of it.

**Where the corpus genuinely stops.** It has no diagnosis of depression as distinct from grief, no account of what a talking therapy is, and nothing at all about how a drug might act on mood. Questions that follow from those — whether a particular therapy's assumptions sit well with your beliefs, whether to see a Muslim therapist or one who is not, what to do if a therapist advises something you consider unlawful — are not settled by anything here. They are worth raising with the therapist directly; a good one will work with your commitments rather than around them.

**What to do.**

Treat this as you would any other illness, because that is how these books frame it. If you are struggling, see a doctor or a qualified therapist. If you are prescribed medication, do not stop it because someone told you faith should be enough — no position in this collection supports that, and one of its most respected authors says the person who tells you so has left the way of the Muslims.

Alongside that, not instead of it, the tradition gives you words for the worst moments. At times of distress: `لا إله إلا الله العظيم الحليم، لا إله إلا الله رب العرش العظيم، لا إله إلا الله رب السماوات ورب الأرض ورب العرش الكريم` [19]. And what 'Ali called the words of relief: `كلمات الفرج لا إله إلا الله العلي العظيم لا إله إلا الله الحليم الكريم سبحان الله رب السماوات السبع ورب العرش العظيم والحمد لله رب العالمين` [20].

Say them. Also make the appointment. The books you are reading here treat both as the same act — reaching for the well-being that comes from God, by the means He put in the world.
