# Is it wrong to slack off at work?

## Answer

**Yes — and the reason is more precise than "be diligent." In these books, what your employer buys is either your time or a specified job, and which one it is determines exactly what you owe. Slacking is not a lapse of character; it is taking payment for something you did not hand over.**

**The distinction that decides it.** Ibn Qudama divides hired workers into two kinds. The first: `فالخاص: هو الذي يقع العقد عليه في مدة معلومة، يستحق المستأجر نفعه في جميعها، كرجل استؤجر لخدمة، أو عمل في بناء أو خياطة، أو رعاية، يوما أو شهرا، سمي خاصا لاختصاص المستأجر بنفعه في تلك المدة دون سائر الناس` [1] — the **exclusive** worker is one contracted for **a known period**, of which the hirer is entitled to his benefit **throughout**; called exclusive because the hirer has his benefit in that period **to the exclusion of everybody else.**

The second: `والمشترك: الذي يقع العقد معه على عمل معين، كخياطة ثوب، وبناء حائط، وحمل شيء إلى مكان معين، أو على عمل في مدة لا يستحق جميع نفعه فيها، كالكحال، والطبيب` [2] — the **shared** worker is contracted for a **specified job** — sewing a garment, building a wall, carrying a thing to a place — or for work in a period without the hirer owning all his benefit in it, like the eye-doctor or the physician.

Most employment is the first kind. You have sold a defined block of your time, and during it your employer's claim on your attention is described as exclusive. That is what makes an hour spent on your own affairs while on the clock a transfer of something that was not yours to keep — not a failure of enthusiasm.

Freelance and contract work is usually the second kind. There the obligation attaches to the deliverable, not the hours; how long you take is your affair, and finishing early is not theft.

**And the consequences are stated as a matter of entitlement.** Ibn Taymiyya: `لا ريب أنه إذا ترك العمل المشروط عليه لم يستحق الأجرة وإن عمل بعضه أعطى من الأجرة بقدر ما عمل وإذا تلف شيء من المال بسبب تفريطه كان عليه ضمان ما تلف بتفريطه والتفريط هو ترك ما يجب عليه من غير عذر` [3] — if he leaves the work stipulated of him **he does not deserve the wage**; if he did part of it, he is given wage in proportion to what he did; and if property is spoiled through his negligence, he owes compensation for it — **and negligence is leaving what is due from him without an excuse.**

Read the last clause carefully, because it is the definition the whole question turns on. Not doing what you owe, without a reason. Illness, an impossible workload, a manager who gave no direction — those are excuses in the literal sense the passage means. Preferring not to is not.

**Poor work carries liability, not just disapproval.** Ibn Qudama on the tradesman: `وهو ضامن لما جنت يده، فالحائك إذا أفسد حياكته ضامن لما أفسد` [4] — he is liable for what his hand causes; the weaver who ruins his weaving is liable for what he ruined. The list continues in the same register: `والطباخ ضامن لما أفسد من طبيخه. والخباز ضامن لما أفسد من خبزه، والحمال يضمن ما يسقط من حمله عن رأسه` [5] — the cook for what he spoils of his cooking, the baker for his bread, the porter for what falls from his load.

Botching a job is a matter of compensable damage. That is a firmer standard than most workplaces apply.

**What this does and does not mean.**

It does **not** mean you owe every waking moment, or that a break, a conversation, or a slow afternoon is a sin. The contract defines the obligation, and normal breaks are part of what both sides understood. The question is whether you are giving what was agreed, not whether you are working at maximum intensity.

It does mean:

- **Time is the thing sold, in most jobs.** Personal work during paid hours is not a grey area on this analysis; it is taking wage for benefit you kept.
- **Deliberately working slowly to make a task fill more paid hours** is the clearest case of all, since it converts the employer's time into your own.
- **Doing the job badly when you could do it properly** carries liability for what your carelessness costs.
- **If you cannot do what is expected — too much work, no training, unclear instructions — say so.** The definition of negligence is leaving what is due **without an excuse**, so making the obstacle known is part of discharging the obligation, not a way of avoiding it.

**What the corpus does not address.** It has nothing on salaried professional work where output is not measurable by the hour, on employers who demand unpaid overtime, or on a worker being paid less than the job is worth. The obligation described above is one side of a contract, and these books assume the other side is being honoured too — the duty to pay the worker promptly and in full is treated with comparable seriousness elsewhere in the same chapters.

Do the work you were paid for. Where you genuinely cannot, say so rather than quietly not doing it — that is the difference between a difficulty and `تفريط`.
