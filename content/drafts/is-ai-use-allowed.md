# Is using AI allowed in Islam?

> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give

## Answer

**This one we decline to rule on** — and the reason is not squeamishness. The corpus this site answers from was closed centuries before anything like this existed, and no scholar in it ever considered the question. There is no ruling to report, and inventing one would be exactly the thing the sources most warn against.

But *"there is no ruling"* is not the same as *"there is nothing to say"*. The tradition has a great deal to say about what it takes to answer a religious question, and that turns out to be the relevant material.

**What it takes to be a source of religious answers.**

Ibn al-Salah's conditions for a *mufti* are the classical statement, and they are worth reading slowly with a machine in mind. He must be "**a Muslim, trustworthy, reliable, keeping clear of the causes of moral corruption and of what forfeits propriety**" [1]. And he must be "**of jurist's instinct, sound of mind, weighty in thought, correct in handling and in derivation, alert**" [2].

Every single item on that list is a description of a person. Trustworthiness is a relationship. Propriety is a life lived in view of other people. *Fiqh al-nafs* — the jurist's instinct — is a formed disposition, not an output. None of these can be predicated of a program, however good the program is.

That is the ground of the answer this page will not give you. Not that a machine is forbidden, but that a machine is not the kind of thing the tradition ever imagined asking.

**Two warnings from the same book that land squarely.**

Ibn al-Salah insists that whoever writes out a question be careful, "for the one who writes the request for a ruling should be someone who asks well and frames it to the point, with **clarity of script and wording, and guarding both against what invites misreading**" [3].

He then tells a story to show why. A man in Baghdad wanted a ruling about someone who said to his wife *you are divorced if* — and then stopped, because something interrupted him. So the question was written: *what do the jurists say about a man who said to his wife: you are divorced if? then he stopped at 'if'.* But the writing carried no vowel marks, "**and it was misread by the jurists, because the question was bare of pointing, and they took it to be a suspension of the divorce upon the completion of the endowment of a man named 'Abdan. So they said: if 'Abdan's endowment is completed, she is divorced; and if this endowment is not completed, there is no divorce**" [4].

A marriage was ruled on, in writing, by qualified jurists, on the basis of a misparse. That is a nine-hundred-year-old description of what happens when a religious question is answered from text alone by something that has read the letters correctly and understood nothing.

The second warning runs the other way, at the person asking. Ibn al-Salah: "**it is not fitting for the layman to demand of the mufti the proof for what he has given a ruling on**, nor to say to him *why?* and *how?* — but if he would like his heart to be settled by hearing the proof, let him ask about it in another sitting, or in that sitting after having accepted the ruling stripped of its proof" [5].

We think that advice is exactly wrong for this situation, and the reason is instructive. It assumes a mufti whose character you can weigh, whose reputation stands behind the answer, and who bears responsibility for it. Where that person is absent, the demand for the proof is not impertinence — it is the only check you have left. **This is why every answer on this site is built around its citations rather than its conclusions, and why nothing here is published without a named human reviewer.**

**What the sources do say about answering without knowledge.**

The Qur'an's instruction where you lack knowledge is to go to people: *"Ask the followers of the Reminder if ye know not"* [6], repeated in another place [7]. It does not say *consult the record*; it names a class of persons.

And the rebuke to arguing past the edge of what you know is direct: *"Lo! ye are those who argue about that whereof ye have some knowledge — why then argue ye concerning that whereof ye have no knowledge? Allah knoweth. Ye know not"* [8].

There is also this, which is the posture the whole question calls for. When the Muslims gave leave for the captives of Hawazin to be freed, the Prophet ﷺ said: "**I do not know which of you has given leave. So whoever has not given leave, go back until your representatives bring your matter up to us**" [9]. He declined to act on an answer he could not verify, and sent the question back to be established properly.

**So, what this actually turns on.**

Not on a rule about a technology. It turns on four things, and you can weigh each of them for whatever you are using it for:

- **Whether the thing is being used for information or for authority.** A tool that drafts your email, tidies your code, or translates a document is doing what a dictionary and a typewriter did. A tool you take religious rulings from is standing where Ibn al-Salah's conditions apply, and it does not meet them.
- **Whether a person is answerable for the output.** In the tradition the mufti carries the weight of the answer; that is much of what makes it an answer at all. Where nobody carries it, you have text, not a ruling.
- **Whether you can check it.** The Baghdad story is what happens when the answer is trusted and the reasoning is not visible. Take from these tools what you can verify.
- **Whether it does harm** — the ordinary rules about deception, wronging people, wasting your life, and taking others' work without right apply here as they apply everywhere else, and they do not need a new ruling to reach a new machine.

**A note we owe you.** This site is itself an instance of this question. It does not generate answers when you ask; it retrieves passages from named printed works and returns answers drafted in advance, each one carrying its citations, and none of them visible to you until a named human being has read and approved it. That design is not modesty. It is what we could build that would survive the objections above.

- found via: "القول على الله بغير علم"

```
Qur'an 22:3 — Surah Al-Hajj, verse 3 of chapter 22

Translation (Marmaduke Pickthall):
Among mankind is he who disputeth concerning Allah without knowledge, and followeth each froward devil;

Arabic:
وَمِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ مَن يُجَٰدِلُ فِى ٱللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍۢ وَيَتَّبِعُ كُلَّ شَيْطَٰنٍۢ مَّرِيدٍۢ
```

### Passage 27 — Qur'an 2:118

- kind: quran · source: The Meaning of the Glorious Koran · chunk: 6856
- found via: "القول على الله بغير علم"

```
Qur'an 2:118 — Surah Al-Baqara, verse 118 of chapter 2

Translation (Marmaduke Pickthall):
And those who have no knowledge say: Why doth not Allah speak unto us, or some sign come unto us? Even thus, as they now speak, spake those (who were) before them. Their hearts are all alike. We have made clear the revelations for people who are sure.

Arabic:
وَقَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ لَوْلَا يُكَلِّمُنَا ٱللَّهُ أَوْ تَأْتِينَآ ءَايَةٌۭ ۗ كَذَٰلِكَ قَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِم مِّثْلَ قَوْلِهِمْ ۘ تَشَٰبَهَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ ۗ قَدْ بَيَّنَّا ٱلْءَايَٰتِ لِقَوْمٍۢ يُوقِنُونَ
```

### Passage 28 — Qur'an 3:66

- kind: quran · source: The Meaning of the Glorious Koran · chunk: 7090
- found via: "القول على الله بغير علم"

```
Qur'an 3:66 — Surah Aal-i-Imraan, verse 66 of chapter 3

Translation (Marmaduke Pickthall):
Lo! ye are those who argue about that whereof ye have some knowledge: Why then argue ye concerning that whereof ye have no knowledge? Allah knoweth. Ye know not.

Arabic:
هَٰٓأَنتُمْ هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ حَٰجَجْتُمْ فِيمَا لَكُم بِهِۦ عِلْمٌۭ فَلِمَ تُحَآجُّونَ فِيمَا لَيْسَ لَكُم بِهِۦ عِلْمٌۭ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ
```
