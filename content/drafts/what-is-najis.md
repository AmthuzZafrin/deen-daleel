# What counts as impure (najis)?

## Answer

**Najis means ritually impure — a specific, short list of substances, not a
general feeling of dirtiness.** This distinction is the whole of it. Something can
be filthy without being najis (mud, sweat, engine oil) and something can be najis
while invisible (a dried drop of urine).

**The list.** Al-Quduri names the heavy category by its members: **whoever gets on
him some of the heavy impurity — such as blood, urine, faeces or wine — to the
measure of a dirham or less, prayer with it is valid; if it exceeds that, it is
not.** [1] Working from what the books actually treat:

| Najis | Not najis |
|---|---|
| Urine, faeces | Sweat, tears, saliva, mucus |
| Blood that flows | Hair, nails, wool |
| Wine and intoxicating drink | Mud, dust, dirt, grease |
| Pigs, and dogs on most reckonings | Water, however used |
| Carrion, and the flesh of animals not slaughtered | Living animals other than dog and pig |
| Madhy and wady | — |

**Semen is a real disagreement**, and it is worth knowing which side you are on
because it changes what you do with your bedding. Al-Nawawi: **many held that
semen is pure — that is narrated from Ali ibn Abi Talib, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, Ibn
Umar, Aisha, Dawud, and Ahmad in the sounder of the two narrations, and it is the
school of al-Shafi'i and the hadith scholars.** [2] The Hanafis hold it impure but
easy to deal with: **semen is impure — what is wet must be washed, and once it has
dried on the garment, rubbing it off suffices.** [3] Either way you can clean it
without much trouble. Al-Nawawi explains the split neatly: **the evidence of those
who say it is impure is the narration about washing, and the evidence of those who
say it is pure is the narration about rubbing** [4] — Aisha reports both.

**Where it matters.** Three things must be free of najasa for prayer: **purifying
impurity is obligatory from the body of the one praying, and his garment, and the
place he prays on** [5]. Nothing else — your house, your car, your hands outside
prayer are not the law's concern.

**And it is not urgent.** Al-Nawawi: **removing impurity that one did not sin by
becoming smeared with is not required immediately; it is only obligatory when he
intends to pray and the like — though hastening its removal is recommended.** [6]
So if a child is sick on you at breakfast, you are not in a state of sin until
Dhuhr. You clean it before you pray.

**Two things that keep this from becoming an obsession.**

*The measures are generous.* The Hanafi threshold quoted above is a dirham's
worth of the heavy impurities — about the area of a large coin — and there is a
lighter category with a far wider allowance again: **and if a light impurity
touches him, such as the urine of an animal whose meat is eaten** [7], prayer is
valid up to a quarter of the garment. Every school excuses what people cannot
realistically avoid, and there is a separate answer here on how much of a stain
is too much.

*Dry, invisible traces are often no longer a problem at all.* Al-Quduri: **if
impurity touches the ground and it dries in the sun and its trace is gone, prayer
in that place is valid** [8]; and for footwear, **if impurity with a body to it
touches the khuff and dries, rubbing it on the ground suffices** [9].

*Cleaning it is simple.* Al-Nawawi: **removing impurity does not require a
number; cleanness alone suffices.** [10] One wash that gets it out is a wash. There
is a separate answer here on the method, and separate answers on blood, on dog
saliva, and on a baby's urine.

**What does not make you najis.** You do not become impure. A person is not najis
— not while in janaba, not during menstruation, not by touching a dog. The rulings
attach to substances on you and to acts you must perform, never to you.
