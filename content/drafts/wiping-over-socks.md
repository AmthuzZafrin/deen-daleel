# Can I wipe over my socks instead of washing my feet?

## Answer

**Yes — this is one of the best-attested permissions in the whole of the law.**
Instead of washing your feet, you wipe your wet hands over the top of your
footwear. It is called *mash 'ala al-khuffayn*, and it works at home as much as
on a journey.

**How firmly established it is.** Al-Nawawi does not hedge: **everyone who counts
in matters of consensus agreed on the permissibility of wiping over the khuffs,
whether travelling or resident, whether from need or otherwise.** [1] The
transmission behind that is enormous — al-Hasan al-Basri said, **seventy of the
Companions of the Messenger of Allah told me that the Messenger of Allah used to
wipe over the khuffs.** [2] Al-Jassas treats it as reaching the standard of
*tawatur*, mass transmission.

**Why the verse doesn't cancel it.** The obvious objection is that Qur'an 5:6
tells you to **lightly rub your heads and (wash) your feet up to the ankles** [3],
so how can wiping stand? Two answers, both old.

First, the timing. Jarir ibn Abdullah was asked whether he saw the Prophet wipe
*after* Surah al-Ma'ida was revealed, and his reply is famous: **and did I accept
Islam except after al-Ma'ida was revealed?** [4] He became Muslim after the verse,
and saw the Prophet wipe. So the verse did not abolish the practice.

Second, the reading. The verse has two transmitted recitations of "your feet" —
one reading pairs it with washing, one with wiping — and the Hanafis take both:
**their obligation is washing when they are exposed, and wiping when they are
covered by the khuff, acting on both recitations as far as possible.** [5] The
verse governs both cases; the footwear decides which.

**The one condition that matters.** You must put the footwear on while already in
wudu. This is the point of the most-cited hadith in the chapter — al-Mughira went
to pull the Prophet's khuffs off for him and was told: **leave the khuffs, for I
put my feet into them in a state of purity — and he wiped over them.** [6] Ibn
Abd al-Barr calls this the agreed foundation: **one does not wipe over the khuffs
except one who put his feet into them while they were pure.** [7]

So the sequence is: full wudu including washing the feet → put footwear on →
from your next wudu onwards, wipe instead of washing.

**How to do it.** Wet your hands and pass them over the *top* of each foot, from
the toes toward the ankle. That is all. Not the sole, not around the sides.

**How long it lasts.** A resident may wipe for a day and a night; a traveller for
three days and nights — **wiping over the khuffs is time-limited to three days
when travelling and to a day and a night when resident** [8]. There is a separate
answer here on exactly how that period is counted, which matters more than people
expect.

**What ends it.** Taking them off, and janaba. Even a partial removal counts:
**if the wiper decides to take off his khuffs and pulls the foot out of the khuff
though it is still in the shaft, his wiping is nullified.** [9] Once it is over,
you wash your feet again in your next wudu, and you may start a fresh period by
putting them back on in a state of full purity.

**Two honest notes.**

*Washing is still the better act.* Al-Nawawi: **our colleagues held that washing
is more excellent, because it is the root.** [10] The permission is a relief, not
an upgrade — take it when you need it and don't feel you are cutting a corner
when you use it.

*Whether your particular socks qualify is a genuinely disputed question*, and it
is not the same question as this one. Leather boots are agreed on; ordinary
cotton socks are not, and there is a separate answer here that lays out who says
what. Read it before you rely on this at home in a pair of gym socks.
