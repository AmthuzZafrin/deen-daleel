# Is dropshipping allowed?

> Reviewer note: Turns on possession and on salam, which is the classical exception for paying now against later delivery — and on its conditions.


## Answer

**Dropshipping is not one transaction, and the difference between its two forms is exactly the line these books draw.** Taking an order for goods you do not own, then buying and forwarding them, is the thing the Prophet ﷺ was asked about directly. Selling a described item you undertake to deliver, with the price paid now, is *salam* — permitted by Qur'an, Sunna and agreement. The mechanics decide which one you are running.

**The prohibition, in the case that matches most closely.**

Hakim b. Hizam described his own business: a man comes wanting something Hakim does not have, so Hakim goes to the market, buys it, and delivers it. The answer was `لا تبع ما ليس عندك` [1] — do not sell what you do not have.

That is a very close description of the standard dropshipping model, and it is worth admitting rather than talking around.

Ibn Qudama gives the defect: `ولأنه باع ما لا يقدر على تسليمه، فأشبه الطير في الهواء` [2] — he sold what he cannot deliver, so it resembles a bird in the air. The objection is to selling something whose delivery is not within your power at the moment of the contract. If your supplier can cancel, run out, or fail, the bird is still in the air.

**The permitted structure, which exists precisely because trade needs it.**

`من أسلف في شيء فليسلف في كيل معلوم، ووزن معلوم، إلى أجل معلوم` [3] — whoever pays in advance, let him do so for a known measure, a known weight, to a known term. Ibn Qudama defines it: `وهو أن يسلم عوضا حاضرا، في عوض موصوف في الذمة إلى أجل` [4] — a present payment for a counter-value described as an obligation, to a term. And he records its standing: `فقال ابن المنذر أجمع كل من نحفظ عنه من أهل العلم على أن السلم جائز` [5].

Note what makes it work. The seller does not own the goods either. What he owes is a *described obligation*, and he is on the hook for it — the conditions are that the description, the quantity and the date are all fixed. Ibn Qudama's reason for permitting it is frankly commercial: `ولأن بالناس حاجة إليه` [6] — people need it — since growers and traders need money before the harvest.

And the Companions did not investigate whether the supplier had stock. Asked whether the Nabateans they paid in advance had crops, the reply was `ما كنا نسألهم عن ذلك` [7] — we did not ask them about that. What mattered was that a definite obligation had been undertaken by someone answerable for it.

**So the question for your shop.**

Are you selling *this specific item, from that supplier's inventory*, which you neither own nor control? That is Hakim's case.

Or are you undertaking a described obligation — this specification, this quantity, delivered by this date — which you are liable to fulfil however you source it, and refund or compensate if you do not? That is far closer to salam, and the reason is that the risk has actually landed on you.

The test that runs through all of it: if the supplier fails, who bears it? If the answer is "the customer just doesn't get their order", you are selling what you do not have. If the answer is "I do — I must source it elsewhere or make them whole", you have taken on the liability that makes the transaction yours to sell.

One condition of salam does not fit most e-commerce: the price is paid in full at contract. Whether taking payment at order satisfies that, and whether a fixed delivery date is `أجل معلوم` in a world of shipping estimates, are questions for a scholar.

**Affiliate selling is a different question, and simpler.**

If you are paid a commission for directing a buyer to a seller, you are not selling anything — there is no sale of yours to be void. That is closer to brokerage or agency, and the material above does not touch it. The questions it does raise are honesty in what you claim, disclosure that you are paid, and whether the underlying product is lawful. It is not, in itself, `بيع ما ليس عندك`.

**What these books do not reach.**

Nothing here addresses print-on-demand, where the item does not exist until ordered and is made to the buyer's specification. Nothing addresses marketplace platforms that hold stock on your behalf. Nothing addresses whether a supplier's binding contract to you counts as having the goods `عندك`. Nothing addresses returns, chargebacks, or platform guarantees, all of which change who really bears the risk.

**What to ask.**

- If your supplier fails, who compensates the customer?
- Are you selling a described obligation, or a specific item sitting in someone else's warehouse?
- Is the price fixed and paid at the time of the order, and the delivery date genuinely fixed?
- Do you have a binding arrangement with the supplier, or are you relying on a listing being up when the order arrives?
- Are you describing the goods accurately, including the shipping time?

Ask about your actual arrangement, not "dropshipping". On these texts one version of it is a clear problem and another is a recognised contract, and the word does not distinguish them.
