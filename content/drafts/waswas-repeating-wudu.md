# I keep repeating wudu because I'm never sure it was right — what do I do?

## Answer

**Stop repeating it. Not "try to relax about it" — stop, deliberately, as the
correct religious act.** What you are describing is *waswasa*, and the tradition
treats it as a recognised affliction with a specific treatment, not as excess
piety to be gently discouraged.

**The scholars' verdict on the behaviour itself is severe.** Ibn Taymiyya, asked
about precisely this: **what he mentioned of whispering in purification — such as
washing the limb more than three times, and refusing to pray on the mosque's mats
and the like — this too is an innovation and a misguidance by the agreement of the
Muslims. It is not recommended, nor obedience, nor an act of nearness.** [1]

Read that carefully, because it is the opposite of what the feeling tells you.
The extra washing is not worship that you are permitted to skip. It is not
worship at all. You are not choosing between the safe option and the easy one;
you are choosing between the sunna and something the scholars named a bid'a.

The Prophet set the ceiling himself. A bedouin **came to the Prophet and asked
him about wudu, and he showed him three times each, then said: this is wudu;
whoever adds beyond this has done wrong, transgressed and oppressed.** [2] Three
words, escalating. The fourth wash is not neutral.

**What the disorder actually is.** Al-Nawawi, quoting Abu Muhammad al-Juwayni:
**and whispering — its source is ignorance of the pathways of the Shari'a, or a
deficiency in the natural constitution of the intellect.** [3] That is a
sixth-century description of two things we would name separately today:
not knowing the ruling, and a mind that will not let go. Both are real, and both
are treatable — the first by learning the ruling, the second by refusing to obey
the feeling.

He is equally clear that caution has a limit past which it stops being caution:
one may take precautions **on condition that it does not reach the whispering
that embitters his life and clouds over the duties of worship for him — for the
one who reaches that has departed from the paths of the righteous predecessors.**
[4] *Embitters his life.* He knows exactly what he is describing.

And his diagnosis of the pattern is worth having, because it is uncomfortably
accurate: **this is the way of the Haruriyya, the Khawarij — they were afflicted
with extremism where it did not belong, and with laxity in the place of
caution.** [5] Scrupulosity does not make you more careful overall. It spends
your whole attention on one washed forearm while the prayer itself gets away.

**The ruling you must act on, and it is unanimous.** Certainty is not removed by
doubt. Ibn Abd al-Barr: **certainty is not to be abandoned for doubt, until a
certainty comes that removes it.** [6] You had wudu. You *doubt* you lost it.
The doubt does not count — and al-Nawawi closes the escape route that the feeling
will immediately reach for: **there is no difference in doubt between the two
possibilities being equal as to whether the hadath occurred or not, or one of them
being stronger, or it predominating in his estimation — no wudu is due upon him in
any case.** [7] "But I think it probably broke" is doubt. It does not count
either.

**A treatment plan**

1. **Make wudu once. Three washes maximum per limb, counted.** Then stop, and
   walk away from the sink even if it feels wrong. Especially if it feels wrong.
2. **Do not go back to check.** Al-Nawawi permits a *single* precautionary wudu
   in genuine doubt — **it is recommended for him to perform wudu out of caution;
   and if he does so out of caution while his doubt continues, his obligation is
   discharged** [8]. One. Not one more.
3. **Pray in the state you are in.** This is the actual therapy: doing the
   permitted thing while the feeling screams. The feeling weakens by being
   disobeyed, and by nothing else.
4. **Expect it to get worse before it gets better,** and expect the thought to
   change its argument each time you defeat it. That is what it does.
5. **Treat the anxiety as anxiety.** Ibn Taymiyya and al-Nawawi both located part
   of this in the constitution rather than the conscience. If this has your sleep
   and your day, a doctor is not a religious failure — it is treating the part of
   this that is not a fiqh question.

**Something worth holding onto.** Every one of the passages above was written
because scholars kept meeting people in exactly your state, centuries before
anyone had a clinical word for it. You have not invented a new failure. You have
an old and well-documented one, with a prescription attached — and the
prescription is to do less, not more.

The verse itself ends where you should: **Allah would not place a burden on you,
but He would purify you and would perfect His grace upon you, that ye may give
thanks.** [9] Not a burden. If your purification has become one, something has
gone wrong with the purification, not with you.
