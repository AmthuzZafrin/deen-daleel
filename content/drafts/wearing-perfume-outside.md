# Can women wear perfume in public?

## Answer

The prohibition is real, and it is narrower than it sounds: it attaches to the
scent being *smelled by men when you go out*, and it lifts the moment you come
home. It is not a rule that you may not own or wear perfume.

**The text.** "When a woman goes out to the mosque, let her wash off the perfume
as she washes from major impurity" [1]. Al-Nawawi lists the conditions the
scholars drew from the hadiths of the chapter for a woman attending the mosque:
"that she not be perfumed, nor adorned, nor wearing anklets whose sound is
heard, nor sumptuous clothes, nor mixing with men, nor a young woman or the like
of one by whom people are tempted; and that there be nothing in the roads by
which corruption is feared" [2].

**Its stated reason.** Ibn Hajar: "what is in its meaning is annexed to perfume,
because the cause of the prohibition of it is what it contains of stirring the
motive of desire — such as fine clothing, jewellery that shows, lavish adornment,
and likewise mingling with men" [3]. So the ruling is not about fragrance as a
substance. It is about a category — things that draw notice — and perfume is
its clearest instance.

Which is why some scholars extended it to a shoe: "some of the scholars annexed
to that her wearing the creaking sandal, and other things that draw the eye
towards her" [4]. Sound and scent are treated as the same kind of signal.

**And there is a men's/women's distinction in the perfume itself.** Ibn Hajar
reports the hadith that "a man's perfume is what shows its scent and hides its
colour, and a woman's is the reverse" [5], with the reason: "the aspect of the
distinction is that a woman is commanded to conceal herself in the state of
emerging from her house — and perfume that has a scent, had it been prescribed
for her, would have been an increase in the temptation by her" [6].

**Now the scope, which is the part usually left out.** Ibn Hajar reconciles that
hadith with the one about a woman perfuming her husband with her own hands:
"the reconciliation between it and the hadith of this chapter is that she has
the alternative of washing off its trace when she wants to go out — because the
prohibition on her is *specific to the state of going out*" [7].

Al-Nawawi says the same about the timing: "its meaning is when she intends to
attend it; as for one who attended it and then returned to her house, she is not
prevented from perfuming herself after that" [8]. And Ibn 'Abd al-Barr records
Sa'id: "they only carried his words about women's perfume to mean when she
wishes to go out — but when she is with her husband, let her perfume herself as
she wishes" [9].

Three separate authorities making the same point: the prohibition is bounded by
the act of going out among men, and it ends when that ends.

**So, practically.** At home, with your husband, among women, among your
mahrams: perfume yourself as you like, and the sources say so explicitly rather
than merely permitting it by silence. Going out where men will smell it: wash it
off, or use something with no projecting scent — the scented soap, the deodorant
you cannot smell from a distance, the perfume in your hair under a scarf. The
line the sources draw is at the scent reaching men, not at the bottle.

Ibn al-Mubarak's advice to a woman who insists on going out captures the whole
of it in one clause: "let her husband permit her to go out in her plain clothes,
and not adorned" [10]. Go out; don't go out *presented*.
