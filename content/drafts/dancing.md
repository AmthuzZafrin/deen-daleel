# Is dancing allowed?

## Answer

There is one clear report of dancing in front of the Prophet ﷺ, and the disagreement is entirely about how far it reaches.

**The report.**

'A'isha: "**Abyssinians came dancing** on a day of 'Id in the mosque, and the Prophet ﷺ called me, and I put my head on his shoulder and began watching their play — **until I was the one who turned away from looking at them**" [1].

The verb is *yazfinun*, which is dancing, not fencing. And note the detail: he called her over, she watched from against his shoulder, and he waited until she had had enough.

Another wording has her ask first: "she said of the players: *I wish I could see them.* She said: so the Messenger of Allah ﷺ stood, and I stood at the door looking between his ears and his shoulder, and they were playing in the mosque" [2].

And when someone objected: "while the Abyssinians were playing with their spears before the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, 'Umar b. al-Khattab came in and reached for pebbles to pelt them with — and the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said to him: **let them be, 'Umar**" [3].

Ibn al-'Arabi uses exactly this against a scholar who objected to people playing on 'Id day: "it is related of Shurayh that he passed by people playing on the day of 'Id and said: *the Lawgiver did not command this.* **But there is something to be said about that** — for the Abyssinians used to play with shields and spears in the mosque on the day of 'Id, **and the Prophet ﷺ was watching**" [4].

**The counter-argument, which is the majority's and is a fair one.**

Ibn Hajar reports it: "some argued from this chapter for the permissibility of dancing and listening to instruments — and **the majority faulted that on the difference of the two aims**: for the Abyssinians' play with their spears was for training in war, so it cannot be used as evidence for dancing as amusement" [5].

That is a real objection, and it is why the report does not settle as much as it first appears. Zayn al-Din b. al-Munir makes the same point about the vocabulary: "he called it play, even though its basis is training for war, which is serious — because of its resemblance to play, since he aims a thrust and does not deliver it, giving his opponent that impression" [6].

Though it should be said that the reports do not all fit the training frame. Anas: "**when the Prophet ﷺ came to Medina, the Abyssinians played out of joy at that** — they played with their spears" [7]. That is celebration, and it is what the text says it is.

**The rule Ibn Hajar actually draws.**

He states both halves in one sentence, and it is the most careful thing in the file: "**his not objecting indicates the permitting of the like of that in the manner he approved — for he does not approve falsehood.** And the default is keeping clear of play and amusement, so one restricts oneself to what the text has come with, in time and in manner, so as to minimise departure from the default" [8].

Read it as it stands. The permission is real, because his silence is not neutral. And the permission is bounded — by occasion and by form — rather than open.

He then draws the positive rulings from the same hadith: "**the prescribing of enlarging on one's family in the days of the 'Ids with the kinds of things that give them expansion of soul and rest of body from the burden of worship** — and that turning away from it is better" [9]. And: "**showing joy on the 'Ids is among the emblems of the religion**" [10].

There is a place for celebration in the calendar by design. The Prophet ﷺ, on arriving in Medina and finding two festival days, said: "**God has replaced them for you with two better than them — the day of Sacrifice and the day of Fitr**" [11]. He did not abolish the festivals; he substituted better ones.

**One condition worth naming.**

From the same hadith, Ibn Hajar takes: "**the places of the people of goodness are kept clear of amusement and idle talk — even where there is no sin in it — except with their permission**" [12]. The Abyssinians were in the mosque; it happened with the Prophet's leave. A space consecrated to worship is not simply available.

**Practically.**

- **There is no blanket prohibition on dancing in these sources**, and anyone who says the Prophet ﷺ merely tolerated something forbidden has to answer Ibn Hajar's "he does not approve falsehood".
- **The clear case is celebration in its place** — 'Id, a wedding, a homecoming, family. That is precisely the setting of every report here.
- **Segregation is assumed throughout.** 'A'isha watched from behind him; the Abyssinians were a group of men. Nothing here licenses mixed dancing, and nothing here is about display of the body.
- **The majority's caution stands where the aim is different.** Dancing as pure spectacle, in a place and manner built for attracting attention, is not what any of this covers, and Ibn Hajar's own rule — restrict to the occasion and manner the texts give — points away from it.
- **The mosque is not automatically available**, even for what is permitted elsewhere.

The best summary is the scene itself: a young woman with her head against her husband's shoulder, watching men dance in the mosque on a festival day, staying until *she* had had enough, while the man who tried to stop it was told to leave them alone.
