# Can I inherit from my non-Muslim parents?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain

## Answer

The question bank flagged this one as beyond the corpus. That turns out to be half right, and the half that is wrong matters, so it is worth separating the two parts.

**The classical rule is clear and very nearly unanimous.** It rests on one narration from Usama b. Zayd: "A Muslim does not inherit from a disbeliever, nor a disbeliever from a Muslim." [1] Al-Nawawi reports the settled position in two halves — "the Muslims are agreed that a disbeliever does not inherit from a Muslim; and as for the Muslim, he likewise does not inherit from a disbeliever, in the view of the great majority of the scholars, from the Companions, the Successors, and those after them" [2]. Ibn Qudama puts it the same way and then lists who held it — Abu Bakr, 'Umar, 'Uthman, 'Ali, Usama, Jabir, and after them al-Zuhri, 'Ata', Tawus, al-Hasan, 'Umar b. 'Abd al-'Aziz, al-Thawri, Abu Hanifa and his companions, Malik, al-Shafi'i "and the generality of the jurists" [3]. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr's summary is the shortest: everyone who spoke on fiqh held "that a Muslim does not inherit from a disbeliever, just as a disbeliever does not inherit from a Muslim" [4].

**But it was not literally unanimous, and the dissent is worth knowing about**, because it is usually left out and it changes how the rule feels. A group held that inheritance runs one way — the Muslim inherits from the non-Muslim relative, but not the reverse. Al-Nawawi names them: Mu'adh b. Jabal, Mu'awiya, Sa'id b. al-Musayyab, Masruq "and others" [5]. Ibn Hajar records Mu'adh actually deciding a case on it: "two brothers brought a dispute to him, one Muslim and one Jewish; their father had died a Jew, and the Jewish son had taken his property, and the Muslim contested it — so Mu'adh gave the inheritance to the Muslim" [6]. Their argument was the report "Islam increases and does not decrease". The majority answered that this is an analogy set against an explicit text, "and there is no analogy where a text is present" [7]; al-Jassas answers it from a different direction, citing Masruq that this was "a newly introduced decision in Islam" [8]; al-Nawawi is gentler and simply suggests "perhaps this group had not received this hadith" [9]. There is even a third position, that it turns on whether the estate has already been divided, "reported from 'Umar and 'Uthman, and from 'Ikrima, al-Hasan and Jabir b. Zayd, and it is one narration from Ahmad" [10]. The rule that came out of all this is firm, but the reader should know it was argued.

Two details of the classical rule are often missed. First, the bar is not merely on taking a share — Ibn al-'Arabi holds that a non-Muslim heir does not even *block* other heirs the way a living heir normally would, rejecting the contrary view attributed to Ibn Mas'ud [11]. Second, the bar is about difference of religion, not about being non-Muslim as such: non-Muslims inherit from each other normally, and Ibn Qudama says of that "we know of no disagreement among the people of knowledge" [12]. The reason given for the whole rule is the break in *walaya*, the tie of mutual protection — "the Muslim does not inherit from a disbeliever, nor the disbeliever from a Muslim, because of the difference of religion between them" [13].

**Now the part the sheet does answer that people rarely hear: a bequest is a different instrument from an inheritance, and it crosses the line that inheritance cannot.** Al-Marghinani states it plainly: "It is permissible for a Muslim to make a bequest to a non-Muslim, and for a non-Muslim to make one to a Muslim" — the first on the authority of *Allah forbiddeth you not those who warred not against you on account of religion*, and the second because a voluntary transfer between the two is already lawful during life, "so likewise after death" [14]. This is not a loophole; it is a different legal category. Inheritance, in these works, is a fixed entitlement that attaches automatically by one of a short list of causes — Ibn al-'Arabi gives them as "marriage, lineage, and clientage" [15] — whereas a bequest is a gift that has to be made and accepted. The religious-difference bar is written into the first list. It is not written into the second.

**What this sheet genuinely cannot settle is your actual situation**, and here the original flag was right. Almost everything practical turns on facts these works never contemplated:

- Whether the money reaches you under a will your parent wrote, or under an intestacy statute that distributes automatically, or through a jointly held account, a pension nomination or a life-insurance beneficiary designation. These are not the same act, and the classical distinction above cuts straight through the middle of them.
- Whether a distribution ordered by a civil probate court is *irth* in the sense the jurists meant at all, or something the jurists would classify differently.
- Whether you may decline it, whether declining is even possible in your jurisdiction, and what happens to the share if you do.
- Whether being in a country that is neither of the classical categories changes the analysis. Ibn Qudama, notably, refuses to make territory the criterion at all — "the analogy of the school, in my view, is that people of one religion inherit from one another even if their territories differ, because the general import of the texts requires that they inherit, and no text, consensus or sound analogy has come specifying otherwise, so the generality must be acted upon" [16]. That reasoning cuts against importing territorial distinctions into a modern setting, but he is arguing about something else, and stretching it would be my inference, not his ruling.

None of that is evasion — it is where a corpus that closes in the nineteenth century actually stops. The rule is settled; its application to a probate file is a contemporary question, contemporary scholars differ on it, and the answer depends on documents someone has to read. Take those documents to a scholar or a Muslim estate practitioner who works in your jurisdiction. What you can go in already knowing is the shape of the question: whether what is coming to you is an inheritance share or a bequest, because the sources treat those two very differently.
