# Can I make wudu with nail polish on?

## Answer

**No — ordinary nail polish has to come off first.** It forms a solid film over
the nail, and water does not pass through it, so the nail never gets washed and
the wudu is incomplete.

This is not a modern ruling improvised for a modern product. The Hanafi fatwa
literature settled it centuries ago in exactly these terms: **the dye, when it
takes on body and dries, prevents the completion of wudu and of ghusl.** [1]
*Takes on body* — تجسد — is the operative word, and it is a remarkably precise
one. It is the difference between a colour and a coating.

**Colour is not a barrier; a coating is.** The same page draws the line with two
worked examples side by side: **if dry or wet clay is stuck at the root of his
nail, it does not pass; but if his hand is smeared with leaven or henna, it does
pass.** [2] Henna dyes the skin — the water still reaches it. Clay sits *on* the
skin — the water does not. Nail polish is the second kind, which is why henna is
fine and polish is not, however similar they look. And the rule reaches the nail
specifically: **what is under the nails is part of the limbs of wudu, so that if
there is dough in it, water must be made to reach what is beneath it.** [3]

**The Shafi'is arrive at the same place independently.** Al-Nawawi: **and if
there is dirt under his nails preventing water from reaching the skin, his wudu
is not valid, on the sounder view.** [4] Two schools, different reasoning, same
answer — which is what you want to see before treating a rule as settled.

**Why the "it's difficult" argument does not work here.** There *is* a hardship
exemption in the books, and it is generous, but look at who it is for. Abu
al-Qasim **was asked about the man with long nails who keeps grime under them, or
who works with clay, or the woman who has dyed her finger with henna, or the
harvester, or the dyer. He said: all of these are alike — their wudu suffices
them, since abstaining from it cannot be managed except with hardship.** [5]
Tradesmen. A dyer cannot un-dye his hands to pray. That is the case the
dispensation exists for, and nail polish is its opposite: chosen, cosmetic, and
removable in five minutes. Qadi Khan makes the limit explicit for worked dough
dried onto the hands — **he does not come out of janaba until he rubs that place
and makes water run beneath it, because there is no hardship in it** [6]. No
hardship, no exemption.

**What to actually do**

- Remove the polish before wudu. Once you have wudu, you may reapply it — the
  polish does not break wudu, it only prevents you making it.
- Plan around it. Polish applied after Fajr wudu will not survive to Dhuhr, so
  most people who wear it find it simpler to keep nails bare on ordinary days.
- **Prayers you already prayed while unknowingly wearing it are not something to
  tear yourself apart over.** But do not repeat the mistake once you know.

**A note on precision.** The one thing that must be reached is skin and nail,
completely — not approximately. The Prophet **bathed from janaba, then saw a spot
the water had not reached, so he squeezed his hair over it** [7]. He did not
repeat the whole bath; he wet the missed patch. That is the standard: reach
everything the verse names — **when ye rise up for prayer, wash you faces, and
your hands up to the elbows** [8] — and where you have missed something, fix that
part.

**Breathable or "wudu-friendly" polishes** are sold on the claim that water
passes through them. If that claim is true of a particular product, the ruling
above simply does not apply to it, because there is no barrier. Whether it is
true of the bottle in your hand is a question about the bottle, not about the
law, and this collection cannot answer it for you.
