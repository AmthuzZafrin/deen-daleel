# What are the 99 names and what do I do with them?

## Answer

**Short answer: the Qur'an tells you to call on Allah by His beautiful names, and
an authentic hadith attaches a promise to ninety-nine of them — but the famous
printed list of ninety-nine is a compilation by scholars, not words the Prophet ﷺ
is established to have spoken, and the number is not a ceiling on how many names
Allah has.** Both of those are stated openly in the classical sources, and
knowing them makes the practice better rather than worse.

**The command.** **"Allah's are the fairest names. Invoke Him by them. And leave
the company of those who blaspheme His names."** [1] The instruction is to *use*
them in calling on Him — and it comes with one boundary attached, which we come
back to at the end. The point is repeated elsewhere: **"Cry unto Allah, or cry
unto the Beneficent, unto whichsoever ye cry (it is the same). His are the most
beautiful names."** [2] Different name, same One addressed.

**The hadith, as al-Bukhari records it.** **"إن لله تسعة وتسعين اسما مائة الا
واحدا من أحصاها دخل الجنة"** — *Allah has ninety-nine names, one hundred less
one; whoever *ahsa* them enters the Garden* [3]. Read the report carefully:
**there is no list in it.** The number and the promise, nothing enumerated.
Al-Bukhari glosses the key word inline in the same place — **"أحصيناه
حفظناه"** — *"we counted it" means "we memorised it"* [4].

**So where does the list come from?** From al-Tirmidhi, who records the hadith
with the ninety-nine names spelled out — and who then tells you exactly what he
thinks of it: **"هذا حديث غريب حدثنا به غير واحد عن صفوان بن صالح، ولا نعرفه إلا
من حديث صفوان بن صالح، وهو ثقة عند أهل الحديث"** — *this is a gharib hadith;
more than one narrated it to us from Safwan ibn Salih, and we do not know it
except from the hadith of Safwan ibn Salih, and he is trustworthy according to
the people of hadith* [5]. He adds: **"ولا نعلم في كبير شيء من الروايات ذكر
الأسماء إلا في هذا الحديث"** — *we do not know of the mention of the names in
much of anything among the narrations except in this hadith* [6]. The compiler of
the list flagged its singularity himself, in the same breath as recording it.

Ibn Taymiyya states the conclusion the hadith specialists drew: **"وقد اتفق اهل
المعرفة بالحديث على أن هاتين الروايتين ليستا من كلام النبى وانما كل منهما من
كلام بعض السلف"** — *the people of expertise in hadith agreed that these two
narrations are not from the speech of the Prophet ﷺ; rather each of them is from
the speech of some of the salaf* [7]. And he notes that other scholars produced a
*different* ninety-nine: **"ولهذا جمعها قوم اخرون على غير هذا الجمع واستخرجوها من
القرآن منهم سفيان بن عيينة والامام أحمد بن حنبل وغيرهم"** — *and this is why
another group compiled them differently from this compilation and extracted them
from the Qur'an, among them Sufyan ibn 'Uyayna and Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal and
others* [8].

**What that does and does not change.** It does not touch the promise — the
hadith carrying the number and the reward is in Sahih al-Bukhari. What it changes
is your relationship to *the poster on the wall*: it is a scholarly compilation
of names drawn from the Qur'an and Sunna, made by people qualified to do it, and
more than one such compilation exists. Treating a particular arrangement as
though the Prophet ﷺ dictated it is more than the evidence supports.

**Ninety-nine is not a cap.** Al-Nawawi reports this as settled: **"واتفق العلماء
على أن هذا الحديث ليس فيه حصر لأسمائه سبحانه وتعالى فليس معناه أنه ليس له أسماء
غير هذه التسعة والتسعين وإنما مقصود الحديث أن هذه التسعة والتسعين من أحصاها دخل
الجنة فالمراد الاخبار عن دخول الجنة باحصائها لا الاخبار بحصر الأسماء"** — *the
scholars agreed that this hadith contains no restriction of His names; its
meaning is not that He has no names besides these ninety-nine. Rather the
intent of the hadith is that whoever ahsa these ninety-nine enters the Garden —
so what is being reported is entry into the Garden by encompassing them, not a
restriction of the names* [9].

Al-Khattabi's illustration, quoted by Ibn Taymiyya, makes it plain: **"كما يقول
القائل أن لى ألف درهم أعددتها للصدقة وإن كان ماله أكثر من ذلك"** — *as one says:
I have a thousand dirhams that I set aside for charity, even though his wealth is
more than that* [10]. Ibn Taymiyya adds the reading from the verse itself:
**"فأمر أن يدعى بأسمائه الحسنى مطلقا ولم يقل ليست اسماؤه الحسنى إلا تسعة وتسعين
إسما"** — *He commanded that He be called upon by His beautiful names without
restriction, and did not say His beautiful names are only ninety-nine* [11].

**The du'a that settles it — and that you can actually use.** Ibn Taymiyya cites
the supplication in which the Prophet ﷺ taught his companions to say: **"أسألك
بكل إسم هو لك سميت به نفسك أو أنزلته في كتابك أو علمته أحدا من خلقك أو إستاثرت
به في علم الغيب عندك"** — *I ask You by every name that is Yours, by which You
named Yourself, or sent down in Your Book, or taught to any of Your creation, or
kept to Yourself in the knowledge of the unseen with You* [12]. Four categories,
and the fourth is names no human being has ever heard. That is the clearest
possible statement that the ninety-nine are a portion — and the report notes that
when the Companions asked whether they should learn these words, he told them
they should.

**What to actually do with the names.**

- **Call on Him by them, matched to what you are asking.** That is the verse's own
  instruction. Ask the Provider for provision, the Forgiver for forgiveness, the
  Healer for healing — the fit between the name and the need is the practice.
- **Memorise them, on the reading most scholars took.** Al-Nawawi reports:
  **"فاختلفوا في المراد باحصائها فقال البخاري وغيره من المحققين معناه حفظها وهذا
  هو الأظهر لأنه جاء مفسرا في الرواية الأخرى من حفظها وقيل أحصاها عدها"** — *they
  differed over what is meant by *ahsa*; al-Bukhari and other verifying scholars
  said it means memorising them, and this is the more apparent, since it came
  explained in the other narration as "whoever memorises them"; and it was said
  *ahsa* means counting them* [13]. Memorising is the reading with a narration
  behind it — though the same scholars also discussed deeper senses of the word.
- **Learn the du'a above.** It reaches the names you do not know, which no list
  can.
- **Let them tell you something.** Ibn Hajar notes that affirming the names is
  affirming what they describe: **"ففي اثبات أسمائه اثبات صفاته لأنه إذا ثبت انه
  حي مثلا فقد وصف بصفة زائدة على الذات وهى صفة الحياة"** — *in affirming His
  names is the affirming of His attributes, for if it is established that He is
  Living, for instance, then He has been described with an attribute beyond the
  essence, which is the attribute of life* [14]. A name is information, not a
  password.

**The one boundary.** The verse ended by telling you to leave those who commit
*ilhad* in His names. Ibn Hajar reports what the exegetes made of that: **"من
الالحاد في أسمائه تسميته بما لم يرد في الكتاب أو السنة الصحيحة"** — *among the
deviation regarding His names is naming Him with what has not come in the Book or
the authentic Sunna* [15]. The same rule is stated for descriptions generally:
**"ولا يجوز وصفه الا بما دل عليه الكتاب والسنة الصحيحة الثابتة أو أجمع عليه"** —
*it is not permitted to describe Him except by what the Book, the established
authentic Sunna, or consensus indicates* [16]. You do not invent names for Allah,
however admiring the invention.

**Where this stops.** Which ninety-nine exactly, and which name is the greatest
name, were not settled — al-Nawawi reports a view that their identification is
deliberately concealed, the way the greatest name and the Night of Decree are.
None of that affects what you are asked to do, which is to call on Him by the
names you have.
