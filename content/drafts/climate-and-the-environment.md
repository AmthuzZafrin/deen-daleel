# Does Islam say anything about the environment?

> Reviewer note: Israf, fasad fil-ard and the khalifa material are all citable, along with the reports on not wasting water even at a running river, and on planting.


## Answer

**Yes — though not as a chapter called "the environment", which does not exist in these books.** What exists instead is a set of commitments that between them cover most of the ground: a prohibition on ruining the earth, a prohibition on waste that is unexpectedly severe, and a positive encouragement to plant which is framed in terms most people find surprising. Taken together they add up to something, and it is worth seeing what it actually is rather than what a modern argument would like it to be.

**The prohibition on corrupting the earth.**

`ولا تفسدوا في الأرض بعد إصلاحها وادعوه خوفا وطمعا إن رحمة الله قريب من المحسنين` [1] — do not cause corruption in the earth after it has been set right; and call on Him in fear and hope, for God's mercy is near to those who do good.

Two things in the phrasing are worth pausing over. The earth is described as already `مصلحة` — set right, in working order — so the corruption in question is the undoing of a functioning thing. And the verse ends by tying the whole matter to `المحسنين`, those who do well, which is the same register the tradition uses for the treatment of animals and of people.

**The prohibition on waste, which is stated far more harshly than people expect.**

`إن المبذرين كانوا إخوان الشياطين وكان الشيطان لربه كفورا` [2] — the squanderers are the brothers of the satans, and Satan was ever ungrateful to his Lord.

Note that this is not a mild counsel of thrift. Wastefulness is placed in the company of ingratitude towards God, and the reason offered is that using up what you were given without need is a way of treating it as though it came from nowhere.

That is the strand of this material with the sharpest modern application, and it does not require anyone to have known anything about the climate. It applies to a household, a wardrobe, a plate of food and a supply chain in the same terms.

**And the positive side, which is the most striking text of the three.**

`يغرس غرسا إلا كان ما أكل منه له صدقة، وما سرق منه له صدقة، وما أكل   السبع منه فهو له صدقة، وما أكلت الطير فهو له صدقة، ولا يرزؤه أحد إلا كان له صدقة` [3] — no one plants a planting but that what is eaten from it is charity for him, and what is stolen from it is charity for him, and what the wild beast eats from it is charity for him, and what the birds eat is charity for him; and none diminishes it but that it is charity for him.

Read the list. What is *stolen* from it counts. What a wild animal eats counts. What birds take counts. The reward is not conditional on the planter benefiting, or on anyone deserving to benefit, or on the beneficiary being human at all. A tree feeding birds is recorded as charity from the person who put it in the ground.

That is a remarkably wide way of valuing a living thing, and it is the nearest this corpus comes to saying directly that the natural world has a standing of its own.

**What this adds up to, stated carefully.**

A duty not to ruin what works, a prohibition on consuming beyond need that is framed as ingratitude, and a standing reward for planting that extends to creatures who take from it without asking. That is a real position, and someone can build on it.

What it is not is a ruling on any modern policy question. These texts do not tell you whether to fly, what to eat, how a state should tax carbon, or which of the competing accounts of a global problem is correct. Anyone quoting them at you as though they settle those things is doing something the texts do not support.

**What these books do not reach.**

Nothing here addresses collective harm — the case where no single person's act does damage but the aggregate does, which is the entire structure of the modern problem. The classical framework works with identifiable harms between identifiable parties, and that is a real limitation rather than an oversight. Nothing addresses obligations to people not yet born. Nothing addresses how to weigh a poorer country's development against a richer one's emissions. Nothing addresses whether an individual is answerable for a share of something diffuse.

**What is actually usable.**

The parts of this that translate without strain are the ones about your own conduct rather than about policy:

- Waste is a religious matter, not merely an economic one, and [2] is the text for it — food thrown away, things bought and unused, water run needlessly.
- Ruining something functional is a distinct wrong on [1], independent of who owns it.
- Planting is rewarded on [3] regardless of who benefits, which is a reason to do it that does not depend on any calculation of effect.

If you are being told that concern for this is a distraction from religion, none of the above supports that. If you are being told the tradition contains a developed environmental ethic ready for modern use, that is more than these texts will bear. The honest position is that it contains the beginnings of one, and that the collective dimension is the part a scholar has to build rather than quote.
