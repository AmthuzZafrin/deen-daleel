# Is meat from factory farms still halal?

> Reviewer note: Distinct from the slaughter answers: the question is the animal's life, not its death. The ihsan-in-slaughter report and the reports on starving and burdening animals are the material.


## Answer

**Almost everything written about halal meat concerns the last thirty seconds of an animal's life. This corpus has a great deal to say about the rest of it, and that material is far less often quoted.** Whether an intensively farmed animal is *halal* to eat, and whether raising it that way was permitted, are two different questions — and the sources are much clearer on the second than most discussions admit.

**The rule that governs both, and note what it covers.**

`إن الله كتب الإحسان على كل شيء،` [1] — God has prescribed excellence in all things — and then, immediately: `فإذا قتلتم فأحسنوا القتلة، وإذا ذبحتم فأحسنوا الذبحة، وليحد أحدكم شفرته وليرح ذبيحته.` [2] — so when you kill, kill well; when you slaughter, slaughter well; let one of you sharpen his blade and let his animal rest. Al-Tirmidhi: `هذا حديث حسن صحيح.` [3].

The slaughter instructions are usually read as the whole content of the hadith. They are the *illustration*. The rule is the first clause, and it is stated with the widest possible scope — `على كل شيء`, in all things. Sharpening the knife is offered as what excellence looks like in one instance, not as the boundary of where excellence is owed.

An animal's life is one of the things `كل شيء` covers.

**The report that settles whether confinement alone can be a grave wrong.**

`عذبت امرأة في هرة، سجنتها حتى ماتت، فدخلت فيها النار، لا هي أطعمتها وسقتها، إذ هي حبستها، ولا هي تركتها تأكل من خشاش الأرض` [4] — a woman was punished over a cat she imprisoned until it died, and entered the Fire on its account: she neither fed it nor gave it water while she confined it, nor did she release it to eat of the creatures of the earth.

Al-Nawawi draws the rule out explicitly: `وفى الحديث دليل لتحريم قتل الهرة وتحريم حبسها بغير طعام أو شراب` [5] — in the hadith is proof that killing a cat is forbidden, and that confining it without food or drink is forbidden.

Read the structure of the charge. She did not kill it. She confined it, failed to provide for it, and denied it the ability to provide for itself. The offence is precisely the combination of confinement and unmet need — and it is a person entering the Fire over a single cat.

That report does more work here than any argument about slaughter methods. Whatever else is true, this tradition holds that how an animal is *kept* can be a matter of the gravest consequence, entirely apart from how it dies.

**So, the two questions kept apart.**

*Was the farming permitted?* On [1], [2], [4] and [5], cruelty in the raising of an animal is a wrong — and the wrong does not evaporate because the slaughter was performed correctly. Anyone who says the treatment of a living animal is religiously irrelevant so long as the throat is cut properly is not reading this material.

*Is the meat halal to eat?* That is a separate question, governed by the slaughter rules, and the sources do not say that a sin committed by the farmer makes the meat itself forbidden to a later purchaser. The two do not automatically travel together, and it would be overreaching to claim they do.

The honest position is that intensive confinement can be a real wrong that these texts condemn, while the resulting meat may still meet the technical conditions. That is uncomfortable, and it is what the material supports.

**What these books do not reach.**

Nothing here addresses industrial scale, stocking density, indoor systems, selective breeding for growth rates, or the specific practices people mean by "factory farming" — no author saw any of it. Nothing addresses mechanical or automated slaughter, or stunning, which has its own separate answer here. Nothing addresses whether a consumer bears responsibility for a producer's conduct, which is the actual question for most people asking, and which turns on principles about assisting in wrongdoing rather than on any text above. Nothing addresses the economics — that welfare-reared meat costs more, and that eating less of it is an option the sources never had to consider.

**What is usable.**

- The treatment of an animal during its life is a religious matter on [1] and [4]. That is settled, and it does not depend on any modern argument.
- Correct slaughter does not retrospectively license how the animal was kept. Two obligations, not one.
- If you can choose better-raised meat, the material above is a reason to, and not a merely sentimental one.
- If you cannot afford to, nothing here makes ordinary meat forbidden to you, and you should not be told otherwise.
- Eating less meat and better meat is a response fully consistent with all of it, and one the tradition would recognise even though it never had to recommend it.

Take to a scholar the consumer question specifically — whether buying from a producer whose practices you consider cruel implicates you — because that is the live issue and it is answered from a different body of material than the one quoted here.
