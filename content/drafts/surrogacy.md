# Is surrogacy allowed?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

> Reviewer note: No author considered it. What the corpus is dense on is nasab and on milk-kinship, both of which bear on who the child's mother is. Set out that material and what it makes the hard question; do not rule.


## Answer

**No author in this corpus considered a woman carrying a pregnancy that was not conceived from her, and there is no ruling here to report.** What these books do have is unusually dense material on *who a child's mother is* and on how relationships are created by bearing and by nursing — and that material makes clear why surrogacy is the hard case rather than an easy one. Setting out what it does and does not settle is the useful thing this page can do.

**Motherhood in these books is established by bearing.**

The maxim on the father's side is `الولد للفراش وللعاهر الحجر` [1] — the child belongs to the marital bed, and the fornicator gets the stone. Al-Nawawi glosses the bed as the man: `أي لمالك للفراش، وهو الزوج` [2].

That rule exists because paternity was not observable and needed a legal anchor. Maternity needed none: the woman who gave birth was the mother, and the question of a competing claimant did not arise. Nothing in this literature is built to adjudicate between a woman whose egg it was and a woman who carried it, because that possibility does not appear anywhere in it.

So the first honest statement is that the classical rule is not silent by oversight. It answers a question that could not previously be asked, and the modern case splits the very thing it treated as single.

**Why the milk-kinship material matters here, and it matters a great deal.**

`يحرم من الرضاعة ما يحرم من الولادة` [3] — nursing makes forbidden what birth makes forbidden. A woman who suckles a child becomes, for the purposes of marriage law, its mother; her children become its siblings; her husband's relatives become its relatives.

Stop and notice what that establishes. This tradition already holds that a woman who did not conceive a child can become its mother in law, through a bodily relationship formed after birth — through *feeding it*. If a few feeds create maternity and a web of prohibited marriages around it, the natural question is what nine months of gestation creates.

That is not an argument this page is making. It is the reason a scholar cannot treat a surrogate as a mere vessel, and it is why the answers people give quickly in either direction are usually too quick.

**And it is not only a question about labels.**

Because milk-kinship generates real prohibitions — who this child may later marry, who is `محرم` to it — an unresolved maternity is not an abstraction. It determines whom a person may marry twenty years later. Al-Nawawi's chapter is occupied with exactly these consequences, including who may enter upon a woman on the strength of such a tie.

A child born to an unclear maternity inherits an unclear map of who its siblings are. That is the concrete harm the classical caution about lineage is protecting against.

**What these books do not reach, stated plainly.**

They do not address whose egg it is. They do not address a woman gestating an embryo genetically unrelated to her. They do not address whether gestation without genetic relation creates maternity, milk-kinship, both, or neither. They do not address the contract — whether a womb can be the subject of a hire at all, or what happens when either party changes her mind. They do not address the surrogate's own rights, the child's right to know, or the case where the surrogate is a relative. And they do not address the separate question of whether the fertilisation itself was lawful, which is prior to all of it and is taken up in the answers on IVF and on donor gametes.

**What to ask.**

Take this to a scholar you can speak with, and bring the specifics, because the arrangements differ enormously:

- Whose egg and whose sperm, and whether both are from a married couple. If anything comes from outside the marriage, that is a separate and prior question.
- Whether the carrier is genetically related to the child.
- What the arrangement says about who the mother is at birth, and what the law where you live says.
- Whether the carrier will nurse the child, since on [3] that has its own consequences.
- What the child will be told, and when.

Ask them specifically how maternity is established on their school's reasoning, and what the milk-kinship rules imply for a gestational tie. If an answer does not engage with that, it has not engaged with the question — that is where the difficulty actually sits.
