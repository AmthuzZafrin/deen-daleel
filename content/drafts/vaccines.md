# Are vaccines allowed?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

## Answer

**No book in this collection discusses vaccination.** Every work here was written before inoculation existed as a practice anyone could argue about, and the silence is total. The Arabic word now used for a vaccine, `لقاح`, appears in these books meaning a milch-camel. So this page cannot tell you whether a particular vaccine is permitted. What it can do is show you which older questions yours is made of — and those are answered here at length, and settle more than you might expect.

Your question is three questions stacked on top of each other.

**First: is it lawful to act against an illness you do not yet have?** The books answer this directly, because an emergency forced them to. The instruction on plague: `إذا سمعتم بالطاعون بأرض فلا تدخلوها وإذا وقع بأرض وأنتم بها فلا تخرجوا منها` [1] — if you hear of plague in a land, do not enter it; if it breaks out where you are, do not leave. Do not walk into infection and do not carry it out. That is a quarantine rule, given to healthy people about a disease they have not caught.

Then comes the exchange every later author returns to. 'Umar turned the army back at Sargh, and Abu 'Ubayda asked whether he was fleeing God's decree: `أفرارا من قدر الله فقال عمر لو غيرك قالها يا أبا عبيدة نعم نفر من قدر الله إلى قدر الله` [2] — *yes, we flee from God's decree to God's decree.* Then the image that carries the argument: `أرأيت لو كان لك إبل هبطت واديا له عدوتان أحدهما خصبة والأخرى جدبة أليس ان رعيت الخصبة رعيتها بقدر الله وان رعيت الجدبة رعيتها بقدر الله` [3] — a valley with a green slope and a barren one; grazing either is God's decree, so choosing the green one is not an escape from decree but a move within it.

Ibn al-'Arabi states that this did not lapse with that generation: `وهذا حكم باق في ملتنا لم يتغير` [4] — a ruling still standing in our religion, unchanged. And the first reason he gives for the prohibition is the principle underneath the whole question: `فإن صيانة النفس عن كل مكروه مخوف واجب` [5] — **guarding the self against every feared harm is obligatory.**

Al-Qurtubi then makes the move that matters most here. The plague ruling is not a rule about plague; it is the model for any danger guarded against: `فكذلك الواجب أن يكون حكم كل متق من الأمور غوائلها سبيله في ذلك سبيل الطاعون` [6]. God commanded `لكن أمرنا الله تعالى بالتحرز من المخاوف` [7] — wariness of dangers — and `وباستفراغ الوسع في التوقي من المكروهات` [8], exhausting one's capacity in avoiding them. He adds a case treated as beyond dispute: `ولا نعلم خلافا أن الكفار أو قطاع الطريق إذا قصدوا بلدة ضعيفة لا طاقة لأهلها بالقاصدين فلهم أن يتنحوا من بين أيديهم وإن كانت ألآجال المقدرة لا تزيد ولا تنقص` [9] — people may withdraw before an enemy too strong for them, *even though the appointed terms of life neither lengthen nor shorten*. That sentence is the whole answer to "but it is all decreed anyway."

Note that the rule cuts both ways: leaving is treated with real severity, `الفرار من الطاعون كالفرار من الزحف والصابر فيه كالصابر في الزحف` [10] — fleeing plague is like fleeing the battle line, and enduring it is like standing firm in one.

**Second: does taking a precaution mean your trust in God is defective?** The books treat this as an error and correct it hard. Ibn Taymiyya on the person who claims reliance while dropping the means: `من أخذ يدخل في التوكل تاركا لما أمر به من الأسباب فهو أيضا جاهل ظالم عاص لله بترك ما أمره` [11] — ignorant, wrongdoing, disobedient, because doing the commanded thing is itself worship. Both are commanded: `فمن إكتفى بأحدهما فقد عصى أحد الأمرين` [12] — whoever settles for one has disobeyed the other.

Ibn Hajar puts the distinction in a line: `فالتوكل لا ينافي تعاطي الأسباب لان التوكل عمل القلب وهي عمل البدن وقد قال إبراهيم عليه السلام ولكن ليطمئن قلبي وقال عليه الصلاة والسلام أعقلها وتوكل` [13] — reliance is the heart's work, means are the body's; tie the camel and trust. He draws the same lesson from the Prophet posting a guard: `وفي الحديث الاخذ بالحذر والاحتراس من العدو` [14]. Al-Nawawi says medicine specifically is no defect in reliance: `وأن الطب غير قادح في التوكل إذ تطبب رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم والفضلاء من السلف` [15]. And the definition he reports as the jurists' own has precaution built into it: `حده الثقة بالله تعالى والايقان بأن قضاءه نافذ واتباع سنة نبيه صلى الله عليه وسلم في السعي فيما لا بد منه من المطعم والمشرب والتحرز من العدو كما فعله الأنبياء صلوات الله تعالى عليهم أجمعين قال القاضي عياض هذا المذهب هو اختيار الطبري وعامة الفقهاء` [16]. Al-Qushayri's formula: `اعلم أن التوكل محله القلب وأما الحركة بالظاهر فلا تنافي التوكل بالقلب` [17] — reliance sits in the heart, and outward movement does not contradict it.

There was a minority holding otherwise, and you should know it exists. Ibn Hajar records that `لكن مقام الرضا والتسليم أعلى من تعاطي الأسباب` [18] — the station of contentment and surrender stands above resorting to means. He records it and rejects it: `والحق أن من وثق بالله وأيقن أن قضاءه عليه ماض لم يقدح في توكله تعاطيه الأسباب أتباعا لسنته وسنة رسوله` [19].

**Third: what about `لا عدوى`, "there is no contagion"?** This is the objection people actually raise, and the books met it long ago, because two sound reports appear to collide: contagion is denied, and yet a man with sick camels is told not to bring them to healthy ones.

Al-Nawawi's reconciliation is the standard one and it is precise: `فنفى في الحديث الأول العدوي بطبعها ولم ينف حصول الضرر عند ذلك بقدر الله تعالى وفعله وأرشد في الثاني إلى الاحتراز مما يحصل عنده الضرر بفعل الله وارادته وقدره` [20] — the first denies contagion *by the diseases' own nature*, without denying that harm occurs at that point by God's decree and act; the second directs us to guard against the thing at which harm occurs by God's act and will.

Al-'Imrani, quoted in al-Majmu', names the target of the denial: `وإنما نفى النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم العدوي الذي يعتقده الملاحدة، وهو أنهما يعتقدون أن الأدواء تعدى بأنفسها وطباعها` [21] — what was rejected is the belief that diseases transmit *by themselves and by their own natures*. What is affirmed is stated just as plainly: `وإنما العدوي الذي نريده أن يقول إن الداء جرت العادة أن يخلق الداء عند ملاقاة الجسم الذي فيه الداء` [22] — God has made it His custom that the disease is created upon contact with a diseased body, as He has made it His custom that a white child is born to white parents, though He could do otherwise.

That is the whole of it. What is rejected is a claim about what *causes* disease, not a claim about *when* disease appears. Nothing in it forbids you from expecting infection to follow exposure, or from acting on that expectation.

Ibn Hajar lists the other routes. Al-Baqillani: `إثبات العدوي في الجذام ونحوه مخصوص من عموم نفي العدوي` [23] — leprosy and its like are simply excepted from the general denial. Ibn Qutayba: `أن الامر بالفرار من المجذوم ليس من باب العدوي في شئ بل هو لأمر طبيعي وهو انتقال الداء من جسد لجسد بواسطة الملامسة والمخالطة` [24] — not `عدوى` at all but a natural matter, the disease moving body to body by touch and mixing; which is why `ولهذا يأمر الأطباء بترك مخالطة المجذوم لا على طريق العدوي بل على طريق التأثر بالرائحة` [25], physicians order avoiding contact. Abu 'Ubayd read the instruction as protecting belief rather than health: `ليس في قوله لا يورد ممرض على مصح إثبات العدوي بل لان الصحاح لو مرضت بتقدير الله تعالى ربما وقع في نفس صاحبها أن ذلك من العدوي فيفتتن ويتشكك في ذلك فأمر باجتنابه` [26].

On the practical side the books are not shy. Ibn Taymiyya, asked about a man with leprosy: `نعم لهم أن يمنعوه من السكن بين الأصحاء` [27] — the community may stop him living among the healthy — and he notes `وكذلك روى أنه لما قدم مجذوم ليبايعه أرسل إليه بالبيعة ولم يأذن له في دخول المدينة` [28], the man who came to pledge allegiance was given it at a distance and not admitted to the city. Al-Qurtubi's counsel: `فالأولى للمؤمن أن لا يتعرض إلى ما يحتاج فيه إلى مجاهدة فيجتنب طرق الأوهام ويباعد أسباب الآلام مع أنه يعتقد أن لا ينجي حذر من قدر` [29] — keep away from the causes of harm, while still holding that no caution saves anyone from decree.

**Where the corpus stops — well short of your question.**

All of that establishes a frame: preventing an illness you have not caught is a recognised category of act; doing so is not a failure of trust; keeping away from a source of infection is expressly allowed. That frame does not deliver a ruling on any actual vaccine, for three reasons these authors could not have addressed.

*What is in it.* A vaccine's ingredients — porcine gelatin as a stabiliser, alcohol as a solvent, cell lines used in manufacture — raise genuinely classical questions about impure substances in medicine, and those questions are genuinely disputed. They are taken up on the pages for alcohol in medicine and gelatin in medicine, and neither ends in agreement.

*What a vaccine does.* It is given to a well person and accepts a small present risk against a larger later one, for that person and for others around them. Weighing a certain small harm now against a probable large harm later, across a population, is a calculation these books never ran, because they had no way to measure either side. How differently the same text can land is visible in al-Tahawi's reading of the plague ruling, preserved in al-Fatawa al-Hindiyya: `تأويله أنه إذا كان بحال لو دخل وابتلى به وقع عنده أنه ابتلى بدخوله ولو خرج ونجا وقع عنده أنه نجا بخروجه فلا يدخل ولا يخرج صيانة لاعتقاده` [30] — the prohibition guards a person's *belief*, lest he think he was struck because he entered or saved because he left; on which reading one who firmly knows all is by God's decree is not covered by it at all. The same collection reports `أنه مر بحائط مائل فأسرع في المشي فقيل له أتفر من قضاء الله قال أفر من قضاء الله إلى قضاء الله` [31] — hurrying past a leaning wall. Read one way these are rules about physical danger; read the other, about the heart. Which reading governs a modern preventive medicine is not a question anyone here was asked.

*You.* Whether a given vaccine suits a given person turns on their condition, what else they take, pregnancy, age, and what is circulating where they live. That is a doctor's judgement and no book can make it.

**What to do.** Ask a doctor about the medicine, and a scholar you can actually speak to about the ingredients — give them the specific product name, because that detail decides the ingredient question and nothing general can. If avoiding animal-derived components matters to you, ask whether an alternative formulation exists; often one does. And do not let a page like this stand in for either conversation.

What these books give you is not the answer but the ground it stands on: that guarding yourself against a feared harm is a duty rather than a weakness, and that seeking a means is not a retreat from God but a movement from one decree of His to another.
