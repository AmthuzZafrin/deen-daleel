# Can I go somewhere alcohol is being served?

## Answer

There is a specific hadith about this, and it is more precise than "avoid alcohol".

**The text.**

Jabir reported the Prophet ﷺ: "**whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him not sit at a table on which wine is passed round**" — and in another wording, "on which wine is drunk" [1].

Ibn 'Umar's version adds a second thing: "the Messenger of Allah ﷺ forbade two [ways of] eating: **sitting at a table on which wine is drunk**, and that a man should eat lying on his stomach" [2]. Abu Dawud, who transmits it, flags the chain himself: "**this hadith Ja'far did not hear from al-Zuhri, and it is *munkar***" [3] — so the Jabir wording is the one to rely on, and it says the same thing.

Notice what the prohibition attaches to. Not to being near a drinker, not to a building, not to non-Muslim company. To **being at the table where it is being drunk** — sharing in the occasion of it.

**The rule the jurists built from it — and it is workable.**

Al-Nawawi, on being invited somewhere where there is a wrong: "**if he is invited to a place in which there is something objectionable — a pipe or wine — then if he is able to remove it, it is obligatory on him to attend**, both because of the obligation of accepting the invitation and in order to remove the wrong. **And if he is not able to remove it, he does not attend** — because of what is narrated that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ forbade sitting at a table around which wine is passed" [4].

That is a two-branch rule. The question is not *is alcohol present* but *can you change what is happening*. If you can, you go, and your going is the point. If you cannot, you do not.

**And what to do when it is around you anyway.**

Nafi': "**I was travelling with 'Abdullah b. 'Umar, and he heard a shepherd's pipe. He put his two fingers in his ears, then turned aside from the road, and kept saying: Nafi', can you hear? — until I said no. Then he took his fingers out of his ears and returned to the road, and said: this is how I saw the Messenger of Allah ﷺ do**" [5].

He did not stop the shepherd, or lecture him, or abandon the journey. He removed himself from it for as long as it lasted and went back. That is a model for walking past something you cannot fix.

**So, the pub.**

The place is not what the texts are about; the table is. Which means the answer depends on what you are actually doing there:

- **A work lunch, a birthday meal, a Sunday roast in a pub dining room** — you are eating, others may have a drink with their food, nobody is drinking *at your table* in the sense of the hadith being the occasion. Most scholars' reasoning does not reach this, and refusing every such invitation will cut you off from ordinary life for no textual gain.
- **A drinking session — the round, the toast, the table where the point is the alcohol** — this is the case the hadith describes, and the answer is no. Not because the people are bad, but because the hadith names the table.
- **In between**, use al-Nawawi's test. Can your presence change what happens? If your friends will happily order coffee and move to a table with you, go and be the reason. If your presence changes nothing and the evening is the drinking, you have your answer.

**Practically, and this matters more than the ruling.**

- **Suggest the alternative rather than declining the person.** *I can't do the pub, but I'd love to do dinner Thursday* keeps the friendship, and the friendship is something the sources want you to keep.
- **Say it once, plainly, without apology or sermon.** *I don't drink* is a complete sentence. You are not required to explain the fiqh of it to anyone.
- **You may still go for the part that is fine and leave before the part that is not.** That is what Ibn 'Umar's road did.
- **Never let this become a reason to disappear.** Losing every friend you had is not what any of this is for, and a friend who only ever sees you decline will stop asking.
