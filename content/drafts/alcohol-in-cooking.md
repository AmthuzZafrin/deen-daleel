# What if alcohol was used in cooking and burned off?

## Answer

**Short answer: the question the classical sources ask is not "did the alcohol
evaporate" but "does the result still intoxicate, and does it still count as
wine" — and on that framing there is a real basis for permission and a real
disagreement.** Anyone who tells you this is obviously settled, either way, is
skipping the part where four schools worked on it.

**The category the jurists used: cooked grape juice.** Ibn Hajar, on the reduced
syrup known as *tila'*: **"فإذا طبخ عصير العنب حتى تمدد أشبه طلاء الإبل وهو في
تلك الحالة غالبا لا يسكر"** — *when grape juice is cooked until it thickens it
resembles the tar used on camels, and in that state it generally does not
intoxicate* [1]. He names the Companions who treated it as lawful alongside
'Umar — Abu Musa, Abu'l-Darda', 'Ali, Abu Umama and Khalid ibn al-Walid.

And 'Umar did not merely permit it. Al-Nasa'i records: **"كتب عمر بن الخطاب إلى
بعض عماله أن ارزقوا المسلمين من الطلاء ما ذهب ثلثاه وبقي ثلثه"** — *'Umar ibn
al-Khattab wrote to some of his governors: provision the Muslims with tila' of
which two-thirds has gone and a third remains* [2]. A caliph issuing it as
rations.

Qadi Khan states the resulting rule: **"والشراب الرابع من العنب هو عصير العنب إذا
طبخ حتى ذهب ثلثاه ما دام حلوا يحل شربه عند الكل"** — *the fourth drink from
grapes is grape juice cooked until two-thirds has gone; so long as it stays
sweet, drinking it is lawful according to all* [3].

**Notice what the test is.** Not the absence of a chemical, which nobody could
measure. The tests used are **whether it intoxicates** and **whether it has
turned**. Ibn Hajar reports the early markers: **"أشرب العصير ما لم يغل وعن الحسن
البصري ما لم يتغير"** — *drink the juice as long as it has not fermented; and
from al-Hasan al-Basri: as long as it has not changed* [4].

**A second principle: predominance.** Al-Jassas argues from how the case is
actually judged: **"بدلالة أن قطرة من خمر لو وقعت في حق ماء فشرب منه إنسان لم يقل
إنه شارب خمر ولا يجب عليه الحد"** — *by the evidence that if a drop of wine fell
into a vessel of water and a person drank from it, he would not be called a
wine-drinker and no prescribed penalty would be due upon him* [5] — and the
converse: **"ولو أن خمرا صب فيها ماء فمزجت به فكان الخمر هو الغالب لإطلاق الناس
عليه أنه شارب خمر"** — *and if water were poured into wine and mixed with it, so
that the wine was predominant, people would say of him that he is a
wine-drinker* [6].

That is a workable test, and it is the one that most closely matches a splash of
wine in a large pot of sauce.

**Now the disagreement, because it is real.** The other framework asks whether
something that *was* khamr can be made lawful by processing it. Al-Nawawi reports
the majority position: **"هذا دليل الشافعي والجمهور أنه لا يجوز تخليل الخمر ولا
تطهر بالتخليل"** — *this is evidence for al-Shafi'i and the majority that
deliberately turning wine into vinegar is not permitted, and it does not become
pure by being so turned* [7]. On that view, deliberately transforming khamr does
not launder it.

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr reports the Hanafi position, which runs the other way: **"وأجاز
أبو حنيفة وأصحابه مع تخليلها أن يصنع من الخمر المربى وغيره وبأي وجه أفسدت وزالت
علة السكر منها طابت عندهم وطهرت"** — *Abu Hanifa and his companions permitted,
along with turning it to vinegar, that preserves and other things be made from
wine; and by whatever means it is spoiled and the cause of intoxication is
removed from it, it becomes good and pure in their view* [8].

**Where that leaves a wine sauce.** Two frameworks, pointing different ways:

- On **the predominance test** and on **Abu Hanifa's rule about the intoxicating
  cause being removed**, a dish in which wine is a small ingredient and which
  cannot intoxicate anyone has a genuine basis for permission.
- On **the majority position about deliberate transformation**, the fact that
  khamr was knowingly put in is itself the problem, and the cooking does not
  answer it.

Neither is a fringe view. Which one governs you depends on which school you
follow, and this is a reasonable thing to put to a scholar rather than to settle
from a summary.

**What is clear either way.**

- **"It cooks off" is not a general licence.** The classical permission runs
  through cooked *juice* — something that never became wine — not through wine
  that was later heated.
- **A dish that could intoxicate you is out**, on every position above.
- **Trace amounts behave differently from ingredients**, on al-Jassas's test: the
  drop in the vessel and the splash of water in the glass are not the same case.
- **Vanilla extract, flavourings and similar are a related but distinct
  question**, since those are usually not khamr at all — see *Is vanilla extract
  haram because of the alcohol?*
- **If you are cooking for yourself, you do not need to use wine.** The
  disagreement above is worth knowing so you are not alarmed by a restaurant
  sauce; it is not a reason to introduce the question into your own kitchen.

**Where this stops.** The passages here address cooked grape products, mixtures,
and deliberate transformation. They do not address industrially produced
ethanol-containing ingredients, which are chemically and legally a different
case, nor do they settle any particular recipe.
