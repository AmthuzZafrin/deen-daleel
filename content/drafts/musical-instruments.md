# Are musical instruments specifically forbidden?

> Reviewer note: Same school-skew caution as music.

## Answer

This is the actual disputed ground. The voice is not really in dispute; instruments are. And on instruments the sources split hard enough that consensus has been claimed in both directions.

**The prohibitive case, stated by its strongest advocate.**

Ibn Taymiyya: "it is established in *Sahih al-Bukhari* and elsewhere that the Prophet ﷺ mentioned those who deem lawful *al-hir*, silk, wine and *al-ma'azif* — by way of censuring them, and that God will punish them. So this hadith indicates the prohibition of *al-ma'azif*; and *al-ma'azif* are the instruments of amusement according to the lexicographers, and this is a name that takes in all these instruments" [1].

That hadith is the whole prohibitive case in one line, and it is a strong one. Two things about it are worth knowing before you treat it as closing the matter: it is a report about people who will *deem lawful* four things together — the pairing matters to how it is read — and Ibn Hajar, who commented on that same collection, records that the scholarly reception was not uniform.

**Consensus claimed both ways.**

Ibn Hajar, on instruments specifically: "some people have reported consensus on their prohibition, and some have reported the opposite" [2].

That is a commentator on the very collection containing the *ma'azif* hadith telling you the matter was disputed. When both a consensus and its negation are transmitted, what you have is a majority position — and the majority did prohibit — not a settled one.

**The exception no one disputes: the *duff*.**

Muhammad b. Hatib reported the Prophet ﷺ saying: "the divider between the lawful and the unlawful is the *duff* and the voice at a marriage" [3].

Al-Rubayyi' bint Mu'awwidh: "the Messenger of Allah ﷺ came in upon me on the morning after my marriage was consummated and sat on my bed, and some girls of ours were beating a *duff* and lamenting those of my forefathers who were killed. One of them said: *and among us is a prophet who knows what will be tomorrow* — so he said to her: **stop this, and say what you were saying before it**" [4].

Read what he corrected. Not the drum, not the singing, not the gathering — a theological claim in one line.

Ibn Hajar takes the ruling from the chapter itself: "the beating of the *duff* is prescribed at the marriage — at the contract, and at the consummation for instance, and at the wedding feast likewise" [5].

Ahmad b. Hanbal draws a line inside the instruments: "there is no harm in the *duff* at a wedding and at a circumcision; and I dislike the drum, and it is the objectionable one — it is the *kuba*, which the Prophet ﷺ forbade" [6]. Ibn Qudama then notes what the licence's grammar implies: "the reports about announcing [the marriage] are meant as recommendation — the evidence being his command in them to beat the *duff* and raise the voice, and that is not obligatory" [7].

So: an instrument, commanded at a wedding, in a school not otherwise permissive.

**And this is where the category breaks down.**

Ibn 'Abidin, glossing the phrase "instrument of amusement", gives the list: "such as the *barbat*, the *mizmar*, the ***duff***, the drum and the *tanbur*" [8].

The *duff* is on the list. It is classed as an instrument of amusement by a Hanafi jurist — and it is also the one the Prophet ﷺ sat beside and Ahmad said there was no harm in. Whatever the operative principle is, it cannot simply be *instrument*, because the same object appears on both sides.

Ibn Hajar is careful in the other direction: permission of the *duff* at a wedding "does not entail the permission of" the rest [9]. Both cautions are right, and together they mean the ruling attaches to something other than the category name.

**A detail that reveals how the jurists actually held this.**

In the Hanafi discussion of liability for destroying another person's property, Ibn 'Abidin writes of instruments: "the sale of all of them is valid, because they are property with assessable value, given their fitness to be benefited from for other than amusement — so this does not preclude liability, like the singing slave-girl" [10]. And he gives the valuations: "for the *duff*, its value as a *duff* into which cotton is put; and for the *barbat*, a bowl for *tharid*" [11].

Ibn Taymiyya's school goes the other way: "the jurists said that one who destroys them owes no compensation, when he has removed the forbidden composition; and if he has destroyed the property value, there is dispute about it — and Ahmad's well-known position, and Malik's, is that there is no liability in these cases either" [12].

That disagreement about *smashing someone's lute* is more revealing than the abstract rulings. One school treats the object as property you owe money for; another treats it as contraband. They are not disagreeing about whether music is nice. They are disagreeing about whether the object is inherently unlawful or unlawful in a use.

**The reason the ruling is about use, not sound.**

Al-Nawawi, on Abu Bakr's protest — *the pipe of Satan in the house of the Messenger of Allah?* — reads it as being about the dignity of a place: "in it is that the places of the righteous and the people of virtue are kept clear of desire and idle talk and the like — and there was no sin in it" [13]. That last clause is doing a great deal: the objection was made in good faith, and it was wrong.

And on the Prophet's silence: "the Prophet ﷺ was silent about it only because it was permitted for them; and he covered himself with his garment and turned his face away, turning aside from the amusement, and so that they would not feel ashamed and stop what was permitted to them" [14].

He turned away and let it continue. That is not the posture of someone confronting something forbidden; it is the posture of someone declining an entertainment he did not want while refusing to spoil it for others.

**Where this leaves you.**

- **The majority of scholars prohibit instruments beyond the *duff*.** If you follow a school, this is most likely its position, and the *ma'azif* hadith is why. Follow it.
- **The *duff* at a wedding, a circumcision, or an 'Id is agreed permitted**, and in one report actively prescribed. Anyone who tells you all percussion is forbidden has gone past the sources.
- **A real minority permitted more**, and the claim of consensus against them is contradicted inside the same commentary tradition. If someone tells you this question is closed, they are not describing the books.
- **Nobody in the sources rules on a recording, a film score, or a call tone.** Everything above is about people playing instruments in a room. Extending it is an act of reasoning, and whoever does the extending owes you their reasoning rather than a hadith.

What every party agrees on is the content and the effect: what is sung, what it does to the heart, and whether it displaces prayer. That much you can settle without waiting for the dispute to end.
