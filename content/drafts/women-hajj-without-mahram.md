# Can a woman go to Hajj without a male relative?

## Answer

This is one of the genuinely contested questions in the law, and you should know that before you are
told there is only one answer. The four schools split, and they split on the *obligatory* Hajj
specifically.

### The map

Al-Nawawi sets it out school by school. The Shafi'i position: **"the upshot is that going out for the
obligatory Hajj is permitted with a husband, a mahram, or a trustworthy woman, and is not permitted with
anyone besides these, even if the road is safe"** [1]. He then distinguishes the obligatory from the
voluntary: **"the sound position is that it is permitted for her, on the journey of the obligatory Hajj,
to go out with trustworthy women or a trustworthy woman, and a mahram is not a condition; but it is not
permitted in the voluntary, nor the journey of trade or visiting and the like, except with a
mahram"** [2].

The others, in his own summary: **"Malik said it is not permitted with a single trustworthy woman;
rather it is permitted with a mahram or with trustworthy women"** [3], and **"Abu Hanifa and Ahmad said
it is not permitted except with a husband or a mahram"** [4]. A minority went further than everyone:
**"some of our companions said it is permitted without women and without a woman if the road is safe,
and this is what al-Hasan al-Basri and Dawud said"** [5].

So: Hanafi and Hanbali require a husband or mahram. Maliki accepts a group of trustworthy women. Shafi'i
accepts even one, for the obligatory Hajj. A minority accepts a safe road.

### Why they differ

Everyone is reading the same hadith: **"a woman shall not travel three days except with a mahram"** [6].
The disagreement is over what it governs.

Al-Sarakhsi, arguing the Hanafi case, first states the opposing argument fairly — **"moreover this is a
journey to discharge an obligation, so a mahram is not a condition in it, like the journey of
migration"** [7] — the reasoning being that if a convert may flee to safety unaccompanied, a woman may
travel to fulfil a duty God imposed. He answers it with the incident in the hadith: a man told the
Prophet ﷺ that he had enlisted for an expedition and his wife wished to perform Hajj, and was told
**"perform Hajj with your wife"** [8]. Al-Sarakhsi's inference: **"in the Messenger of God's ﷺ ordering
the husband to abandon the military expedition and go out with her is evidence that she may not go out
except with a husband or a mahram"** [9]. Al-Jassas reaches the same conclusion — **"it is thereby
established that a woman's having a mahram is among the conditions of her ability"** [10].

Note what the Hanafi reasoning is *about*. Al-Sarakhsi is explicit that the concern is exposure, not
capacity, and even the Shafi'i alternative is aimed at the same thing: **"so she goes out with a company
of trustworthy women, to find companionship with them and to have no need of mixing with men"** [11].
Read that way, the schools differ over the means, not the purpose.

### The details that matter in practice

**Who counts as a mahram.** Al-Sarakhsi: **"its definition is one whom it is not lawful for him to marry
her permanently, by reason of kinship, breastfeeding, or marriage ties"** [12] — so father, son, brother,
uncle, father-in-law, son-in-law, foster relations. Not a cousin. And **"it is equal whether the mahram
is free or owned, Muslim or non-Muslim"** [13].

**Distance.** The mahram condition is not on every trip. **"The distance in which Abu Hanifa stipulates
the mahram is three days; if it is less than that, it is not stipulated"** [14].

**If you never find one.** In the Hanafi and Hanbali reading, being without a mahram is a lasting
incapacity, treated like permanent illness: **"she is a woman who has no mahram and no husband to go out
with her — such people are bound to the Hajj through their wealth, upon despair and inability to perform
it themselves"** [15]. And the accounting is honest about the possibility of being wrong: **"if the sick
man or the woman has someone perform Hajj for them, and then the sick man does not recover and the woman
does not find a mahram until they die, it suffices them; but if the sick man recovers and the woman
finds a mahram, it does not suffice them"** [16].

### Where this leaves you

If you follow the Hanafi or Hanbali school, you need a husband or mahram, and until you have one the
obligation is not on you. If you follow the Shafi'i or Maliki school, travelling in a trustworthy
women's group satisfies it for the obligatory Hajj — which is the basis on which many organised women's
groups travel today. Both are positions with a millennium of scholarship behind them; neither is a
concession invented recently.

Two things everyone in the tradition agrees on: safety of the journey is the underlying concern, and
this is a matter for you to settle with a scholar of the school you follow — not one to settle from a
webpage, including this one.
