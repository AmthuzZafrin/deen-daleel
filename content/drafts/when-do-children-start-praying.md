# When should my children start praying and fasting?

## Answer

**Prayer: seven to teach, ten to insist.** The instruction comes in two well-known wordings. "Teach the child the prayer at seven years, and strike him for it at ten" [1], and in the version through 'Amr b. Shu'ayb: "Command your children to pray when they are seven years old, and strike them for it when they are ten, and separate them in their sleeping places" [2].

**But it is not obligatory on the child until puberty**, and that is the majority position rather than a minority softening. Ibn Qudama gives the reason from the text: "the position of the school, in my view, is by a single narration: that prayer and fasting are not obligatory until he reaches puberty ... because of the Prophet's words: *the pen is lifted from three — from the child until he reaches puberty, from the insane until he recovers, and from the sleeper until he wakes*" [3]. What the seven-and-ten rule is doing, then, is training, not enforcing an obligation the child already carries.

One consequence is worth knowing because it varies: al-Tirmidhi reports that "Ahmad and Ishaq hold this, and they said: whatever prayers the boy omits after ten, he makes them up" [4]. Not everyone required that.

**Fasting: the same shape, one age later, with an extra condition.** Ibn Hajar sets out the landscape: "the majority hold that it is not obligatory on one below puberty, and a group of the early scholars considered it recommended — among them Ibn Sirin and al-Zuhri, and al-Shafi'i said it — that they are commanded to it as training when they are able for it. His companions fixed it at seven and ten, like the prayer; Ishaq fixed it at twelve years; and Ahmad, in one narration, at ten years" [5]. Ibn Qudama adds al-Awza'i's rather practical test: "and among those who held that he is commanded to fast when he is able for it are 'Ata', al-Hasan, Ibn Sirin, al-Zuhri, Qatada and al-Shafi'i. Al-Awza'i said: if he is able to fast three days consecutively without flagging or weakening, he can bear the fast of Ramadan" [6].

Ibn Qudama then explains why fasting gets its own test rather than simply borrowing the prayer's ages: "taking ten as the measure is preferable, because the Prophet commanded striking for the prayer at that age, and measuring the fast by the prayer is better, given how close the two are to each other and their meeting in being bodily acts of worship among the pillars of Islam — **except that fasting is harder, so capability was made a condition for it, because someone may be able for the prayer who is not able for it**" [7]. Age is the marker; capability is the actual criterion.

There is a real dissent here too. Ibn Hajar: "the well-known position from the Malikis is that it is not prescribed for children at all" — and he describes how al-Bukhari answered them, by opening the chapter with a report from 'Umar's time, since the strongest thing the Malikis rely on is the practice of the people of Madina, "and there is no practice to rely on stronger than the practice in the time of 'Umar, given his great scrupulousness and the abundance of Companions in his era; and he said, rebuking a man who broke his fast in Ramadan: how do you break your fast when our children are fasting? And Ibn al-Majishun of the Malikis was exceptional, saying: if children are able for the fast they are bound to it, and if they break it without excuse they must make it up" [8].

**The single most useful thing on this sheet is not a ruling.** It is al-Rubayyi' bint Mu'awwidh describing how the Companions actually did it with small children on 'Ashura': "we used to fast it afterwards, and we would make our children fast, and we would make them a toy out of wool — so when one of them cried for food, we gave him that, until it was time to break the fast" [9]. A distraction made of scraps, a child young enough to cry for food, and adults who plainly did not expect the child to manage it on willpower. That is the register the training is supposed to have.

**And the endpoint — when it stops being training.** Ibn Qudama: "fasting is not obligatory on him until he reaches puberty. Ahmad said of a boy who has had a wet dream: he fasts and does not omit it; and the girl when she menstruates. And this is the position of most of the people of knowledge" [10]. Puberty itself has defined markers. Ibn Qudama: "puberty is attained by one of three causes; the first is *ihtilam*, which is the emission of semen ... in waking or in sleep — and there is no disagreement about this" [11]. Al-Tirmidhi gives the full set as the practice of the scholars: "they hold that when a boy completes fifteen years his ruling is the ruling of men, and if he has a wet dream before fifteen his ruling is the ruling of men. Ahmad and Ishaq said: puberty has three stages — reaching fifteen, or *ihtilam*; and if neither his age nor his *ihtilam* is known, then the growth of hair" [12]. For girls, Ibn Hajar records it as settled: "the scholars are agreed that menstruation is puberty in the case of women" [13].

**So, practically.** Start teaching prayer at seven and expect it to be taken seriously by ten; expect some jurists to require made-up prayers after that age and others not to. Start fasting when the child can actually manage it — around ten in the most-cited view, twelve on Ishaq's, and al-Awza'i's three-consecutive-days test is a sensible way to find out. Neither becomes a duty the child carries until puberty: fifteen, or the physical signs, or menstruation, whichever comes first. And the Companions did it with distractions and short days and a wool toy, not with pressure.
