# Is it wrong to have doubts?

## Answer

**Short answer: the Companions brought this exact complaint to the Prophet ﷺ, and
his answer was not a rebuke.** It was that the distress they felt about the
thoughts was itself a sign of faith. If you are frightened by something that has
surfaced in your own head, this is one of the few questions where the sources
answer you almost word for word.

**The report.** Muslim has a chapter for it — "the waswasa in faith, and what one
who finds it should say" [1] — and under it:
**"إنا نجد في أنفسنا ما يتعاظم أحدنا أن يتكلم به، قال: «وقد وجدتموه؟» قالوا:
نعم، قال: «ذاك صريح الإيمان»"** — *"We find in ourselves something one of us
would find too enormous to speak of." He said: "And you have actually found
this?" They said: yes. He said: "That is sheer faith."* [2]. In another wording:
**"سئل النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم عن الوسوسة، قال: «تلك محض الإيمان»"** — *he was
asked about waswasa and said: that is pure faith* [3].

Notice the shape of the exchange. They expected to be told something was
seriously wrong. He confirmed what they were describing and then reframed it
entirely.

**What that phrase actually means, since it is easy to misread.** The
commentators are careful here. Ibn Hajar quotes al-Khattabi:
**"وليس المراد ان الوسوسة نفسها صريح الايمان بل هي من قبل الشيطان وكيده"** — *it
is not meant that the waswasa itself is sheer faith; rather it is from Shaytan
and his scheming* [4]. Al-Nawawi: **"معناه استعظامكم الكلام به هو صريح
الايمان"** — *the meaning is that your finding it too enormous to utter is the
sheer faith* [5].

So the sign is not the thought. The sign is your **reaction** to the thought.
Al-Tibi puts it exactly: **"أي علمكم بقبيح تلك الوساوس وامتناع قبولكم ووجودكم
النفرة عنها دليل على خلوص ايمانكم فان الكافر يصر على ما في قلبه من المحال ولا
ينفر عنه"** — *your recognition that these whisperings are foul, your refusal to
accept them, and the revulsion you feel toward them, is evidence of the purity of
your faith; for the disbeliever persists in the absurdity in his heart and feels
no revulsion at it* [6].

Ibn Taymiyya states the same test: **"فهنا لما إقترن بالوسواس هذا البغض وهذه
الكراهة كان هو صريح الإيمان وهو خالصه ومحضه لأن المنافق والكافر لا يجد هذا البغض
وهذه الكراهة مع الوسوسة بذلك"** — *here, because the whispering was accompanied
by this hatred and this aversion, that is sheer faith, its pure and unmixed form;
for the hypocrite and the disbeliever do not feel this hatred and aversion
alongside such whispering* [7]. And al-Nawawi records the reading some scholars
gave: **"وقيل معناه أن الشيطان إنما يوسوس لمن أيس من اغوائه فينكد عليه بالوسوسة
لعجزه عن اغوائه"** — *it is said the meaning is that Shaytan only whispers to
one he has despaired of misleading, so he vexes him with whispering because he
cannot mislead him* [8].

**The one this most often is.** The report names the thought people are most
afraid to admit to: **"لا يزال الناس يتساءلون حتى يقال: هذا خلق الله الخلق، فمن
خلق الله؟ فمن وجد من ذلك شيئا، فليقل: آمنت بالله"** — *people will keep asking
until it is said: this is Allah who created creation — so who created Allah?
Whoever finds any of that, let him say: I believe in Allah* [9]. And:
**"فإذا بلغ ذلك، فليستعذ بالله ولينته"** — *when it reaches that, let him seek
refuge in Allah and stop* [10].

That is the prescribed response, and it is worth noticing what it is *not*. It is
not "argue it out". It is: affirm, seek refuge, and disengage. For an intrusive
thought that is exactly right — engaging feeds it.

**It is normal, not rare.** Ibn Taymiyya: **"ولهذا لما كانت هذه الوسوسة عارضة
لعامة المؤمنين"** — *since this whispering happens to believers generally* [11].
He then reads the Qur'anic image of the flood carrying froth, and the ore heated
for its metal throwing off scum, as a picture of exactly this: doubts and false
notions rising in the heart of a believer and being cast off, while what is of
value stays.

And there is a report Ibn Hajar transmits of a man who came saying he had a
thought he would rather be burnt to ashes than utter — the reply was
**"قال الحمد لله الذي رد أمره إلى الوسوسة"** — *praise be to Allah, who reduced
his affair to mere whispering* [12]. A man in terror of his own mind, met with
relief on his behalf.

**Two different things, and the difference matters.**

The passages above address the **intrusive** kind: thoughts that arrive
unbidden, that you find repellent, that you did not choose. For those, the answer
is the one above, and it is emphatic.

**Deliberate questioning** — wanting to understand why you believe something,
reading, asking hard questions — is a different activity, and the hadiths above
are not about it. Nothing quoted here forbids it. What these passages do
establish is that having a thought is not the same as holding a belief [4][6],
which is the distinction the whole anxiety usually rests on.

**Practically.** If it is the intrusive kind: say *amantu billah*, seek refuge,
and turn to something else, exactly as at [9] and [10]. Do not argue with it, do
not investigate whether you "really" think it, and do not confess it to people —
picking at it is what makes it grow. If it is persistent, distressing and
compulsive, treat it as the compulsion it may be; religious scrupulosity is a
recognised pattern and it responds to help, and getting that help is not a
failure of faith.

If it is genuine intellectual questioning, take the questions to someone who
knows the material and will not be scandalised by them. They are not new
questions.

**On the language above:** "waswasa" is a whispering — an intrusive thought;
"sarih al-iman" and "mahd al-iman" both mean sheer, unmixed faith [2][3]; "amantu
billah" is "I believe in Allah", the phrase prescribed at [9]; "isti'adha" is
seeking refuge in Allah.

**A note on scope.** These passages address the anxiety, not the arguments. If
you have specific doubts about specific claims and want them answered rather than
soothed, that is a legitimate request and a different kind of conversation — ask
someone who can have it with you properly.
