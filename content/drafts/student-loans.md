# Are student loans allowed?

## Answer

**Borrowing to study is not the difficulty — the corpus is clear that borrowing for a lawful purpose is unobjectionable. The difficulty is whether a particular scheme charges a stipulated increase on what you borrow, and that turns on how the scheme actually works where you live. Some do; some are structured in ways that contemporary scholars argue are not loans at interest at all.**

**Borrowing itself is not blameworthy.** Ibn Hajar, drawing lessons from the Prophet's own borrowing: `وفيه أن الاقتراض في البر والطاعة وكذا الأمور المباحة لا يعاب وأن للامام أن يقترض على بيت المال لحاجة بعض المحتاجين` [1] — **borrowing for a good and obedient purpose, and likewise for permissible matters, is not to be faulted**; and the ruler may borrow against the treasury for the need of those in want.

Studying is a permissible purpose at minimum, and for many people a good one. Nothing here treats the act of taking a loan for it as a compromise.

The tradition is warmer still about the lender. Al-Qurtubi: `ثواب القرض عظيم لأن فيه توسعة على المسلم وتفريجا عنه` [2] — the reward for lending is great, because it relieves and eases a Muslim; and he cites the report `رأيت ليلة أسري بي على باب الجنة مكتوبا الصدقة بعشر أمثالها والقرض بثمانية عشر فقلت لجبريل : ما بال القرض أفضل من الصدقة قال لأن السائل يسأل وعنده والمستقرض لا يستقرض إلا من حاجة` [3] — charity is tenfold and **a loan eighteen**; why is a loan better than charity? — *because the one who asks may ask while having something, but no one borrows except out of need.*

That is a striking valuation, and it should settle any sense that needing to borrow is shameful. It also carries its obligation: `يجب على المستقرض رد القرض` [4] — repaying is obligatory on the borrower.

**The line that decides the actual question.** Ibn Hajar states it exactly: `وفيه جواز وفاء ما هو أفضل من المثل المقترض إذا لم تقع شرطية ذلك في العقد فيحرم حينئذ اتفاقا وبه قال الجمهور` [5] — it is permitted to repay better than what was borrowed **provided that was not stipulated in the contract; if it was stipulated it is forbidden by agreement**, and this is the majority position. Al-Nawawi puts it in one clause: `لأن المنهي عنه ما كان مشروطا في عقد القرض` [6] — what is prohibited is what was **stipulated in the loan contract**.

So the whole question is: does the scheme require you to repay more than you borrowed, as a term of the agreement?

**Which is why the answer depends on the scheme, and this page cannot see yours.**

- **A commercial loan at a stated interest rate** is the plain case. The increase is stipulated; the material above is unambiguous.
- **A government scheme with an inflation-linked uplift and income-contingent repayment** is the case scholars actually argue about. The features that make people hesitate to call it a straightforward interest loan are real: repayment depends on earning above a threshold, the balance is written off after a period, and the uplift is presented as preserving value rather than as a return to the lender. Contemporary scholars differ on whether that amounts to a stipulated increase or not, and the classical texts do not settle it — no author here had an instrument of that kind in view.
- **Grants, scholarships, sharia-compliant alternatives, family support, or paying as you go** avoid the question. Where these are genuinely available, the reasoning about need does not arise.

**What is genuinely absent.** These books have nothing on inflation as a concept, and therefore nothing that tests the argument that an inflation uplift is not an increase but the preservation of the sum lent. That is the crux of the modern discussion, and I will not manufacture a classical position on it in either direction. Nor is there anything on income-contingent repayment, on the difference between a debt you may never repay and one you certainly will, or on whether a state scheme not run for profit is a lender in the sense these texts assume.

**What to do.**

1. **Find out what your scheme actually charges** — a rate applied to the balance, or an inflation adjustment, and whether repayment is conditional on income. Get this from the scheme's own terms, not from what people say about it.
2. **Exhaust the alternatives first.** Grants, bursaries, scholarships, a cheaper course, part-time study, family help, or an Islamic finance provider where one exists. The argument from need only begins once these are genuinely unavailable.
3. **Take the specifics to a scholar**, and tell them the mechanism rather than the name. This is one of the questions where knowledgeable scholars reach different conclusions on the same country's scheme, and you are entitled to follow a considered position you are given — but you need to give them the actual terms to consider.
4. **Borrow the minimum**, and repay it as the obligation it is. Whatever view is taken of the uplift, the principal is a debt, and the pages on debt describe what carrying one does to a person.

If the honest answer is that your scheme charges a stipulated increase and alternatives exist, the material above is clear about what that is. If your scheme is one of the contested kinds, that is a real dispute among qualified people rather than a loophole, and it deserves to be resolved by someone who can look at the paperwork with you.
