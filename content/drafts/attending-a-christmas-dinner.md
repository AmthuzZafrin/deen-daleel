# Can I go to my family's Christmas dinner?

## Answer

Yes — go. Two distinct questions arise, and the sources answer both, one of them with a stated consensus.

**Question one: the food.**

Ibn Qudama: "**the people of knowledge are agreed on the permissibility of the slaughtered animals of the People of the Book**, because of God's saying *and the food of those who were given the Scripture is lawful for you* — meaning their slaughtered animals. Al-Bukhari said: **Ibn 'Abbas said: their food is their slaughtered animals.** And likewise said Mujahid and Qatada; and its meaning is related from Ibn Mas'ud" [1].

Al-Tabari transmits the same reading from Ibrahim al-Nakha'i: on *and the food of those given the Scripture is lawful for you*, "he said: **their slaughtered animals**" [2].

So the turkey is not the problem. A Christian household's roast is the very case the verse is about, and Ibn Qudama records agreement on it.

The exceptions are the ones you would expect and can handle at the table without a speech: **pork in any form**, **alcohol** (including in a sauce or a pudding), and **anything dedicated to other than God** in the offering. Take what is fine, leave what is not, and say *I don't eat that* if asked.

**Question two: the occasion.**

This is where the caution in the sources lies, and it is worth stating at its strongest before qualifying it.

Ibn Taymiyya: "**eating the sacrifices of their festivals falls within this that they agreed to dislike — indeed in my view it is more severe**" [3]. And on the verse *those who do not witness falsehood*, 'Amr b. Murra's reading: "**they do not keep company with the people of idolatry upon their idolatry, and do not mix with them in it**" [4].

Read that second one closely, because it is the most precise formulation in the file. The prohibition attaches to keeping company with them **in the idolatry** — not to keeping company. A church service is that; a meal in a dining room is not.

**And the Hanafis give the operative test in a related case.**

Discussing fasting on the Persian festival days, Ibn 'Abidin explains why fasting deliberately on *nayruz* or *mihrajan* is disliked, and then when the dislike lifts: "**if he fasted two [days] the dislike goes, because none of them glorifies these two days together** — even though the Christians glorify Sunday" [5]. He notes that these "**are two festivals of the Persians**" [6].

The principle underneath is that the objection is to **singling the day out in a way that constitutes glorifying it**. Do the same act across two days, or for a reason unconnected to the day, and the objection lifts. That is exactly transferable: you are going because your family will be in one room, which happens once a year on that date. You are not going because you honour the date.

**What this comes to.**

- **Go.** Especially to your parents. The thing the sources guard is worship, and a dinner is not worship.
- **Eat their food.** There is a stated consensus that the meat of the People of the Book is lawful, and the objection about festival sacrifices is about meat slaughtered **as an offering for the rite** — which a supermarket turkey is not.
- **Decline the specific items**: pork, alcohol, anything dedicated in the offering.
- **Do not attend the service.** That is the one thing the evidence clearly reaches, and it is easy to be absent for an hour.
- **Do not take part in the rite at the table** if there is one — a grace addressed to a trinity, a toast. Be quiet and courteous; you do not have to perform anything.
- **You do not have to pretend it is your festival.** Going to eat with your mother is not a claim about theology, and everyone at the table knows it.

The risk in this question is not really the one people worry about. The larger risk to a new Muslim is the empty chair — the year the family concludes that the religion has taken you from them. Nothing in these sources asks that of you, and the material on kindness to parents asks the opposite.
