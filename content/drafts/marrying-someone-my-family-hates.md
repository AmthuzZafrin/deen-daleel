# My family won't accept who I want to marry — what now?

## Answer

**Two things are true at once here, and most of the distress in this situation
comes from only being told one of them.** Your family's role in your marriage is
real and the sources take it seriously — it is not a formality you can wave
away. But a guardian who blocks a suitable match is described in the fiqh by a
specific term, *'adl*, and it is treated as a wrong that the authorities are
supposed to correct, not as a right he is exercising.

**The guardian's role.** Ibn Qudama sets out the majority view: **"أن النكاح لا
يصح إلا بولي، ولا تملك المرأة تزويج نفسها ولا غيرها"** — *the marriage is not
valid without a guardian, and a woman does not have the power to marry herself
or another off* [1], citing the report **"أيما امرأة نكحت نفسها بغير إذن وليها،
فنكاحها باطل باطل باطل"** — *any woman who marries herself off without her
guardian's permission, her marriage is void, void, void* [2]. Al-Tirmidhi
reports the same as the practice of the scholars: **"والعمل في هذا الباب على
حديث النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم لا نكاح إلا بولي عند أهل العلم من أصحاب النبي صلى
الله عليه وسلم"** [3]. The Hanafis dissent — Ibn 'Abd al-Barr records that
al-Zuhri and al-Sha'bi held **"إذا تزوجت المراة بغير إذن وليها كفؤا فهو
جائز"** — *if a woman marries a suitable man without her guardian's permission
it is valid* [4], and that Abu Yusuf held the contract stands once the judge
approves it where the guardian will not [5]. So there is a genuine difference of
opinion here, and this answer does not settle which school you follow.

**What the guardian cannot do is force you.** Ibn Hajar, commenting on the
chapter heading **"باب لا ينكح الأب وغيره البكر والثيب الا برضاهما"** — "neither the
father nor anyone else may marry off a virgin or a previously married woman
except with their consent" [6] — and reports it as a point of agreement for a
woman previously married: **"فالثيب البالغ لا يزوجها الأب ولا غيره الا برضاها
اتفاقا"** [7]. On the unmarried woman he says **"والحديث دال على أنه لا اجبار
للأب عليها إذا امتنعت وحكاه الترمذي عن أكثر أهل العلم"** — *the hadith shows the
father has no power to compel her if she refuses, and al-Tirmidhi reported this
from most of the people of knowledge* [8]. The hadith is **"الأيم أحق بنفسها من
وليها، والبكر تستأذن في نفسها وإذنها صماتها"** — *the previously married woman
has more right to herself than her guardian, and the virgin is asked permission,
her silence being her consent* [9]. And there is a case: **"أن الولي لا يزوجها
إلا برضاها وأمرها، فإن زوجها فالنكاح مفسوخ على حديث خنساء بنت خذام؛ حيث زوجها
أبوها وهي ثيب فكرهت ذلك، فرد النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم نكاحه"** — *Khansa' bint
Khidham was married off by her father against her wishes, and the Prophet ﷺ
annulled the marriage* [10].

**And what he cannot do is block a match you want.** Al-Nawawi puts the balance
plainly: **"أن لها في نفسها في النكاح حقا ولوليها حقا وحقها أوكد من حقه"** —
*she has a right in herself regarding marriage and her guardian has a right, and
her right is the stronger of the two* [11]. He then states the remedy:
**"ولو أرادت أن تتزوج كفؤا فامتنع الولي أجبر فإن أصر زوجها القاضي فدل على تأكيد
حقها ورجحانه"** — *if she wishes to marry a suitable man and the guardian
refuses, he is compelled; if he persists, the judge marries her, which shows the
strength and priority of her right* [12].

That is not al-Nawawi's invention. Ibn al-'Arabi reads the Qur'anic prohibition
on *'adl* as directed at exactly this: **"فنهى الله تعالى أولياء المرأة من منعها
عن نكاح من ترضاه"** — *Allah forbade a woman's guardians to prevent her from
marrying one she is content with* [13]. The verse was revealed about Ma'qil b.
Yasar, who refused to let his divorced sister remarry the husband who wanted her
back; Ibn Hajar records the ending — **"فقلت الآن أفعل يا رسول الله قال فزوجها
إياه"** — *I said: now I will do it, Messenger of Allah — and he married her to
him* [14]. Ibn Hajar also gives the procedure: **"وفى حديث معقل أن الولي إذا عضل
لا يزوج السلطان الا بعد أن يأمره بالرجوع عن العضل فان أجاب فذاك وان أصر زوج عليه
الحاكم"** — *the authority does not marry her off until he has ordered the
guardian to withdraw his obstruction; if he complies, well and good, and if he
persists, the judge marries her over his head* [15]. Al-Kasani states the
consequence in Hanafi terms: **"انه بالامتناع صار عاضلا إذ لا يحل له الامتناع من
الإجازة إذا زوجت نفسها من كف ء فإذا امتنع فقد عضلها فخرج من أن يكون وليا
وانقلبت الولاية إلى الحاكم"** — *by refusing he becomes an obstructor, since it
is not lawful for him to refuse where she has married a suitable man; he thereby
ceases to be a guardian and the guardianship passes to the judge* [16]. And Ibn
Hajar notes the principle that fills the gap: **"والسلطان ولى من لا ولى لها"** —
*the authority is the guardian of one who has no guardian* [17].

**Where the line falls.** Ibn 'Abidin is precise about which refusals count as
*'adl* and which do not: **"أي بامتناعه عن التزويج) أي من كف ء بمهر المثل، أما
لو امتنع عن غير الكفء، أو لكون المهر أقل من مهر المثل، فليس بعاضل"** —
*obstruction means his refusal to marry her to a suitable man at a fair dower; if
he refuses because the man is not suitable, or because the dower is below the
fair rate, he is not an obstructor* [18]. So the guardian's veto is real but
bounded: it attaches to the suitability of the man and the fairness of the terms.
What the texts quoted here do *not* do is spell out what makes a man suitable —
that is the concept of *kafa'a*, the schools differ substantially over what it
includes, and it is the question your case probably turns on. Take that one to a
scholar rather than to this answer.

**One thing to rule out immediately: doing it in secret.** Ibn Taymiyya:
**"إذا تزوجها بلا ولي ولا شهود وكتما النكاح فهذا نكاح باطل باتفاق الأئمة"** — *if
he marries her with no guardian and no witnesses and they conceal the marriage,
this marriage is void by the agreement of the imams* [19], and **"ونكاح السر هو
من جنس نكاح البغايا"** — *a secret marriage is of the same kind as the marriages
of prostitutes* [20]. Whatever route you take, a hidden nikah is not one of them.

**So, practically.** Find out what the objection actually is. "We don't like
him" and "he is not a suitable match at a fair dower" are different objections,
and only the second is one the fiqh recognises as a guardian's ground [18]. Ask
someone your family respects — an imam, an older relative, a family friend — to
sit with them; the sequence in the sources is that the obstruction gets named
and the guardian gets a chance to withdraw it before anything is done over his
head [15]. Give it real time; Ma'qil's refusal was answered by pressure and
persuasion, not by going around him [14]. Keep the person you want to marry out
of the argument as far as you can, since their meeting your family is usually
what changes minds.

If it genuinely does not move — if the objection is not one that the sources
recognise and your family will not budge — the route the fiqh provides is to go
to an Islamic authority, an imam or a shari'a council, and have them act in the
guardian's place [12][16][17], not to marry in secret [19][20]. That is a
serious step and it is meant to be a last one.

**One more thing worth saying.** Being caught between your family and the person
you want to marry is genuinely painful, and the fact that the fiqh has a
procedure for it tells you the situation is old and common. Nothing above obliges
you to break with your family; what it establishes is that you are not without
recourse if they will not move.

**On the language above:** "wali" is the marriage guardian, normally the father;
"'adl" is his wrongful refusal to marry her to a suitable man; "kafa'a" is the
suitability of the match, which the schools define differently; "mahr al-mithl"
is the dower comparable women in her family receive; "thayyib" is a woman
previously married, "bikr" one not.

**This is not a ruling on your case.** Whether a particular objection counts as
*kafa'a* or as *'adl*, and whether an imam or council where you live can act as
wali, are questions that turn on your school, your circumstances, and the body
you would go to. Put them to a scholar who can hear the whole situation.
