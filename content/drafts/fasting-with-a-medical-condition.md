# I have a medical condition — should I fast?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

> Reviewer note: Route to a doctor as well as a scholar. Do not rule.

## Answer

**This page cannot tell you whether to fast — that turns on a medical judgement about your body that no book here has access to. But the framework is far clearer, and far more generous, than most people asking this question expect, and knowing it changes what you should be asking your doctor.**

**Illness is not a loophole; it is written into the verse that commands the fast.** *"(Fast) a certain number of days; and (for) him who is sick among you, or on a journey, (the same) number of other days; and for those who can afford it there is a ransom: the feeding of a man in need"* [1]. And when the obligation is stated in full, so is its purpose: *"whosoever of you is sick or on a journey, (let him fast the same) number of other days. Allah desireth for you ease; He desireth not hardship for you"* [2].

The exemption arrives in the same breath as the command. It is not a concession later scholars extracted; it is part of how the obligation was given.

**The standard is unbearable hardship, not danger of death.** This is the single most useful sentence for anyone in your position, and al-Nawawi states it flatly: `هذا إذا كان يناله بالصوم مشقة لا تحتمل ولا يشترط خوف الهلاك` [3] — this applies where fasting brings on hardship that cannot be borne, **and fear of destruction is not a condition**.

A great many people fast through conditions they should not, because they believe the bar is "would this kill me." It is not, and it never was. Ibn Qudama describes the elderly in the same terms: `أن الشيخ الكبير، والعجوز، إذا كان يجهدهما الصوم، ويشق عليهما مشقة شديدة، فلهما أن يفطرا ويطعما لكل يوم مسكينا` [4] — where fasting exhausts them and is severely burdensome. Exhaustion and severe burden, not collapse. Al-Nawawi records agreement on the underlying point: `وأجمعوا على أن للشيخ والعجوز العاجزين الفطر` [5].

**There are two categories, and which one you are in is a medical question.** This is the part to get right, because the obligation that follows is different in each.

*If recovery is expected*, you break the fast and make up the days later. Ibn Qudama: `وهذا محمول على من لا يرجو إمكان القضاء، فإن رجا ذلك فلا فدية عليه، والواجب انتظار القضاء وفعله إذا قدر عليه` [6] — if a person can hope to make the days up, there is no ransom on them; the obligation is to wait and perform the make-up when able. An infection, a course of treatment, a surgery you will recover from: these are that case.

*If recovery is not expected*, the days are not made up at all; food is given instead. `والمريض الذي لا يرجى برؤه، يفطر، ويطعم لكل يوم مسكينا ; لأنه في معنى الشيخ` [7] — **the sick person whose recovery is not hoped for** breaks the fast and feeds one person in need for each day, because he falls under the same meaning as the elderly. A chronic, lifelong condition is that case, and the law does not leave you owing a debt of days you will never be able to repay.

Notice that the deciding fact — whether recovery is expected, and whether the hardship is bearable — is one your doctor knows and this page does not. That is the whole reason it cannot answer you.

Related cases are handled by the same reasoning. On pregnancy and nursing, Ibn Qudama: `أن الحامل والمرضع، إذا خافتا على أنفسهما، فلهما الفطر، وعليهما القضاء فحسب. لا نعلم فيه بين أهل العلم اختلافا ; لأنهما بمنزلة المريض الخائف على نفسه` [8] — where they fear for themselves they may break and simply make up the days, **with no disagreement known among the scholars**, because they stand in the position of a sick person who fears for themselves. And more broadly, Ibn Qudama on someone who fears harm to himself: `أباح له الفطر ; لأنه يخاف على نفسه، فهو كالمريض` [9] — breaking was permitted him because he fears for himself, so he is like a sick person.

**Taking the dispensation is itself righteousness — not a lesser option you should feel bad about.** Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, on the report that fasting while travelling is not righteousness, explains: `السفر بر أيضا لمن شاء أن يأخذ برخصة الله تعالى ذكره` [10] — breaking the fast is also righteousness, for whoever chooses to take God's dispensation. He then reports the practice: `سافرنا مع رسول الله فصام قوم وأفطر قوم فلم يعب الصائم على المفطر ولا المفطر على الصائم` [11] — we travelled with the Messenger of God; some fasted and some broke, and neither faulted the other.

And the tradition preserves a warning against over-reaching in devotion, from someone who did. 'Abdullah b. 'Amr wanted to fast every day and pray every night, and was told: `فإنك لا تستطيع ذلك، فصم وأفطر، ونم وقم` [12] — you will not be able to sustain that; fast and break, sleep and pray. In old age his verdict on his own zeal was: `لأن أكون قبلت الثلاثة الأيام التي قال رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم، أحب إلي من أهلي ومالي` [13] — that I had accepted the three days he named would be dearer to me than my family and my property.

The instinct to push past what your body can carry is not new, and it was corrected in person.

**One principle worth carrying with you.** Ibn al-'Arabi, weighing a case where two harms meet: `فاجتمعت فيه مضرتان دفعت الأعلى بالأدنى فقدم المتحقق على المتوهم` [14] — two harms came together, so the greater was repelled by the lesser, and **the certain was given precedence over the merely supposed**. A definite harm to your body is not traded away against a speculative benefit.

**What this page cannot decide, and why.**

Whether your condition meets the standard above is a fact about you. Insulin-dependent diabetes, kidney disease on dialysis, a medicine that must be taken at fixed hours or with food, a pregnancy with complications, a heart or blood-pressure condition, an eating disorder in recovery — every one of these turns on specifics no text can supply, and several of them are cases where fasting is dangerous for one person and manageable for another with the same diagnosis. The corpus also knows nothing about whether a treatment schedule can be safely shifted, which is often the actual question.

**What to do.**

Ask your doctor a precise question rather than a general one. Not "is fasting bad for me" but: *what specifically happens to my body if I take no food or water from dawn to sunset; can my medication timing be adjusted safely; and is this a temporary situation or a permanent one.* That last part is exactly what decides whether you owe make-up days or feeding.

Then take that answer to a scholar you can speak with, and tell them which of the two categories the doctor puts you in. If the answer is that you should not fast, the material above is unanimous that you are not falling short of anything — you are doing what the verse itself provides for. And if you are told to break the fast, do not test it privately first to see whether you can manage; the standard the jurists set is unbearable hardship, and you are not required to reach it to find out.
