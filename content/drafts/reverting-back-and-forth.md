# I keep slipping and coming back — is that normal?

## Answer

Yes, it is normal. So normal that Sahih Muslim has a chapter for it, and the
chapter is titled: *the acceptance of repentance from sins, even when the sins
and the repentance recur.*

The hadith in that chapter is one you should probably read whole. A servant sins
and says, *my Lord, forgive me my sin.* God says: **my servant knew that he has a
Lord who forgives sin and takes to account for sin.** [1] Then he does it again.
Then again. And at the third or fourth time the answer is: **do what you will, I
have forgiven you.** [2]

That last line has been misread in both directions, so take al-Nawawi's gloss on
it: **its meaning is, so long as you sin and then repent, I forgive you.** [3]
Not a licence to stop caring. A refusal to close the door on someone who keeps
coming back to it.

He states the arithmetic plainly, and there is no upper limit in it: **even if
the sin recurred a hundred times, or a thousand, or more, and he repented each
time, his repentance is accepted and his sins fall away.** [4] He adds something
worth knowing if the record feels too long to itemise — **and if he repented for
all of it with a single repentance afterwards, his repentance is valid.** [5] You
do not have to go back through the list.

**The timing is arranged for people who are inconsistent.** From the same
chapter: **God stretches out His hand by night so that the wrongdoer of the day
may repent, and stretches out His hand by day so that the wrongdoer of the night
may repent** [6] — and it does not stop until the sun rises from the west. There
is not a moment in the cycle at which you are outside the window.

And the sentence that answers your question most directly, narrated by
al-Tirmidhi: **every son of Adam errs, and the best of those who err are the ones
who keep returning.** [7] Not "the ones who never erred." The best of the errers
are the returners — which is a category you can only belong to by having gone
away and come back. Al-Tirmidhi himself notes the report is *gharib*, known only
through one chain; [8] that is his assessment, recorded here rather than tidied
away, and the hadith of Abu Hurayra above carries the same meaning in Sahih
Muslim.

**One distinction, in case you meant something heavier.** Slipping in practice —
missing prayers, going back to something you had left, months where none of it
holds — is what everything above addresses, and it is not leaving Islam. If what
you meant is that you have doubted whether you believe at all, that is a
different question, and there is a separate answer for it in this collection.
Neither is a reason to stop.

The pattern you are describing is not evidence that your Islam did not take. It
is the ordinary shape of a religious life, and it has an entire chapter devoted
to it.
