# Why does Islam have so many rules?

## Answer

Part of the answer is that there are fewer rules than it looks like, and part of it is that the sources themselves treat rule-multiplication as a danger rather than as piety.

**The Qur'an says the burden is meant to be light.** "Allah would make the burden light for you, **for man was created weak**" [1]. And: "with hardship goeth ease" [2].

Ibn Taymiyya states the principle as a summary: "**God has not placed upon the Muslims any constraint in their religion; rather He wills ease for them and does not will hardship for them**" [3].

**Al-Jassas turns that into a working rule, and it is the most useful sentence on this page.** Commenting on *God desireth for you ease; He desireth not hardship for you*: "**this verse is a foundation, that everything which harms a person, exhausts him, brings him illness or increases his illness — he is not charged with it**, because that is contrary to ease" [4]. He extends it across the board: "it indicates that all the obligatory acts and the voluntary ones were only commanded to be done, or permitted to him, **on the condition of the negation of hardship and severe difficulty**" [5]. And he cites the Prophet's own habit as the pattern: "he was never given a choice between two matters but that he chose the easier of them" [6].

Al-Tabari records the same in the mouths of the Companions. A man asked Ibn 'Abbas about fasting on a journey: "**there is an ease and there is a hardship — so take God's ease**" [7]. And Qatada, on the verse: "**so want for yourselves what God wanted for you**" [8].

**The asymmetry between prohibitions and commands is worth learning, because it changes how the whole system feels.** The hadith: "**when I forbid you something, avoid it; and when I command you something, do of it what you are able**" [9]. Prohibitions are absolute; commands are bounded by capacity. Most of what people experience as an oppressive number of rules is on the commands side, and the commands side has *what you are able* written into it.

**And the same hadith names the cause of over-multiplication.** It begins by explaining why earlier communities came to grief — Ibn Hajar glosses the phrase: "*by their questioning* — that is, **because of their questioning**" [10], with their disputing over their prophets. The excess did not come from the revelation. It came from asking for more specification than had been given.

**Ibn al-'Arabi collects the same point on the other side.** He cites the Prophet: "**I was sent with the tolerant, upright way** — and there had been severities and strict obligations among the earlier communities, so God gave this community of leniency and gentleness what He had given no community before it" [11]. And Ibn 'Abbas on *He hath not laid upon you in religion any hardship*: "that is only the breadth of Islam — **what God has placed in it of repentance and of expiations**" [12]. Repentance and expiation are themselves counted as part of the ease: the system is built with a way back into it.

Ibn al-'Arabi's illustration is the *hajj*, where every ritual has an order and every question that day got the same answer: "they began asking him, and a man said: *I did not realise, and I shaved before I sacrificed*. He said: **sacrifice, and there is no harm**. Another came and said: *I did not realise, and I sacrificed before I threw the pebbles*. He said: **throw, and there is no harm**. And he was not asked that day about anything done before or after but that he said: **do it, and there is no harm**" [13]. And among the things Ibn al-'Arabi lists as the lifting of constraint: "**that He does not hold us to account if we forget or make a mistake**" [14].

**There is also a warning, and it is aimed precisely at the impulse to add.** The Prophet described a group who would arise: "**there will come out among you a people whose prayer will make you think little of your own prayer, and whose fasting will make you think little of your own fasting, and whose deeds will make you think little of your own deeds — they recite the Qur'an and it does not pass their throats; they pass out of the religion as an arrow passes through the game**" [15]. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr calls its chain sound and established. It is the sharpest thing in the corpus about mistaking severity for religion — and note that what is described is not laxity but conspicuous excess.

**So, why do there seem to be so many?**

Some of it is real: an entire way of life gets covered, so there is material on food, money, marriage, speech, and the rest — the same way a legal system covers everything, not because each item is a burden but because nothing is left unaddressed.

Some of it is the *fiqh literature*, which is not the same thing as the rules. A book that spends forty pages on wiping over socks is exhausting the possible cases so that a scholar can answer any of them; the reader needs one line of it.

And some of it is added. The sources are unusually direct that this happens, that it happened to earlier communities through over-questioning, and that a reputation for severity is not evidence of being right.

What is actually asked of you is bounded by what you can do, is designed with repentance and expiation built into it, does not hold you to what you forgot or got wrong, and, according to al-Jassas, does not extend to anything that would harm you. If the version of Islam you have encountered does not feel like that, it is worth asking whether you have encountered the religion or somebody's account of it.
