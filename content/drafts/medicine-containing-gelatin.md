# Can I take capsules made with gelatin?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

## Answer

**Gelatin is made by processing collagen out of hide, bone and connective tissue, usually from cattle or pigs. So the question splits into two that the corpus does address at length: may parts of a dead animal other than its flesh be used at all, and does processing change the ruling?** It disagrees on both — and knowing where the disagreement sits is more useful than a verdict, because that is what any modern ruling you are given is actually resting on.

**First: parts of carrion other than the flesh.** The verse forbids `الميتة`, and Ibn Hajar notes the scope: `حرمت عليكم الميتة وهو شامل لجميع أجزائها في كل حال فخصت السنة ذلك بالاكل` [1] — it takes in all its parts in every state, and the Sunna restricted that to eating.

The Hanafi position is directly on point, and it names the relevant tissue. Al-Jassas: `قال أبو حنيفة وأبو يوسف ومحمد وزفر ومحمد بن صالح وعبيدالله بن الحسن يجوز الانتفاع بعظام الميتة ولا بأس بشعر الميتة وصوفها ولا يكون ميتة لأنه يؤخذ منها في حال الحياة` [2] — the bones of carrion may be used, and there is no harm in its hair and wool, and it is not "carrion" because such things are taken from the animal while it is alive. Al-Kasani states the school split: `وأما عظم الميتة وعصبها وشعرها وصوفها وبرها وريشها وخفها وظلفها وحافرها فيجوز بيعها والانتفاع بها عندنا وعند الشافعي رحمه الله لا يجوز بناء على أن هذه الأشياء طاهرة عندنا وعنده نجسة` [3] — bone, **sinew**, hair, wool, fur, feather, hoof and hair-hoof: sellable and usable in our school; not so for al-Shafi'i, the difference resting on whether these things are pure or impure.

That list includes `عصب`, sinew — connective tissue. It is as close as a premodern book comes to naming what gelatin is made from.

Al-Kasani's reasoning is the part that travels: `ولان حرمة الميتة ليست لموتها فان الموت موجود في السمك والجراد وهما حلالان قال عليه الصلاة والسلام أحل لنا ميتتان ودمان بل لما فيها من الرطوبات السيالة والدم` [4] — the prohibition of carrion is not on account of death as such, since fish and locusts are dead and lawful; it is on account of the flowing fluids and blood in it. Parts that do not carry those are not what the prohibition is about.

**And the text on the other side**, which Ibn Qudama treats as decisive: `إني كنت رخصت لكم في جلود الميتة، فإذا جاءكم كتابي هذا فلا تنتفعوا من الميتة بإهاب ولا عصب` [5] — I had permitted you the hides of carrion; when this letter of mine reaches you, do not make use of carrion, neither hide **nor sinew**. He reads it as abrogating the earlier permission, since it came in the last months of the Prophet's life.

So the first question is a genuine four-school disagreement, with sinew named explicitly on both sides.

**Second, and this is where gelatin really turns: can a process lift the ruling?** The classical model is tanning. `أيما إهاب دبغ فقد طهر` [6] — any hide that is tanned is purified; and Ibn Qudama explains the mechanism: `لكون الدبغ إنما يؤثر في دفع نجاسة حادثة بالموت` [7] — because tanning only operates against an impurity that came about *at death*. A process removes the ruling that death imposed.

Whether pig is inside or outside that is itself disputed, and the dissent is worth knowing about. Al-Qurtubi: `المشهور عندنا أن جلد الخنزير لا يدخل في الحديث ولا يتناوله العموم وكذلك الكلب عند الشافعي وعند الأوزاعي وأبي ثور : لا يطهر بالدباغ إلا جلد ما يؤكل لحمه` [8] — the well-known position is that pigskin is not included; al-Awza'i and Abu Thawr held that only the hide of an edible animal is purified by tanning. But: `وسمعت سحنونا يقول لا بأس به وكذلك قال محمد بن عبد الحكم وداود بن علي وأصحابه` [9] — Sahnun said there was no harm in it, and so did Muhammad b. 'Abd al-Hakam, and Dawud and his companions. Even for pig, the classical position is not unanimous.

**The theory underneath, which is why any of this works.** Ibn al-'Arabi: `اعلموا وفقكم الله أن النجاسة ليست بعين حسية وإنما هي حكم شرعي أمر الله بإبعادها` [10] — impurity is not a perceptible substance; it is a legal ruling that God commanded be kept away. If it were a physical thing, no process could remove it. Because it is a ruling attached to a state, a change of state can change it.

And the books do decide transformation cases, in both directions. Blood into musk: `أن الدم لما استحالت رائحته إلى رائحة المسك خرج عن كونه مستخبثا نجسا وأنه صار مسكا وأن المسك بعض دم الغزال` [11] — when blood's scent was transformed into the scent of musk it left off being foul and impure and became musk; and musk is a portion of the gazelle's blood. Wine into vinegar, with the test stated exactly: `والخل النجس إذا صب في خمر فصار خلا يكون نجسا لان النجس لم يتغير، وإذا ألقي في الخمر رغيف أو بصل ثم صار الخمر خلا فالصحيح أنه طاهر` [12] — impure vinegar poured into wine which then becomes vinegar remains impure, **because the impure thing itself did not change**; but if a loaf or an onion is thrown into wine and the wine then becomes vinegar, the sound view is that it is pure.

Read that test carefully: what matters is whether *the thing in question* underwent the change, not whether it was near one.

**So where does gelatin land? The corpus does not decide it, and here is honestly why.** Both arguments are fully formed in these books and neither was aimed at this.

*For permissibility:* gelatin comes from bone and sinew, which the Hanafis hold usable and saleable; the prohibition of carrion is about its fluids and blood, not about death as such; impurity is a ruling rather than a substance; and the tradition already accepts that a process can lift what death imposed.

*Against:* Ibn 'Ukaym's letter names sinew and is read as abrogating; the Shafi'is hold those parts impure from the start; the tanning permission is well-known to exclude pig; and the transformation cases are specific rulings about specific transformations, not a general licence.

Which way a modern ruling goes depends almost entirely on **which school it is working in** and on **what the gelatin is made from**. Bovine gelatin from an animal not slaughtered Islamically is a lighter case than porcine gelatin on every position above; and both are lighter than a case where no alternative preparation exists at all.

**Practically.**

- **Ask the pharmacist first.** A very large number of gelatin-capsule drugs exist as tablets, as liquids, or in plant-derived (HPMC) capsules, and some gelatin is bovine rather than porcine. If an alternative exists, the question becomes much easier on every position set out above — and note that on Ibn al-'Arabi's own reasoning about substitutes, the existence of an alternative is what makes the other one problematic.
- **If it is essential and there is no alternative**, the necessity framework applies, and its standard condition is a doctor's judgement that nothing else will serve. That is a separate question with its own material.
- **Do not stop a prescribed medicine while you work this out.** Nothing above requires it.

**What this does not settle.** Whether industrial hydrolysis of collagen counts as a genuine transformation in the sense of the vinegar and musk cases — that is a chemical judgement no premodern author could make, and it is the actual crux; food gelatin as against medicinal; gelatin in vaccines; and whether gelatin from an animal of a lawful species that simply was not slaughtered Islamically is treated the same as gelatin from pig, which several of the positions above would separate.
