# Can I give zakat to my own family?

## Answer

Some of them, yes — and giving to them is better than giving to a stranger. But three categories are closed to you: your parents and grandparents, your children and grandchildren, and your spouse.

**Why those three are excluded.** Not, as people often assume, simply because you already have to maintain them. Al-Kasani gives the Hanafi reasoning: the condition is that the benefits of ownership must not run between the giver and the receiver, since that would stop the payment being a real transfer of ownership. **On this basis the giving to parents however high and children however low is excluded, because each of them benefits from the wealth of the other** [1]. It would be moving money from one pocket to another.

Al-Jassas shows the maintenance explanation is not sufficient, by testing it: **it indicates that what prevents paying it to them is that each of them is attributed to the other by parentage** [2] — a wealthy father you are not obliged to support is still ineligible, so obligation cannot be the whole reason.

The spouse is settled by consensus in one direction. Al-Kasani: **it is not permissible for a man to pay zakat to his wife, by consensus** [3]. Ibn Hajar reports the same from Ibn al-Mundhir: **they were agreed that a man does not give his wife from zakat, because her maintenance is obligatory on him, so she is made independent of zakat by it** [4].

The reverse — a wife giving zakat to a needy husband — is genuinely disputed, and you may follow either side. Al-Jassas: **they differed over a woman giving her husband from the zakat of her wealth. Abu Hanifa and Malik said she does not give him. Abu Yusuf, Muhammad, al-Thawri and al-Shafi'i said she gives him** [5]. Those who permit it argue from Zaynab, the wife of Ibn Mas'ud: **those who permitted her paying her zakat to him argued from the hadith of Zaynab the wife of 'Abdullah b. Mas'ud, when she asked the Prophet, peace be upon him, about charity to her husband 'Abdullah and to orphans of her brother in her care, and he said: you have two rewards** [6] — the reward of kinship and the reward of charity.

**Everyone else is not merely permitted but preferred.** Al-Jassas states the Shafi'i position, which is close to the practical consensus: **a man is given from zakat those of his relatives whose maintenance is not obligatory on him — and they are those other than the child, the parent and the wife — when they are people of need; they have more right to it than others** [7]. So: brothers and sisters, aunts and uncles, nephews and nieces, cousins, in-laws. Ibn Hajar reads the Zaynab hadith the same way: **in the hadith is encouragement to give charity to relatives, and in the case of obligatory charity it is taken to mean those whose maintenance is not incumbent on the giver** [8].

**One further exclusion, and it surprises people:** the family of the Prophet. Zakat is not lawful to them. The hadith is stark — the Prophet, peace be upon him, seeing his grandson put a date from the charity into his mouth: **do you not know that we do not eat charity?** [9]. Who exactly this covers is disputed; al-Nawawi reports that **Abu Hanifa and Malik said they are Banu Hashim specifically** [10], while the Shafi'is extend it to Banu al-Muttalib. If someone tells you they are a sayyid, give them a gift rather than your zakat.

**A note on the boundary.** Supporting your household is not zakat, but it is not nothing either — Abu Mas'ud al-Badri narrates: **when a Muslim spends on his family, seeking reward for it, it is charity for him** [11]. And al-Hindiyya confirms the exclusion runs across the obligatory payments generally, not zakat alone: **it is not permissible to direct expiation to one to whom zakat may not be paid, such as parents and children and others** [12]. What you spend on your parents and children is rewarded. It just does not discharge this particular obligation.

**In practice:** if your sister is struggling, your zakat can go to her, and it is better spent there than sent abroad. If your mother is struggling, support her — generously — but discharge your zakat elsewhere.
