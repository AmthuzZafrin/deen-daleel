# Can I make du'a in my own language?

## Answer

Yes. Outside the prayer you may ask Allah for anything, in any language you
have. The scholars discussed this specifically, and even the most restrictive
position on the file turns out, when read to the end, to permit it.

**The framing verse.** "And We never sent a messenger save with the language of
his folk, that he might make the message clear for them" (14:4) [1]. Language is
treated in the Qur'an as an instrument of understanding, not as a condition of
being heard.

**The strictest view, and where it lands.** Ibn 'Abidin devotes a section to
exactly this question. He reports al-Qarafi, the Maliki, holding supplication in
a foreign tongue forbidden — with the reason: "on account of its possibly
containing what contradicts due reverence" [2]. Then he reports al-Laqani
qualifying it: "he restricted the foreign expression to that whose meaning is
*unknown*, taking it from his reasoning that it might contain what contradicts
the majesty of lordship" [3] — and the conclusion follows: "**we excluded by that
the case where its meaning is known — in which case its use is permitted
absolutely, in the prayer and outside it** — because Allah said: *and He taught
Adam the names, all of them*, and *and We never sent a messenger save with the
language of his folk*" [4].

So the prohibition was never about the language. It was about not knowing what
you are saying. You know what you are saying in English.

**The Hanafi position: disliked, but mildly.** "What is transmitted among us is
dislike — for it says in *Ghurar al-Afkar*: supplication in a foreign tongue is
disliked, because 'Umar forbade the jargon of the non-Arabs" [5]. And the
underlying reason, from al-Walwalijiyya: "the *takbir* is worship of Allah, and
Allah does not love other than Arabic — and for this reason **supplication in
Arabic is closer to being answered**, and no other tongue occupies the place of
the speech of the Arabs in His pleasure and love for it" [6].

Ibn 'Abidin then states the grade precisely, and this is the operative sentence:
"**the apparent sense of the reasoning is that supplication in other than Arabic
is contrary to what is preferable, and that the dislike in it is of the mild
kind**" [7].

*Khilaf al-awla*, and *karaha tanzihiyya*. Not forbidden. Not sinful. Less good
than the alternative.

**Inside the prayer is a different question.** There the dispute is about
*recitation* rather than supplication, and it is famous. Al-Sarakhsi: "the root
of this question is: if he recites in his prayer in Persian, it is permitted
according to Abu Hanifa, though disliked; and according to his two companions it
is not permitted if he is capable in Arabic — and if he is not capable of it, it
is permitted" [8]. Al-Shafi'i disallowed it outright.

But note what Ibn 'Abidin records about the founder of that position: "**it
preceded at the beginning of the chapter that the Imam retracted to their view**
— that praying with recitation in Persian is not permitted except when unable in
Arabic" [9]. Abu Hanifa himself came off the permissive position on recitation.

For the *supplication* portions inside the prayer, the Hanafis kept more room —
Qadi Khan records: "**in the funeral prayer, if the imam supplicates in Persian
it is valid, and the people's following him is valid, according to Abu Hanifa —
whether he is capable in Arabic or not**" [10].

**And one detail worth carrying away.** Al-Sarakhsi, arguing his school's case,
lists what is unquestionably valid in any language: "**and if he believed in
Persian he would be a believer** — and likewise if he pronounced Allah's name in
Persian at the slaughter, or made the *talbiya* in Persian" [11]. The declaration
of faith itself, in Persian, makes you a Muslim. Whatever the argument about
recitation, no one held that Allah does not understand you.

**So, practically.** Say the fixed parts of the prayer in Arabic — the recitation
and the prescribed formulas — since that is where the dispute is real and the
safest course is clear. Everything else, ask for in English: in sujud, after the
prayer, in the car, at night, in whatever words come. That is permitted outright
on the Maliki qualification and mildly less-preferred on the Hanafi one, and
nobody on this file says it is unheard.

Learning a few Arabic supplications alongside is worth doing, on the reasoning
al-Walwalijiyya gives. But an English du'a you mean is better than an Arabic one
you are only reciting — the whole objection to foreign languages was about not
knowing the meaning, and that objection cuts both ways.
