# What is the waiting period after divorce or death?

## Answer

The *'idda* is the period a woman waits before she may remarry. Its length depends on how the marriage
ended and on her circumstances, and the Qur'an gives each case separately.

### The four cases

**Divorced, and menstruating.** Three menstrual cycles: **"Women who are divorced shall wait, keeping
themselves apart, three (monthly) courses"** [1]. (The schools differ over whether *quru'* means the
periods themselves or the intervals of purity between them — which changes the count by a few weeks, not
the principle.)

**Widowed.** Four months and ten days: **"Such of you as die and leave behind them wives, they (the
wives) shall wait, keeping themselves apart, four months and ten days"** [2].

**Not menstruating** — whether too young, past menopause, or for any reason no cycles occur: **"And for
such of your women as despair of menstruation, if ye doubt, their period (of waiting) shall be three
months, along with those who have it not"** [3].

**Pregnant.** Until the birth, whatever the cause of the separation: **"And for those with child, their
period shall be till they bring forth their burden"** [3]. This overrides the others, and al-Qurtubi
records it as agreed: **"the scholars have reached consensus that if she were pregnant and did not know
of the husband's divorce or death, and then delivered her child, her waiting period has expired"** [4].

### Who owes the widow's 'idda

Almost everyone. Al-Qurtubi: **"the waiting period of death is binding upon the free woman and the
bondwoman, the young and the old, the one who has not reached menstruation and the one who menstruates,
the one past menstruation, and the woman of the People of the Book — whether he consummated with her or
not — provided she is not pregnant"** [5]. Its length: **"and the waiting period of all of them — except
the bondwoman — is four months and ten days, by the generality of the verse"** [6].

Note the difference from divorce there. A divorced woman who was never touched owes no waiting period at
all; a widow owes one regardless. The death 'idda is not only about establishing paternity.

### When it starts

From the event, not from the news. Al-Jassas: **"it is obligatory that the waiting period in both cases
run from the day of the death and of the divorce"** [7] — **"and likewise in divorce, from the day he
divorced; this is the position of al-Aswad b. Yazid among others, and it is the position of the jurists
of the cities"** [8].

There was a minority: **"'Ali, al-Hasan al-Basri and Khilas b. 'Amr said: from the day the news reaches
her, in the case of death"** [9]. Al-Jassas answers with two arguments. The waiting period is not an act
she performs: **"the waiting period is not her act, such that her knowledge would be a consideration in
it; rather it is the passing of time"** [10]. And it follows from the death exactly as inheritance does:
**"since the waiting period is made obligatory by the death, like inheritance — and in inheritance the
time of death is what counts, not the time its news arrives"** [11].

So a woman who learns of her husband's death five months late has completed her waiting period already.

For the month-based periods, if it began at a new moon, count lunar months as they fall: **"if it became
obligatory with the sighting of the crescent, she counts by the crescents, whether the month is deficient
or complete"** [12]. If it began mid-month, the schools have their own reckoning to make up the days.

### Mourning, and what it is not

The widow also observes *ihdad* — leaving off adornment. Al-Qurtubi distinguishes it sharply from the
waiting period itself, and the distinction is the practically useful part: **"do you not see that the
young girl's waiting period expires and there is no mourning upon her"** [13], and the reasoning for the
minority view he is discussing is that **"the waiting period is an act of worship by leaving off
adornment, and that is only valid with intention and purpose"** [14] — which al-Qurtubi rejects, because
the period runs whether or not she knew.

The upshot: failing to observe the mourning does not invalidate or extend the waiting period. They are
separate obligations.

### What the waiting period is for

Three things the sources put together: establishing whether there is a pregnancy, giving a revocable
divorce time to be reversed, and — for a widow — a period of grief the law protects rather than hurries.
The last is why [5] extends it to women for whom paternity could not be in question.

### A note on the exceptions

Whether a *khul'* requires three cycles or one, and how the waiting period interacts with a marriage
never consummated, are separate disputes. If your situation is unusual — an absent husband, an unclear
date of divorce, irregular cycles — the counting rules above are the framework, not the answer. Take the
specifics to a scholar.
