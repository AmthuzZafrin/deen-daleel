# Which English translation should I use?

## Answer

**This site cannot name a translation, and it is worth being straightforward about why.** Every work in the corpus behind these answers was written before 1836. Not one of them has seen an English translation of the Qur'an, so there is no passage anywhere in it that recommends Pickthall over Abdel Haleem, or warns you off anyone. A page that produced a recommendation here would be producing it from somewhere other than the sources — which is exactly what this project exists not to do.

What the sources *can* do is answer the question underneath yours: **why do translations differ so much, and what should I make of it?** On that they are unexpectedly direct, because the Arabic text itself was transmitted in more than one valid reading, and the scholars had to work out what to do about that.

**The variation is built in, and it was a concession.** Ibn al-'Arabi on the cause of the reciters' differences: "that was only on account of the latitude that God permitted in it and showed mercy through — the recitation of the Qur'an upon seven modes. So the Prophet taught it in them, and each of his Companions took one mode, or a portion of them" [1].

**And the crucial classification is Ibn Taymiyya's.** Where two readings carry different meanings: "the meaning of one of them may not be the meaning of the other, yet both meanings are true — and this is a difference of variety and diversity, **not a difference of contradiction and mutual exclusion**" [2]. He then draws the consequence: "these readings in which the meaning varies are all true, and each reading of them, together with the other reading, stands as one verse stands with another verse. Faith in all of them is obligatory, and following what they contain of meaning, in knowledge and in practice — **it is not permissible to abandon what one of them requires on account of the other**" [3]. And he classifies a middle case: "among the readings are those in which the meaning agrees in one respect and diverges in another — as in *yakhda'un* and *yukhadi'un*, *yakdhibun* and *yukadhdhibun*, *lamastum* and *lamastum*, *hatta yathurna* and *yattahharna*, and the like" [4].

That is the exact situation a reader of translations is in. Two English versions of the same verse that do not say the same thing are not necessarily one right and one wrong. The tradition already had a category for that, and it did not resolve it by picking a winner.

He makes the practical point sharply elsewhere: "those jurists who said a thing is obligatory on the reading of one who affirms it, or disliked on the reading of one who does not, **have erred**. Rather the Qur'an indicates the permissibility of both matters ... and one who leaves what another recited does not say that the other's reading is disliked — **rather all of that is permissible by agreement**, even if each group prefers something" [5]. Al-Jassas grounds why: "the two readings can only be by direct instruction from the Messenger to the Companions upon them both" [6].

**The other half of the story is the one place where the tradition did choose to standardise, and why.** Ibn Hajar records the trigger: "'Uthman said: **Do you doubt about the Qur'an?** You say *the reading of Ubayy*, *the reading of 'Abdullah*, and another says: by God, your reading is not straight" [7]. 'Ali's account of the decision: "I hold that we should gather the people on a single *mushaf*, so that there be no split and no dispute. We said: an excellent view" [8]. Ibn Hajar also gives the terminology precisely: "the difference between the *suhuf* and the *mushaf* is that the *suhuf* are the bare sheets in which the Qur'an was gathered in Abu Bakr's time — and they were separate *suras*, each *sura* ordered by its verses on its own, but not arranged one after another; and when they were copied and arranged one after another, they became a *mushaf*" [9].

Ibn Taymiyya reports where that left the seven modes: "what the majority of the scholars among the early generations and the imams hold is that they are one mode of the seven modes — indeed they say that 'Uthman's *mushaf* is one of the seven modes, and it contains the final review" [10]. And Ibn al-'Arabi adds a point that matters for anyone thinking a printed book settles a reading: "these *masahif* were only a reminder, so that the Qur'an would not be lost. **As for the recitation, it was taken by transmission, not from the *masahif***" [11].

**One rule the reciters worked out is directly transferable.** On mixing readings, Ibn Hajar reports the fatwa: "it is permitted to recite it, on condition that the meaning is not disturbed and the grammatical construction is not altered" [12]. Coherence, not consistency of source, is the test.

**So, what to actually do.**

Read more than one translation, and read them side by side on any verse that matters to you. That practice is the closest English equivalent to what the tradition itself preserved — a set of readings held together rather than reduced to one — and where two translations diverge, Ibn Taymiyya's categories tell you what kind of divergence you are probably looking at. Note that the parallel is my inference from what these passages say, not a ruling any of them gives.

Where a translation carries you to a legal conclusion, stop and check a *tafsir* rather than trusting the English. That is exactly what the *lamastum* / *lamastum* case shows: a single word, two valid readings, a whole chapter of *fiqh* turning on it, and jurists who knew both.

And for a recommendation of a specific edition, ask a person who knows both languages and knows what you want it for — study, memorisation, or reading straight through. That is a real question with real answers; it is just not one this corpus can answer, and saying so is more useful than inventing one.

Arabic:
