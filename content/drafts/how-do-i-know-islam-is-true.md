# How do I know Islam is true?

## Answer

**First, the question is a permitted one — the sources insist on it.** Al-Qurtubi, on the closing verses of Al 'Imran, says God ended the sura `بالأمر بالنظر والاستدلال في آياته إذ لا تصدر إلا عن حي قيوم قدير وقدوس سلام غني عن العالمين حتى يكون إيمانهم مستندا إلى اليقين لا إلى التقليد` [1] — with the command to look and to draw inference from His signs, so that their faith would rest on certainty and not on imitation. And on the verses about God's oneness: `لم يقتصر بها في وحدانيته على مجرد الأخبار حتى قرن ذلك بالنظر والأعتبار في آي من القرآن` [2] — He did not confine the matter to bare announcement, but joined it to looking and to reasoning. Wanting a reason is not a defect of faith in this tradition. It is what you were told to do.

**Second — and this is the most practically useful thing here — there are two orders in which people actually arrive, and both are recognised.** Ibn Taymiyya records the dispute. Qadi Abu Ya'la held that `ومثبتوا النبوات حصل لهم المعرفة بالله تعالى بثبوت النبوة من غير نظر واستدلال في دلائل العقول خلافا للأشعرية في قولهم لا تحصل حتى تنظر وتستدل بدلائل العقول` [3] — those who establish prophethood arrive at knowledge of God *through* the establishing of prophethood, without first working through the rational proofs; against the Ash'aris, who held that it does not come until you reason it out. His argument: `أن النبوة إذا ثبتت بقيام المعجزة علمنا أن هناك مرسلا أرسله إذ لا يكون هناك نبى إلا وهناك مرسل` [4] — if prophethood is established by a miracle standing, we know there is One who sent him, since there is no prophet without a Sender.

Then the observation that matters most. Al-Bayhaqi, citing al-Khattabi, notes that some argued from the premises of prophethood rather than from metaphysics `لأن دلائلها مأخوذة من طريق الحس لمن شاهدها ومن طريق استفاضة الخبر لمن غاب عنها فلما ثبتت النبوة صارت اصلا في وجوب قبول ما دعا إليه النبى وعلى هذا الوجه كان إيمان أكثر المستجيبين للرسول` [5] — because that evidence is available by direct perception to those who witnessed it and by widely-diffused report to those who did not; and *this was how most of those who answered the Messenger came to believe*. He gives as instances the account of Ja'far and his companions before the Negus, and the bedouin who asked who created the sky [6].

So: you are not required to complete a course in metaphysics before you are entitled to believe. Most people did not. Both routes are below.

**Route one — from the world to a Maker.** Al-Qurtubi's argument is that things cannot bring themselves about: `لأن الإحداث لا يتأتي إلا من حي عالم قادر مريد وما ليس بموجود لا يصح وصفه بذلك وإن كانت موجودة فوجودها يغني عن إحداث أنفسها` [7] — origination can only come from something living, knowing, capable and willing; what does not exist cannot be described that way, and what already exists has no need to originate itself. He puts the reductio plainly: `فلو جاز ما قالوه لجاز أن يحدث البناء نفسه وكذلك النجارة والنسج وذلك محال` [8] — on their view the building would build itself, and likewise the carpentry and the weaving.

And he then turns the reader inward, which is the part worth actually doing rather than reading: `فالأنسان إذا تفكر بهذا التنبيه بما جعل له من العقل في نفسه رآها مدبرة وعلى أحوال شتى مصرفة كان نطفة ثم علقة ثم مضغة ثم لحما وعظما` [9] — a person who reflects on himself with the intellect he was given sees himself governed and turned through successive states.

**Route two — was Muhammad ﷺ sent?** The tradition's central answer is the Qur'an itself, and specifically a standing challenge that was never met. `though mankind and the jinn should assemble to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like thereof though they were helpers one of another` [10]; and, of those who called it invented speech, `Then let them produce speech the like thereof, if they are truthful` [11]. Al-Tabari opens his commentary on exactly this: `لو اجتمع جميع من بين أقطارها، من جنها وإنسها، وصغيرها وكبيرها، على أن يأتوا بسورة من مثله، لم يأتوا بمثله، ولو كان بعضهم لبعض ظهيرا` [12].

Ibn Hajar explains why the sign took that particular form, and the explanation is a good one: each prophet's miracle matched what his people were expert in. `كما كان السحر فاشيا عند فرعون فجاءه موسى بالعصا على صورة ما يصنع السحرة لكنها تلقفت ما صنعوا ولم يقع ذلك بعينه لغيره وكذلك احياء عيسى الموتى وابراء الأكمه والأبرص لكون الأطباء والحكماء كانوا في ذلك الزمان في غاية الظهور فأتاهم من جنس عملهم بما لم تصل قدرتهم إليه ولهذا لما كان العرب الذين بعث فيهم النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم في الغاية من البلاغة جاءهم بالقرآن الذي تحداهم أن يأتوا بسورة مثله فلم يقدروا على ذلك` [13] — sorcery was rife under Pharaoh, so Musa came with the staff; physicians and sages were at their height in 'Isa's time, so he came raising the dead and healing; and the Arabs were at the summit of eloquence, so he came to them with the Qur'an and challenged them to bring one sura like it, and they could not.

Then Ibn Hajar gives the difference that bears directly on someone asking this today: `معجزات الأنبياء انقرضت بانقراض اعصارهم فلم يشاهدها الا من حضرها ومعجزة القرآن مستمرة إلى يوم القيامة وخرقة للعادة في أسلوبه وبلاغته واخباره بالمغيبات فلا يمر عصر من الاعصار الا ويظهر فيه شئ مما أخبر به أنه سيكون يدل على صحة دعواه وهذا أقوى المحتملات` [14] — the earlier prophets' miracles died out with their generations, so only those present ever saw them; the Qur'an's stands until the Day of Judgement. He calls this the strongest of the interpretations. And: `المعجزات الماضية كانت حسية تشاهد بالابصار كناقة صالح وعصا موسى ومعجزة القرآن تشاهد بالبصيرة` [15] — past miracles were sensory, seen with the eyes; this one is seen with insight. That is the direct answer to "everyone's evidence is a story I have to take somebody's word for — what is there that I can look at myself?"

**Three further strands the sources use.** Fulfilled prediction: al-Jassas, on the account of Badr, says `في هذه القصة ضروب من دلائل النبوة أحدها إخباره إياهم بأن إحدى الطائفتين لهم وهي عير قريش التي كانت فيها أموالهم وجيشهم الذين خرجوا لحمايتها فكان وعده على ما وعده` [16] — the promise of one of the two parties came out as promised. A challenge that was declined: Ibn Hajar notes `وأشهر ذلك تحديه اليهود أن يتمنوا الموت فلم يقع ممن سلف منهم ولا خلف من تصدى لذلك ولا أقدم مع شدة عداوتهم لهذا الدين وحرصهم على افساده والصد عنه` [17]. And a methodological point that is unusually honest: on the many other reported miracles — water from between his fingers, food multiplied, the moon split — Ibn Hajar concedes the individual reports are `ظنية`, probable rather than certain, and rests the case on the aggregate instead: `ومجموع ذلك يفيد القطع بأنه ظهر على يده صلى الله عليه وسلم من خوارق العادات شئ كثير كما يقطع بوجود جود حاتم وشجاعة على وإن كانت أفراد ذلك ظنية` [18] — the sum of them yields certainty that many extraordinary things occurred at his hands, the way one is certain of Hatim's generosity and 'Ali's courage even though no single report of either is decisive. Finally, internal coherence is offered as a test you can run yourself: `Will they not then ponder on the Qur'an? If it had been from other than Allah they would have found therein much incongruity` [19].

**What this does not give you, honestly stated.** The strongest argument in the corpus is the inimitability of the Arabic, and it is at full force only for someone who can read Arabic well. In translation you get the coherence argument, the fulfilled predictions and the aggregate historical case, but not the one al-Tabari and Ibn Hajar lean on hardest. That is a real limitation and worth naming rather than papering over. These works also predate every modern form of the argument — historical-critical scholarship, comparative religion, the objections raised today — and they do not engage them. Nothing here compels assent, and none of these authors claims it does; the verses command you to *look*, which is an instruction, not a proof. Al-Qurtubi attaches a warning to the verse about the creation of the heavens and the earth: `ويل لمن قرأها ولم يتفكر فيها` [20] — woe to whoever recites it and does not reflect on it.

**What to actually do.** Read the Qur'an through, attentively, at least once — that is what the coherence argument asks of you and it is the one test the sources put in your own hands. Look at the case for prophethood before you try to settle metaphysics, because that is the order in which the tradition itself says most people arrived. And if you find you are not certain, note that the sources treat certainty as the end of a process of looking, not the entry ticket to it.
