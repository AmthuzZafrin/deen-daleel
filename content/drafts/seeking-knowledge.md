# How much religious knowledge do I actually need?

## Answer

Less than you fear, and it is a finite list. The tradition does not ask every Muslim to become a scholar; it asks you to learn what your own life actually requires, and it names the items.

**The famous hadith — and its honest grading.**

"Seeking knowledge is an obligation upon every Muslim; and the one who places knowledge with those unfit for it is like one who hangs jewels, pearls and gold on swine" [1].

Ibn Majah's editor does not let this pass unexamined, and the note is a model worth reading: "in *al-Zawa'id*: its chain is weak, because of the weakness of Hafs b. Sulayman. Al-Suyuti said: Shaykh Muhyi al-Din al-Nawawi was asked about this hadith and said: **it is weak — that is, in chain; even if it is sound — that is, in meaning.** And his student Jamal al-Din al-Mizzi said: this hadith has been narrated through routes reaching the rank of *hasan*. And it is as he said" [2].

So: the chain of the best-known wording is weak, the meaning is agreed, and the accumulated routes lift it to *hasan*. That is the accurate position, and it is worth stating rather than either quoting the hadith uncritically or dismissing it.

**What "obligation upon every Muslim" actually means.**

Al-Jassas splits it in two: "with us this resolves into two meanings. **One is seeking knowledge of what a person is tested by, of the affairs of his religion, so it is upon him to learn it** — like one who does not know the limits of the prayer and its obligatory acts and the arrival of its time: it is upon him to learn them; and like one who owns two hundred dirhams: it is upon him to learn what is due from him on it; and likewise fasting, Hajj and the rest of the obligations. And the other meaning is that it is obligatory upon every Muslim but as a communal obligation — if some of them undertake it, it falls away from the rest" [3].

Notice the shape of the first sense. The obligation is **indexed to your circumstances**. You must learn the rules of what you are actually doing. Someone with savings must learn zakat; someone entering a marriage must learn what marriage entails; someone running a business must learn what makes a sale valid. Someone who does none of these does not have to learn them yet.

**The list.**

Ibn 'Abidin gives it concretely: "there is no doubt about the obligatory nature of **knowledge of the five obligations**, and **knowledge of sincerity** — because the soundness of the deed depends on it — and **knowledge of the lawful and the forbidden**, and **knowledge of ostentation**, because the worshipper is deprived of the reward of his deed by ostentation, and **knowledge of envy and self-admiration**, since these two devour deeds as fire devours firewood; and **knowledge of buying and selling, marriage and divorce, for whoever intends to enter into these things**; and **knowledge of the forbidden or disbelief-entailing expressions**" [4].

Look at what that list contains. Four of the seven items are about the *heart* — sincerity, ostentation, envy, self-admiration — not about rulings at all. That is a corrective to the assumption that religious knowledge means memorising fiqh.

On the last item he adds an aside that has aged well: "and by my life, this is among the most important of matters in this age — for you hear many of the common people speaking with what constitutes disbelief while they are heedless of it" [5].

**What is *not* individually obligatory — and why your job may count.**

Ibn 'Abidin defines the other category: "as for the communal obligation of knowledge, it is **every science that cannot be dispensed with in the upkeep of worldly affairs** — such as medicine, arithmetic, grammar, language, theology, the Qur'anic readings, the chains of hadith… and the foundations of crafts and agriculture, like weaving, governance and cupping" [6].

Medicine, arithmetic, farming, trades, administration — all classed as obligations the community must have someone fulfilling. If you are a doctor, an engineer, a nurse or a plumber, you are not doing something spiritually neutral while religion happens elsewhere.

Ibn 'Abidin defines the distinction: "a communal obligation is one in which the performance of some suffices for all, because the aim is that it come into being from the totality of the responsible — like washing the deceased and shrouding him and returning the greeting; unlike the individual obligation, where what is sought is its performance by every individual — that is, by every specific responsible person — so the performance of some does not suffice for the rest" [7].

And which is greater? He records both views and where the school lands: "the individual obligation is superior to the communal, because it is obligatory as a right owed by the self, so it is more important to it and more burdensome — unlike the communal obligation, which is obligatory as a right owed by the generality… and a command, when it is general, is light, and when it is specific, is heavy" [8].

**The merit of seeking it.**

Safwan b. 'Assal's report: "Whoever travels a path seeking knowledge in it, Allah makes easy for him a path to Paradise. And the angels lower their wings in approval for the seeker of knowledge. And the seeker of knowledge — those in the heavens and the earth seek forgiveness for him, even the fish in the water. And the merit of the scholar over the worshipper is like the merit of the moon over the rest of the stars. **The scholars are the heirs of the prophets** — the prophets left neither dinar nor dirham as inheritance; they left only knowledge. So whoever takes it has taken an abundant share" [9].

Ibn Hajar also places learning first among the stages of struggle against the self: "the struggle of the self has four ranks: **carrying it to learn the affairs of the religion**, then carrying it to act upon that, then carrying it to teach one who does not know, then calling to the oneness of Allah" [10].

Learn, act, teach, call — in that order. Which is a useful warning against the common inversion of starting at "call."

**One serious caution.**

"Whoever learns knowledge for other than Allah, or intends by it other than Allah, let him take his seat in the Fire" [11].

That is one reason *knowledge of sincerity* appears in Ibn 'Abidin's obligatory list ahead of most rulings. It is possible to accumulate a great deal of this and be worse off for it.

**In practice.** If you are new to Islam, or returning to it, this is your syllabus and it is short:

1. How to pray, and when — properly, not approximately.
2. Purification: wudu, ghusl, what invalidates them.
3. Fasting Ramadan, and its excuses.
4. Zakat — only once you have savings above the threshold.
5. Hajj — only when it becomes possible for you.
6. What is halal and haram in food, income and dealings you are actually engaged in.
7. The diseases of the heart: sincerity, showing off, envy, self-admiration.
8. What not to say — expressions that carry disbelief without the speaker realising.

Learn each as it becomes relevant. A person who prays correctly, earns lawfully, and watches his heart has met the obligation, even if he cannot name a single madhhab. Everything beyond that is a merit worth pursuing — but do not let the size of what you *could* learn stop you from learning the part that is actually required of you this year.
