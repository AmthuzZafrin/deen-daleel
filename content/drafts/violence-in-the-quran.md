# What about the violent verses in the Quran?

## Answer

The Qur'an contains commands to fight. Pretending otherwise would be dishonest and would also be useless, since anyone can look them up. What the *tafsir* tradition adds is the thing that usually goes missing: **the same verses carry restrictions, the commentators argued for centuries about their scope, and one of the most-repeated modern claims about them — that a later verse cancelled the restrictions — was rejected by al-Tabari with an argument you can check.**

**Start with the verse and its limit clause.** "Fight in the way of Allah against those who fight against you, **but begin not hostilities. Lo! Allah loveth not aggressors**" [1].

The word for aggression is what the commentators spend their time on, because it is what sets the boundary. Ibn 'Abbas glossed it: "**do not kill women, nor children, nor the very old, nor anyone who offers you peace and holds back his hand — if you do this, then you have committed aggression**" [2].

'Umar b. 'Abd al-'Aziz wrote the same to one of his governors, and al-Tabari preserves the letter: "**I have found a verse in the Book of God** — *fight in the way of God those who fight you, and do not commit aggression; God does not love the aggressors* — **that is: do not fight one who does not fight you**, meaning women, children and monks" [3].

Ibn Kathir gathers al-Hasan al-Basri's fuller list of what counts as the forbidden aggression: "**mutilation, misappropriation of spoils, killing women and children and old men who have neither counsel nor fight in them, and monks and hermits, and burning trees and killing animals without benefit**" [4].

**Now the abrogation claim, which is the crux.** Some early authorities held that this restraint was cancelled. Al-Tabari reports it: "some said this verse is the first verse revealed commanding the Muslims to fight the people of association ... then it was abrogated by *Bara'a*" [5], with 'Abd al-Rahman b. Zayd naming the verse *slay the idolaters wherever ye find them* as the abrogator [6]. Others disagreed: "**others said: rather, that is a command from God to the Muslims to fight the disbelievers, and it was not abrogated** — the aggression He forbade them is His prohibition of killing women and children. They said: **and the prohibition of killing them is a ruling still in force today**" [7].

Al-Tabari then adjudicates, and this is the sentence worth carrying:

> "**The worthier of these two positions is that which 'Umar b. 'Abd al-'Aziz stated** — because a claimant's claim that a verse is abrogated, when it may well not be abrogated, without evidence for the soundness of his claim, **is arbitrary assertion; and arbitrary assertion is something nobody is incapable of**" [8].

Anyone can assert that a verse has been cancelled. The burden is on whoever asserts it, and al-Tabari does not think it has been discharged. Ibn Kathir, from a different interpretive angle, is equally unimpressed by the abrogation claim — of 'Abd al-Rahman b. Zayd's version he says simply "**and in this there is something to look into**" [6]. Al-Tabari's own reading of the verse's logic: "**because He permitted refraining from whoever refrains**" [9].

That is not a fringe position being quoted for comfort. It is the position of the earliest and most cited Qur'an commentary in the tradition, argued on method rather than sentiment. And the section of this bank on abrogation records the wider rule it rests on: reconciliation before abrogation, and abrogation established only by explicit statement, a Companion's report, or known chronology — not by a reader's sense that two verses clash.

**On compulsion in religion, the verse is direct and so is the exegesis.** "**There is no compulsion in religion.** The right direction is henceforth distinct from error" [10]. Ibn Kathir: "**do not compel anyone to enter the religion of Islam**, for it is clear, evident, its proofs and demonstrations plain; it has no need for anyone to be compelled to enter it. Rather, whoever God guides to Islam and expands his breast and illuminates his insight enters it upon clear evidence; and whoever God has blinded his heart and sealed his hearing and sight — **entering the religion compelled and coerced will not benefit him**" [11].

Its occasion is worth knowing, because it is not abstract. Among the Ansar were women who, having lost children, had vowed to raise a surviving child among the Jews; when the Ansar became Muslim, "**they said: what shall we do about our sons? And this verse was revealed**" [12]. Al-Tabari: "when God brought Islam they wanted to compel them to it, **so God forbade them that, until they themselves would choose to enter Islam**" [13] — and Sa'id b. Jubayr's summary of the outcome: "**whoever wished to stay, stayed; and whoever wished to go, went**" [14]. Muslim fathers were told they could not force their own Jewish and Christian sons. Ibn Kathir notes that although the occasion was specific, "**its ruling is general**" [15].

**A word on method, since this question is usually asked in an argument.** Ibn Taymiyya: "**it is not possible for there to be in the Qur'an anything contradicting plain reason and perception without the Qur'an itself containing the clarification of its meaning** — for God made the Qur'an a healing for what is in the breasts and an exposition for people" [16]. He immediately concedes the honest part: "many people are troubled by texts they do not understand, so those are difficult *relative to them*, on account of their understanding falling short of the meanings."

That is the right posture for a verse that troubles you: not that it cannot mean what it appears to, and not that your difficulty is illegitimate — but that the material for understanding it exists, and that reading a battlefield verse without its restriction clause, its occasion and its commentary is reading a fragment.

**And what this page cannot do for you.** It cannot address a specific verse you have been shown, because the sheet behind it is about 2:190 and 2:256. It cannot tell you what any modern group does or why. If a particular passage is troubling you, the useful move is to look up what the commentators said about that passage — which, as al-Tabari's handling shows, is frequently the opposite of what it is being used to prove.
