# Is a hair transplant allowed?

> Reviewer note: Sits between the wasl reports and the accepted permission to remove a defect. The distinction the jurists drew — restoring what was lost against altering what was given — is the useful thing here.


## Answer

**This question sits exactly on a line the jurists drew deliberately: between restoring what was lost and altering what was given.** The texts on one side are severe. The text on the other side is a Companion with a rotting silver nose being told by the Prophet ﷺ to get a gold one. Knowing which side a hair transplant falls on is the whole question, and it is not obvious.

**The severe side: joining hair, and changing creation.**

`لعن الله الواصلة والمستوصلة` [1] — God has cursed the woman who joins hair and the one who has it joined for her. Al-Nawawi reports that some took this absolutely: `الوصل ولعن الواصلة والمستوصلة مطلقا وهذا هو الظاهر المختار` [2] — unrestricted, and that is the apparent and preferred reading. His school then distinguished by material, holding that `ان وصلت شعرها بشعر آدمي فهو حرام بلا خلاف` [3] — joining with *human* hair is forbidden without disagreement.

Behind it stands the verse `فليغيرن خلق الله`, which al-Qurtubi reports being read in more than one way; al-Nakha'i and al-Tabari took `فليغيرن خلق الله دين الله` [4] — the changing of God's creation meaning the changing of God's religion — rather than a blanket condemnation of altering the body.

That is worth registering. The verse people quote most confidently in these discussions was itself read by early authorities as being about something else.

**The permissive side, and it is a real report.**

'Arfaja b. As'ad had his nose cut off at the battle of al-Kulab. He made himself a nose of silver, and it rotted and stank. `فأمره النبي - صلى الله عليه وسلم - فاتخذ أنفا من ذهب` [5] — so the Prophet ﷺ ordered him, and he took a nose of gold.

Three things are settled by that in one sentence. A prosthesis replacing a lost part is permitted. It is permitted although it changes how he looks. And it is permitted even in gold, which is otherwise forbidden to men — the need overrode the prohibition rather than being squeezed under it.

**So the distinction the jurists are working with.**

Restoring something lost is one thing; adding what was never yours is another. A nose that was cut off, replaced. Hair that a woman never had, joined on — and joined, in the strongest case, from another human being's body.

A hair transplant is unusual because it does neither cleanly. The hair is the person's own, moved from the back of their head to the front. Nothing is added from outside; nothing that belonged to someone else is attached. On the wasl reasoning, the object of the prohibition — attaching another's hair, or a false appearance of hair one does not possess — is arguably absent.

Against that, baldness is not an injury in the way 'Arfaja's wound was, and someone can argue the transplant is enhancement rather than restoration. Which description fits is precisely what a scholar decides, and it may well turn on the case: scarring, burns and alopecia from illness look much more like 'Arfaja; ordinary pattern baldness looks less so.

**What these books do not reach.**

Nothing here addresses surgical transplantation of one's own tissue, which is what the procedure actually is. Nothing addresses medication for hair loss, which is a treatment question and probably an easier one. Nothing addresses non-surgical systems and wigs, which sit much closer to the wasl material and should be asked about separately. Nothing addresses tattooed scalp pigmentation, which raises the tattoo question instead. And nothing addresses cost, risk or a surgeon's competence, which are real considerations and not religious ones.

**What to ask.**

- Is the hair your own, moved, or is anything attached from another person or from synthetic material? That distinction does most of the work in [1] to [3].
- Is this restoring something lost to injury, burn or illness, or is it ordinary age-related loss?
- Would you describe the result honestly if asked?
- Is the procedure safe, and is the expense something you can bear without harm elsewhere?

There is a separate answer here on cosmetic surgery which covers the general framework, and a scholar will place this case inside it. Bring them 'Arfaja's nose — it is the report the permissive reasoning is built on, and any answer that does not engage with it has only looked at half the material.
