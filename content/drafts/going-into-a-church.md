# Can I go inside a church or temple?

## Answer

Yes. And the surprising thing is that the jurists' question was not whether you may *enter* one — it was whether you may **pray** in one, which is a much stronger permission than you were asking for.

**The ruling.**

Ibn Qudama: "**there is no harm in praying in a clean church.** Al-Hasan, 'Umar b. 'Abd al-'Aziz, al-Sha'bi, al-Awza'i and Sa'id b. 'Abd al-'Aziz permitted it, and it is also related from **'Umar** and **Abu Musa**. **And Ibn 'Abbas and Malik disliked churches — on account of the images**" [1].

Note that even the dissenting position is not about the building or the religion. Ibn 'Abbas and Malik object *on account of the images*, which is a rule that would apply equally to a Muslim's living room with portraits on the wall.

Ibn Qudama's own argument: "**and for us: the Prophet ﷺ prayed in the Ka'ba and there were images in it** — and then it falls under his words: *wherever the prayer overtakes you, pray, for it is a place of prayer*" [2].

That last hadith is the whole principle. The earth is a mosque; a building's ownership does not exclude God from it.

He adds the practical accommodation for a floor you are unsure about: "**if the ground is impure and he plasters it with something pure, or spreads something pure over it, the prayer is valid** — with dislike — on the apparent sense of Ahmad's words" [3]. Bring a mat.

**The one genuine caution.**

It is about images, and it is a caution rather than a prohibition. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, on entering a house with images and statues on its walls: "**that is all disliked**, because of the Messenger of Allah's ﷺ words: *the angels do not enter a house in which there are images*. So if a person does that after knowing of the prohibition, **he is a sinner in their view** — but the owner's cloth or house is not thereby made forbidden to him" [4]. And: "**but he ought to keep clear of all of that, and dislike it, and stand apart from it**, because of the prohibition that has come regarding it" [5].

That is a real scruple, held by real scholars, and it is why the Maliki position exists. It is not the same as saying you may not go in.

**So, practically.**

- **Entering is permitted.** A church, a temple, a synagogue — as a building, to see the architecture, to attend a wedding or a funeral, to shelter, to visit.
- **Praying inside is permitted too** on the majority reading, with the Maliki reservation about images. Spread something clean and pray.
- **Do not take part in the worship.** Do not bow, kneel, cross yourself, recite a creed, take communion, or say *amen* to a prayer addressed elsewhere. Sit or stand quietly as courtesy requires. This is the actual line and it is the only one.
- **Do not go on the day of their festival to keep the festival**, which is a separate question and governed by the festival material.
- **Images are a scruple, not a barrier.** If your school is Maliki and you would rather not sit facing a statue, sit at the back. Nobody minds.
- **A place of worship is owed respect while you are in it.** Dress modestly, keep quiet, do what visitors are asked to do. Nothing in the sources permits rudeness to someone else's sacred space, and Ibn Qudama's whole argument rests on the earth being God's regardless of who built the walls.

**One thing worth carrying away.** The dispute preserved here is between scholars who thought a Muslim could pray inside a church and scholars who thought the images made that inadvisable. Neither side imagined a Muslim could not go in. If someone tells you the door is closed to you, they have gone further than Malik did.
