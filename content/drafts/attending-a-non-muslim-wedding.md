# Can I go to a non-Muslim wedding?

## Answer

Yes, generally — with one clear thing to avoid and one clear thing to check.

**Start with what the jurists were actually arguing about, because it tells you the shape of the question.**

For a Muslim's wedding feast, accepting the invitation is not merely polite. Al-Nawawi: "**the Qadi transmitted the scholars' agreement on the obligation of accepting the invitation to a marriage feast.** He said: and they disagreed about other than it — Malik and the majority said accepting it is not obligatory, and the Zahiris said acceptance is obligatory for any invitation, wedding or otherwise, and some of the *salaf* said so too" [1].

Then the qualification that bears on your question: "**and if a protected person invited him, accepting is not obligatory, on the sounder view**" [2]. Ibn Hajar lists the same among the conditions for the obligation: "**and that the inviter be a Muslim, on the sounder view**" [3].

Read that carefully, because it is easy to misread. The dispute is about whether you are **obliged** to go. Nobody in this discussion suggests you are forbidden to. A duty that does not bind you is not a prohibition; it simply returns the decision to you.

**The list of things that excuse you — and it is the same list for everyone.**

Al-Nawawi sets out the excuses that lift the obligation to accept any invitation: "that there be something doubtful in the food; or that it be confined to the rich; or that there be present someone who would be hurt by his attendance, or whose company does not befit him; or that he is invited out of fear of his harm, or greed for his standing, or to assist him in something false. **And that there not be a wrong present — wine, or amusement, or silk furnishings, or images of animals not spread underfoot, or vessels of gold and silver. All of these are excuses for declining**" [4].

Ibn Hajar states the same condition more briefly: "**and that there not be there someone who would be hurt by his attendance, from a wrong or otherwise**" [5].

Nothing on either list is *the host is not a Muslim*. The list is about the food, the fairness, the company, and what is happening in the room — and it applies to a Muslim's wedding exactly as it applies to anyone else's.

**And the rule for a wrong you cannot remove.**

Al-Jassas, on those who mock God's revelations: "**whoever sits with them detesting that state of theirs has not disbelieved** — even though it is not permitted for him to sit with them" [6].

Two things at once, and both matter. Your inward rejection protects you. And you should still leave the moment rather than sit through it.

**So, practically.**

- **Go to the reception.** The meal, the speeches, the family — this is the part the sources have no objection to, and it is the part your friend actually wants you at.
- **The ceremony is the question to think about.** If it is a civil registration or a signing, there is nothing religious in it and no issue arises. If it is a religious service with worship in it — hymns, a creed, a blessing invoking what you do not hold — that is the case the sources are cautious about. Attend as a silent guest if you do, and take part in nothing. Many people go for the reception only and nobody minds.
- **Do not sit at a table where drinking is the occasion.** That is the specific ruling elsewhere in this section, and here it appears again in al-Nawawi's list of excuses.
- **Do not take part in a rite.** No toast to anything, no bowing, no recitation, no *amen*.
- **Do not eat pork or anything containing alcohol.** Tell the host in advance if you can — most caterers can manage it and most hosts are glad to be asked.
- **Bring a present.** This is one of the clearest places in these sources where gift-giving is encouraged, and a wedding is what it was made for.

**One more thing.** The reason the obligation is stated so strongly for a Muslim's wedding is that a marriage is a thing the community is supposed to *witness and announce*. When a friend or a relative who is not Muslim marries, you are not under that obligation — but the impulse behind it is the same one, and there is nothing in these books telling you to withhold it.

ودم وفم وإنما نقص لعلة فهي من الأدميين من المنقوصات لأنها على حرفين ولأنها كلمة ملفوظة بالأدوات ومن ربنا تبارك وتعالى تامة لأنها بغير الأدوات تعالى عن شبه المخلوقين السادسة قوله   تعالى : ( فيكون ) قرئ برفع النون على الإستئناف قال سيبويه : فهو يكون أو فإنه يكون وقال غيره : هو معطوف على يقول فعلى الأول كائنا بعد الأمر وإن كان معدوما فانه بمنزلة الموجود إذا هو عنده معلوم على ما ياتي بيانه وعلى الثاني كائنا مع الأمر وإختاره الطبري وقال : أمره للشيء ب كن لا يتقدم الوجود ولا يتاخر عنه فلا يكون الشيء مأمورا بالوجود إلا وهو موجود بالأمر ولا موجودا إلا وهو مأمور بالوجود على ما ياتي بيانه قال : ونظيره قيام الناس من قبورهم لا يتقدم دعاء الله ولا يتاخر عنه كما قال ثم إذا دعاكم دعوة من الأرض إذا أنتم تخرجون وضعف بن عطية هذا القول وقال : هو خطأ من جهة المعنى لأنه يقتضي أن القول مع التكوين والوجود وتلخيص المعتقد في هذه الآية : أن الله عز وجل لم يزل أمرا للمعدومات بشرط وجودها قادرا مع تأخر المقدورات عالما مع تاخر المعلومات فكل ما في الآية يقتضي الإستقبال فهو بحسب المأمورات إذ المحدثات تجيء بعد أن لم تكن وكل ما يسند إلى الله تعالى من قدرة وعلم فهو قديم ولم يزل والمعنى الذي تقتضيه عبارة كن : هو قديم قائم بالذات وقال أبو الحسن الماوردي فان قيل : ففي أي حال يقول له كن فيكون أفي حال عدمه أم في حال وجوده فإن كان في حال عدمه إستحال أن يأمر إلا مأمورا كما يستحيل أن يكون الأمر إلا من أمر وأن كان في حال وجوده فتلك حال لا يجوز أن يأمر فيها بالوجود والحدوث لأنه موجود حادث قيل عن هذا السؤال أجوبة ثلاثة : أحدها أنه خبر من الله تعالى عن نفوذ أوامره في خلقه الموجود كما أمر في بني إسرائيل أن يكونوا قردة خاسئين ولا يكون هذا واردا في إيجاد المعدومات
