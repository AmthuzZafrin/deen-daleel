# My family reacted badly — what does Islam say about that?

## Answer

**If you are in danger, that comes first.** If the reaction has gone past words —
if you have been thrown out, threatened, had documents or money taken, or are
being confined — this stops being a question about family ties and becomes a
question about your safety. Get practical help before religious advice. Convert
support organisations exist in most countries and have dealt with exactly this;
so do the ordinary emergency services. Nothing below asks you to stay somewhere
unsafe.

For everything short of that, here is what the sources actually require of you —
and it is far less than most people in your position assume.

**The duty to keep ties does not disappear, but read what the duty is.** Qadi
'Iyad, as al-Nawawi transmits him: **there is no disagreement that keeping ties
of kinship is obligatory in general, and severing them a major sin** [1] — and
then, immediately: **but keeping ties has degrees, some higher than others, and
its lowest is leaving off shunning, and connecting by speech, even if only with
a greeting.** [2]

The floor is a greeting. That is the whole of the minimum.

He adds two things that matter enormously when the other side is the one who
closed the door. **This differs according to capacity and need** [3] — the
obligation is measured against what you can actually do. And: **if someone keeps
up part of the connection without reaching its utmost, he is not called one who
severs.** [4]

So you are not cutting off your family by not phoning a person who hangs up. You
are not cutting them off by keeping your distance from a house where you are
attacked. Keeping the door open from your side, at whatever level is possible,
is the obligation. Forcing it open is not.

**And on the things that were said to you.** Al-Qurtubi, listing what is owed
specifically to close kin — spending on them, keeping track of how they are —
ends the list with: **and overlooking their lapses.** [5] What was said in the
first week is, in the tradition's own vocabulary, a lapse to be overlooked. Not
excused. Overlooked, which is a thing you do rather than a thing you feel.

**On the grief itself**, the Qur'an addresses the Prophet in the middle of his
own people rejecting him: **Endure thou patiently. Thine endurance is only by
the help of Allah. Grieve not for them, and be not in distress because of that
which they devise.** [6] The instruction is not that it should not hurt. It is
that the endurance is not something you are expected to generate on your own.

**One verse people will quote at you, so have it straight.** *Say: if your
fathers, and your sons, and your brethren, and your wives, and your tribe...* are
dearer to you than God and His messenger, then wait. [7] That verse is about
which way you decide when the two genuinely pull apart. It is not a licence to
write your family off, and it does not sit comfortably beside the passages above
if read that way. Both are in the same tradition: you do not let the fear of
losing them choose your religion, **and** you keep greeting them.

**What actually tends to happen** is not in these texts and is not a religious
claim: many families that react badly at three weeks are different at three
years. What survives that stretch is usually the small, unglamorous contact —
the message on a birthday, the greeting they do not return — which the sources
above happen to have set as the floor. Keeping the minimum is not defeat. It is
the whole obligation, and it is also the thing most likely to still be there
later.
