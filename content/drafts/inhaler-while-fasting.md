# Does my asthma inhaler break my fast?

## Answer

**Genuinely disputed. The reasoning in the classical books points toward an inhaler
breaking the fast, because it delivers a substance through the mouth into the airway.
Contemporary scholars are split, and many permit it. If you need it, use it.**

Let me be straight about the order of those sentences: the medical point comes first
because it is the one that matters. Asthma is dangerous. Nobody in this tradition
asks you to risk an attack.

**Why the classical reasoning points toward "breaks it".**

Al-Kasani states the Hanafi rule for the natural openings: **what reaches the body
cavity or the brain through the original openings — such as the nose, the ear, and
the rectum — by his taking a nasal drop, or an enema, or dropping something in his
ear, so that it reaches the cavity or the brain: his fast is invalidated** [1].

The mouth is the most direct of those routes. An inhaler puts a measured dose of
medicated aerosol into it, and you draw it in on purpose.

Ibn Qudama's rule points the same way: **he breaks the fast by everything he
introduces into his interior, or into a cavity in his body such as his brain or his
throat, and the like of that which passes to his stomach — if it reaches there by his
choice, and was something he could have guarded against** [2]. *His throat* is named
explicitly.

And al-Sarakhsi's version of the Hanafi test: **the consideration is what arrives,
not the route** [3].

**Why the other side is not weak either.**

Two things in the sources cut the other way.

First, **settling matters, not merely passing.** Al-Kasani: **if it reaches the head
and then comes out, it does not invalidate — as when he takes a nasal drop at night
and it comes out in the daytime, because when it came out it was known that it did
not reach the cavity, or did not settle in it** [4]. Ibn Nujaym draws the principle:
**this indicates that the settling of what enters in the interior is a condition for
the invalidation of the fast** [5]. He gives the vivid case: **if an arrow strikes the
fasting person and comes out the other side, his fast is not invalidated; and if the
arrowhead remains in his interior, his fast is invalidated** [6].

An inhaler's dose is measured in micrograms, is designed to deposit in the bronchi,
and does not reach or settle in the stomach. That is the argument contemporary
scholars who permit it rely on.

Second, **the reason a substance breaks the fast is that it sustains the body.** Ibn
Nujaym: **as for pouring medicine into the mouth, it invalidates his fast, because
there reached the interior of the body something that sustains the body, so it is
eating in meaning — but no expiation is required of him, because of the absence of
actual eating** [7]. An inhaler does not nourish. And note the second half — even
where medicine does break the fast, it is a make-up day only, never the heavy
expiation.

**So, practically.**

- **If you have asthma or COPD, use your inhaler whenever you need it.** Do not wait,
  do not ration, do not test whether you can get to Maghrib. An attack is a real
  danger; a make-up day is not.
- **If your inhaler is preventive** and the timing is flexible, take doses at suhoor
  and after iftar. That avoids the question entirely and is what most scholars who
  hold it breaks the fast would advise.
- **If you needed it during the day**, follow your school. If your scholars hold it
  breaks the fast, make up that day. If they permit it, nothing is owed. Either way
  you did the right thing by using it.
- **If you need it constantly**, you may fall under chronic illness rather than the
  ordinary case — see the answers on who must fast and on fidya.

**On the difference from a nebuliser or oxygen**: oxygen is not a substance in the
sense the jurists meant, and is not treated as breaking the fast. A nebuliser follows
the same reasoning as an inhaler.

This is a question where a scholar who knows your condition and your school is worth
more than a general answer — but not more than your inhaler.
