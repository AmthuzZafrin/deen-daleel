# What is the grave like before Judgement Day?

## Answer

**Short answer: the barzakh is the interval between dying and the Resurrection —
a barrier, in the Qur'an's own word, that the dead cannot come back through.**
It is not a neutral waiting room: the classical sources describe questioning
there, and either ease or distress depending on the answer.

**The word comes from the Qur'an.** 23:100, describing someone who asks to be
sent back to put things right: **"and behind them is a barrier until the day when
they are raised"** [1]. *Barzakh* is that barrier. The verse's force is that the
request is refused — the interval is one-way.

**What the sources say happens there.** Ibn Taymiyya, in a treatise on the
punishment of the grave, cites the report of the two graves: **"أنهما ليعذبان
وما يعذبان في كبير اما أحدهما فكان يمشى بالنميمة وأما الآخر فكان لا يستتر من
بوله"** — *these two are being punished, and not for something great: one of them
used to go about with malicious gossip, and the other did not shield himself from
his urine* [2]. Note the offences: carrying tales, and carelessness about purity.
Not spectacular crimes.

He also cites Zayd b. Thabit's report: **"إن هذه الأمة تبتلى في قبورها فلولا أن
لا تدافنوا لدعوت الله أن يسمعكم من عذاب القبر الذي أسمع منه"** — *this community
is tested in its graves; were it not that you would cease burying one another, I
would ask Allah to let you hear of the punishment of the grave what I hear* [3].
And the du'a taught for the end of the prayer: **"أعوذ بالله من أربع من عذاب
جهنم ومن عذاب القبر ومن فتنة المحيا والممات ومن فتنة المسيح الدجال"** — *I seek
refuge in Allah from four: the punishment of Hell, the punishment of the grave,
the trial of life and death, and the trial of the Dajjal* [4]. That the refuge is
built into the daily prayer tells you how the tradition ranks it.

**The questioning.** Ibn Hajar gives the angels' question: **"قول الملكين ما تقول
في هذا الرجل محمد"** — *the two angels' saying: what do you say about this man,
Muhammad?* [5]. And he draws a striking inference from the fate of the one who
could not answer: **"و فيه ذم التقليد في الاعتقادات لمعاقبة من قال كنت أسمع
الناس يقولون شيئا فقلته"** — *in it is a censure of blind imitation in matters of
belief, since the one punished was he who said: I used to hear people saying
something, so I said it* [6].

That is worth pausing on. The man in the report is not punished for holding the
wrong belief but for holding it *only because others did*. On Ibn Hajar's
reading, the barzakh asks whether the faith was actually yours.

**How to picture it, and how not to.** Al-Nawawi states the position:
**"والمقصود أن مذهب أهل السنة اثبات عذاب القبر"** — *the position of Ahl
al-Sunna is to affirm the punishment of the grave* [7], and addresses the obvious
objection — that we see the body lying undisturbed — with an analogy:
**"فالجواب أن ذلك غير ممتنع بل له نظر في العادة وهو النائم فإنه يجد لذة وآلاما
لا نحس نحن شيئا منها"** — *it is not impossible; it has a parallel in ordinary
experience, namely the sleeper, who experiences pleasure and pain of which we
perceive nothing* [8].

Ibn Hajar makes the same point about what kind of life is restored:
**"ليست الحياة المستقرة المعهودة في الدنيا التي تقوم فيها الروح بالبدن"** — *it
is not the settled life familiar in this world, in which the soul sustains the
body* [9]. So the sources are not claiming a person is conscious in a coffin in
the ordinary sense; they are describing a different mode of experience, and
saying so explicitly.

**And it ends.** Al-Nawawi quotes the words said to the believer about his place:
**"مقعدك حتى يبعثك الله"** — *your place, until Allah raises you* [10]. Ibn
Taymiyya: **"ثم اذا كان يوم القيامة الكبرى اعيدت الارواح إلى اجسادها وقاموا من
قبورهم لرب العالمين"** — *then, when the Great Day of Resurrection comes, the
souls are returned to their bodies and they rise from their graves for the Lord
of the Worlds* [11]. The barzakh is an interval, not a destination.

**A practical note.** The two offences named at [2] are ordinary and fixable —
gossip, and carelessness about cleanliness after relieving oneself. The
tradition's own emphasis, on that report, falls on small habitual things rather
than dramatic sins. And the refuge-seeking at [4] is something to actually say;
it belongs at the end of the prayer.

**On the language above:** "barzakh" is a barrier or interval; "'adhab al-qabr"
is the punishment of the grave; "fitnat al-qabr" is the trial or questioning
there; "namima" is carrying tales between people.

**A note on scope.** Whether the punishment falls on the body, the soul, or both,
and exactly how, was disputed — al-Nawawi and Ibn Taymiyya both survey the
disagreement in the passages quoted here [7][8]. What is affirmed in common is
that it is real and that its manner is not ours to observe. If a specific
question is troubling you — about someone who died, or about a body that was
never buried — al-Nawawi addresses that directly at [8], and an imam can take it
further.
