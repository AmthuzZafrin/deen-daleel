# Is there anything wrong with high heels?

## Answer

No text names high heels, and no text can — the shoe is modern. But the sources
do discuss women's footwear as a category, and they do supply a principle about
sound and attention that maps onto heels more exactly than most modern analogies
do. Both are worth having, and so is the boundary between them.

**The principle, from the end of the verse of the khimar.** Ibn Kathir explains
the clause *and let them not stamp their feet so as to reveal what they hide of
their adornment*: "in the Jahiliyya, when a woman walked in the road with a
silent anklet on her leg — one whose sound was not heard — she would strike the
ground with her foot so that the men would know its ringing; and Allah forbade
believing women the like of that" [1].

Then he generalises it, and the generalisation is the operative part: "and
likewise, if something of her adornment is concealed and she makes a movement in
order to display what is hidden, it enters into this prohibition" [2].

That is a rule about *deliberately drawing notice through movement*. A shoe that
announces each step, worn so that it is heard, sits squarely inside what Ibn
Kathir describes. A shoe you happen to own that makes a noise on a hard floor
does not, because the clause he is expanding is about a movement made *in order
to* display.

**Footwear itself was discussed, and the objection raised was not the shoe.**
Al-Nawawi records the Shafi'i debate about women wearing sandals of gold and
silver: "as for her wearing sandals of silver and gold, there are two positions.
The first, which the author of *al-Hawi* settled on, is prohibition, because of
the evident extravagance and vanity in it; and the sounder in the view of
al-Rafi'i and others is permissibility, like the rest of what is worn" [3]. And
the general condition he attaches to all women's adornment: "every ornament
permitted to women is only permitted when there is no evident extravagance in
it" [4].

So the classical objection to a conspicuous shoe is *saraf* and *khuyala'* —
extravagance and vanity — and even that objection lost, on the sounder view.

**Vanity is judged by intent, not by the object.** The clearest illustration in
the sources is Abu Bakr's exchange with the Prophet ﷺ. "Whoever drags his
garment out of vanity, Allah will not look at him on the Day of Resurrection.
Abu Bakr said: one of my two sides slips down unless I keep attending to it. The
Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: you are not one who does that out of vanity" [5]. Ibn
Taymiyya draws the rule out of it: "for this reason lengthening the garment
*with that intention* is forbidden" [6]. Al-Shafi'i applies the same grading to
another garment: "letting the garment hang is not permitted, in the prayer or
outside it, for vanity — and for other than it, it is a light matter" [7]. Same
act, two rulings, and the intention decides.

**And where custom differs, the ruling follows custom.** Al-Nawawi on the
crown: "the companions said, if the custom of women is to wear it, it is
permitted; otherwise it is forbidden, because it is the emblem of the great ones
of the Romans. He said: the meaning of this seems to be that it differs by the
custom of the people of each region — so where the custom of women is to wear
it, it is permitted, and where it is not, it is forbidden, out of caution
against imitating men" [8]. Al-Nawawi himself thought even that was too
restrictive: "the chosen view, indeed the correct one, is permissibility without
hesitation, on account of the generality of the hadith and its falling under the
name of jewellery" [9].

**So, practically.** Heels as ordinary footwear, worn the way everyone else in
your society wears shoes: nothing in these sources reaches them, and the
category they would fall under — women's adornment — was ruled permissible on
the sounder view even in its extravagant forms. Heels worn to be heard, or worn
so as to change how you carry yourself in front of men, fall under Ibn Kathir's
generalisation, and that is a real prohibition with a clear text behind it.

The question the sources put to you is not what is on your feet. It is whether
you are making a movement in order to be noticed. Only you can answer that, and
the sources decline to answer it for you.

One thing worth saying plainly, though it has no daleel behind it: shoes that
injure you are a separate matter from shoes that draw attention, and the fiqh
here speaks to the second, not the first.
