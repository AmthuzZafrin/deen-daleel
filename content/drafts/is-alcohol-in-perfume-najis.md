# Is alcohol-based perfume impure?

## Answer

**On the reasoning the books actually give, no — and you may pray wearing it.**
The impurity ruling attaches to *khamr*, an intoxicating drink, and the alcohol in
perfume is neither drunk nor intoxicating as used. But the corpus predates
industrial and denatured alcohol, so what follows is reasoning from the sources'
own principles rather than a verdict any of them issued.

**What the sources rule on.** Ibn Taymiyya sets the definition as wide as it goes:
**every intoxicating drink is khamr, and it is forbidden, by the widely
transmitted Sunna of the Messenger of Allah, by the agreement of the Companions**
[1] — and the material it is made from is irrelevant: **everything in which there
is this exhilarating strength is khamr, from whatever substance it is, from grains
or fruits or otherwise** [2]. On impurity he reports the majority: **this
intoxicant obliges the hadd punishment upon its drinker, and it is impure
according to the imams** [3].

So khamr is najis on the majority position. The question is whether perfume
alcohol is khamr.

**The principle that says it is not.** Ibn Taymiyya, in the same passage, gives
the test — and it is a test of *effect*, not of chemistry: **but if it is cooked
until two thirds of it go and a third remains, it no longer remains an
intoxicant.** [4]

That is decisive for how the category works. A substance derived from khamr, once
it can no longer intoxicate, is no longer khamr. The name and the ruling travel
with the property, not with the origin.

The same logic appears in the most familiar case of all. Al-Nawawi cites it while
arguing about what can remove impurity: **and because khamr, when it turns to
vinegar, becomes pure, and the vessel becomes pure** [5]. Wine is the paradigm
najis substance; vinegar made from it is pure and lawful to eat. Nobody treats the
vinegar as tainted by what it used to be.

**Applying it.** Perfume alcohol — ethanol, usually denatured — is not a drink, is
not sold as one, and would poison rather than intoxicate someone who tried. It
functions as a solvent that evaporates. On the reasoning above, it does not fall
under the ruling for khamr, and a shirt sprayed with perfume is not a shirt
carrying najasa.

**And even on the strictest view, there is a second line of defence.** The
quantity argument: al-Jassas, discussing najasa mixed into a larger body,
represents the position that **when the water is dominant so that it does not
appear in it, the ruling belongs to the water** — because **those parts are
submerged and consumed** [6]. He himself argues against this for water
specifically, but the principle of *istihlak* — a trace absorbed into a larger
whole losing its own ruling — is used across the schools, and the residue left on
skin after a spray of perfume evaporates is about as clear a case of it as exists.

**Where the honest limits are**

- **This is applied reasoning, not a transmitted ruling.** No premodern text
  addresses perfume alcohol, because the substance as we know it did not exist.
  What is transmitted is the definition of khamr, the vinegar case, and the
  two-thirds rule; the application is inference from those.
- **Contemporary scholars differ**, and some hold all ethanol impure regardless of
  source or use. That is a real position, held by people with reasons.
- **Drinking it remains forbidden without qualification**, by every reckoning, and
  nothing above touches that.

**Practically**

| | |
|---|---|
| Alcohol-based perfume, aftershave, deodorant | pray in it; no need to wash |
| Hand sanitiser | same reasoning; it evaporates entirely |
| Medicines containing alcohol | different question, with its own answer here |
| Drinking anything intoxicating | forbidden, no exceptions in the above |

**If you would rather avoid it**, alcohol-free attar is widely sold and there is a
strong sunna of wearing perfume in the first place. Choosing it is a reasonable
precaution. But if you have prayed for years in an ordinary aftershave, there is
nothing here that asks you to repeat anything.
