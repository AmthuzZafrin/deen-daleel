# What is ruqyah and how do I do it?

## Answer

*Ruqya* is healing by recitation: you say words over yourself or over someone who is ill — Qur'an, or Allah's names, or a supplication — asking Allah for cure. It is not a technique, it has no fixed script, and it requires no specialist. The Prophet ﷺ did it for himself, and 'A'isha did it for him.

**The rule that governs everything else.**

'Awf b. Malik said: "We used to perform ruqya in the Jahiliyya, so we said, Messenger of Allah, how do you see that? He said: Present your ruqyas to me. There is no harm in ruqyas so long as there is no shirk in them" [1]. That is the whole standard, and the Prophet ﷺ set it by asking to *hear* the words rather than banning the practice.

Ibn Hajar sets out what the scholars extracted from it — a consensus with three conditions: "the scholars have agreed on the permissibility of ruqyas when three conditions come together: that it be by the speech of Allah, or by His names and attributes; and in the Arabic tongue, or in something else whose meaning is known; and that one believe the ruqya does not have effect by itself, but by the essence of Allah" [2].

Read those in reverse and you have the three ways ruqya goes wrong: believing the words themselves do the work, reciting sounds you do not understand, and reciting something that is not from Allah's speech at all. Ibn Hajar is explicit about the middle one: "whatever of ruqyas leads to shirk is forbidden, and what has no intelligible meaning is not safe from leading to shirk, so it is forbidden out of caution" [3]. If you cannot say what the words mean, do not say them.

**What to recite.**

Anything in the Qur'an. The Prophet ﷺ favoured al-Falaq and al-Nas, and al-Nawawi explains why: "he only performed ruqya with the *mu'awwidhat* because they gather together the seeking of refuge from all disliked things, in summary and in detail" [4]. That is a reason of comprehensiveness, not exclusivity. Al-Tabari put the inference plainly: "if ruqya with the two *mu'awwidhat* is permitted — and they are two suras of the Qur'an — then ruqya with the rest of the Qur'an is like them in permissibility, since all of it is Qur'an" [5].

Al-Fatiha is established by the hadith of Abu Sa'id, and Ibn Hajar generalises from it: "whatever contains seeking refuge with Allah alone, or seeking help from Him alone, or what conveys that meaning — performing ruqya with it is legislated" [6]. So the field is wide: Ayat al-Kursi, the closing verses of al-Baqara, the opening of al-Saffat, or a du'a in your own language whose meaning you know.

**How the Prophet ﷺ actually did it.**

'A'isha said: "When any of his family fell ill, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ would blow upon him with the *mu'awwidhat*. When he fell into the illness in which he died, I began to blow upon him and to wipe him with his own hand, because it was greater in blessing than my hand" [7].

So the form is: recite, blow lightly, wipe with the hand. On the blowing — *nafth* — al-Nawawi: "*nafth* is a light blowing without saliva. In it is the recommendedness of blowing in ruqya, and they have agreed on its permissibility; the majority of the Companions, the Successors and those after them held it recommended" [8].

Some early scholars disliked the blowing, worried by "the women who blow on knots" in Surat al-Falaq. Al-Qurtubi answers the worry directly: "if blowing *on knots* is blameworthy, it does not follow that blowing without knots is blameworthy — and blowing on knots was intended for sorcery that harms souls, whereas this blowing is for setting bodies right; so what benefits is not measured against what harms" [9]. And he closes the dispute the right way: "when they differ, the judge between them is the Sunna — 'A'isha reported that the Prophet ﷺ used to blow in ruqya" [10].

**What is not ruqya.**

The line between ruqya and sorcery is real, and it is drawn at the point where you stop asking Allah and start operating an apparatus. Malik, per al-Qadi 'Iyad, "used to dislike ruqya with iron, with salt, by one who ties knots, and by one who writes the seal of Sulayman — and knot-tying was more severely disliked in his view, because of the resemblance to sorcery in it" [11]. The hadith behind that: "whoever ties a knot and then blows on it has performed sorcery, and whoever performs sorcery has committed shirk; and whoever hangs a thing upon himself is entrusted to it" [12].

Iron, salt, knots, seals, diagrams, numbered squares — these are the marks of a practitioner selling a system, not a Muslim reciting Qur'an. That is the practical test if you are deciding whether to visit someone: ask what he will recite, and whether you will understand it.

**Is it a lapse in tawakkul?**

No. The Companions asked exactly this. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr transmits: "'Do you think the ruqyas we seek, the protective measures we take, and the medicines we treat ourselves with — do they repel anything of the decree, or avail anything against the decree?' The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: They are *from* the decree" [13]. The remedy is not an escape from qadar; it is part of it. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr adds: "the soul is put at ease by treatment and finds comfort in the remedy, and perhaps it will coincide with a decree" [14].

There is a rank above it — the seventy thousand who "do not seek ruqya, and do not take omens, and upon their Lord they rely." Ibn 'Abd al-Barr grants the rank and refuses the inference: that station is "higher and more exalted — and there is no blame upon one who seeks ruqya and treats himself" [15]. Something can be better without the alternative being wrong.

**Scope, payment, and the practitioner.**

Some reports restrict ruqya: "there is no ruqya except from the evil eye, or a venomous sting, or blood that will not stop" [16]. This does not narrow the practice. Ibn Hajar: "the meaning of the restriction is that these two are the root of everything requiring ruqya — so to the eye is joined the permissibility of ruqya for one afflicted with disorder or possession and the like, since they share in arising from satanic conditions, whether from a human or a jinn" [17]. Al-Nawawi says the same of the parallel report: "its meaning is not to restrict its permissibility to these three; rather, he was asked about these three and permitted them, and had he been asked about others he would have permitted them — and he did permit others" [18].

Payment is permitted. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr: "in the permissibility of ruqyas is the permissibility of taking compensation for it, because everything benefited from, it is permissible to take a substitute for. And whoever does it seeking reward and takes nothing for it — his is the greater merit" [19]. So a paid raqi is not by that fact illegitimate. But note the direction of the merit, and be wary of anyone whose fee scales with the number of sessions he diagnoses you as needing.

Malik differed with himself over whether a Jew or Christian may perform ruqya for a Muslim; al-Shafi'i permitted it [20].

**One contested practice.** Writing verses and washing them off to drink — *nushra*. Al-Qurtubi reports it as a genuine disagreement: "Sa'id b. al-Musayyab permitted it — he was asked about a man kept from his wife, may he be released and treated by nushra? He said: There is no harm in it; whatever benefits has not been forbidden" [21]; but "al-Hasan and Ibrahim al-Nakha'i forbade it. Al-Nakha'i said: I fear that an affliction will strike him" [22]. 'A'isha's own practice was the milder version: "she used to recite the two *mu'awwidhat* into a vessel and then order that it be poured over the sick person" [23]. Reciting *onto* water has better standing than writing and dissolving; both are disputed, neither is where you should start.

**In short.** Sit with the person or with yourself, recite al-Fatiha, Ayat al-Kursi, al-Ikhlas, al-Falaq and al-Nas, blow lightly, wipe with your hand, and ask Allah plainly in whatever language you have. Repeat it. That is all of it, and it requires no one's permission and no one's fee. See a doctor as well — treatment is also from the decree.
