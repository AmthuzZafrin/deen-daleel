# Is a wife obliged to cook and clean?

## Answer

The schools genuinely differ, and the disagreement is not the one people expect. The majority position is
that she is **not** legally obliged to.

### The majority: not required

Al-Nawawi's *Majmu'* states it plainly: **"it is not obligatory upon the woman to serve the man or the
house, because what is contracted upon is intimacy"** [1]. The reasoning is contractual — the marriage
contract specifies certain claims, and domestic labour is not among them.

Ahmad is recorded to the same effect: **"our companions and others said: the woman is not obliged to
serve her husband in kneading, baking, grinding, cooking and the like"** [2].

But the same passage immediately adds: **"except that her serving him is a commendable thing"** [3] — not
owed, but good. Al-Saffarini: **"the better course for her is to do what custom has established her as
undertaking"** [4].

### The minority: required

Not a fringe view. Abu Thawr held the strongest form: **"the wife must serve the husband in
everything"** [5]. Ibn Taymiyya took a middle course: **"Ibn Taymiyya made obligatory what is customary
from someone like her for someone like him"** [6] — the standard being local custom, not a fixed list.

The Malikis side with obligation. Al-Qurtubi: **"she must lay out the bedding, cook the pot and sweep the
house, according to her condition and the custom of women like her"** [7].

### The thing both sides agree on

Everyone builds the answer out of custom, and al-Qurtubi says so explicitly: **"this is a matter revolving
around custom, which is one of the foundations of the Law"** [8]. He then describes what that meant in
his own time, and the description is unmistakably about class and circumstance rather than principle:
**"the women of the bedouin and desert-dwellers serve their husbands in fetching water and tending the
animals; among the women of the towns, the man of modest means serves his wife in what is light and
helps her"** [9].

Which means the Maliki answer and the Shafi'i answer converge in practice, and both turn on what is
normal for people like you.

### The husband's side of it

The part of the material least often quoted. Al-Qurtubi: **"the man serves his wife in what is light of
the service, and helps her"** [10] — and he cites the Prophet's own household. 'A'isha reported that
**"he ﷺ used to be in the service of his family, and when he heard the call to prayer he went out"** [11].
Asked what he did at home, she said: **"he was a human being like other human beings — he would clean his
garment, milk his sheep, and serve himself"** [12]. Among his manners: **"he would mend the sandal, sweep
the house, and sew the garment"** [13].

That is the Prophet ﷺ, in a society where domestic labour was entirely a woman's sphere by custom.

### Settle it in advance

The classical books offer a mechanism, and it is worth using. Al-Qurtubi: **"if the matter is unclear, the
wife may stipulate that upon him"** [14] — a condition written into the marriage contract, which the
schools uphold.

The Prophet ﷺ also treated a woman's domestic capability as a legitimate thing to marry for. Jabir
explained marrying a widow rather than a young woman because his sisters were orphaned: **"but a woman who
would comb their hair and look after them" — and he said: you have done right"** [15]. It was neither
assumed nor condemned; it was a reason he stated and had accepted.

### In practice

If she does the housework, that is a favour she is giving, not a debt she is paying — on the majority
view, and the correct response to a favour is gratitude, not expectation. If she does not, no school
permits treating it as *nushuz* on its own.

The practical answer for most couples is [8] plus [14]: work out what is normal and fair for your
circumstances, agree it early, and write down anything either of you is unsure about. And whatever is
agreed, [10] through [13] stand: the sunna is a husband who works in his own home.
