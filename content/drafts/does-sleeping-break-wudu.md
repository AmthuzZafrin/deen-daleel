# Does falling asleep break my wudu?

## Answer

Nodding off sitting up does not. Lying down and going properly to sleep does, on
most views. The reasoning behind that is worth having, because it explains every
version of the ruling at once.

**Sleep is not itself an impurity.** Ibn Taymiyya is explicit: **light sleep by
someone settled on his seat does not break wudu, according to the vast majority
of scholars, from the four imams and others — because sleep in their view is not
an impurity in itself, but a likely occasion of one.** [1] [2]

The image the tradition uses is a waterskin: **the eye is the drawstring of the
seat, so when the eyes sleep the drawstring comes loose.** [3] Sleep does not
make you impure. It makes you stop noticing.

Two pieces of evidence follow from that reading. The Prophet slept, sometimes
audibly, and prayed without renewing wudu — which Ibn Taymiyya explains by his
heart not sleeping, so that anything happening he would have known. And, more
usefully for you: **the Prophet used to delay 'Isha until the Companions' heads
were nodding, and then they prayed without performing wudu.** [4] Nodding off
waiting for the prayer was normal and did not send anyone back to the taps.

**The schools then split on where to draw the line, and al-Nawawi records eight
positions.** The three you are most likely to be following:

- **Much sleep breaks it in any posture; a little does not in any posture** — the
  position of al-Zuhri, Rabi'a, al-Awza'i, Malik, and one report from Ahmad. [5]
- **Sleep in a praying posture — bowing, prostrating, standing, sitting — does
  not break it, whether in prayer or not; sleeping lying down or on your back
  does** — Abu Hanifa and Dawud. [6]
- **Sitting with your seat settled on the ground does not break it; anything
  else does** — the Shafi'i position.

Every one of them gets you to the same place for the ordinary case: upright and
briefly, fine; horizontal and properly asleep, renew it.

**Two practical points that resolve most real situations.**

*Drowsiness is not sleep.* Al-Nawawi gives the test: **the mark of sleep is that
it overcomes the mind and the sense of sight and the other senses drop away;
drowsiness does not overcome the mind — the senses merely slacken without
falling away.** [7] If you could still hear the room, you were drowsy.

*And if you cannot tell which it was:* **if he doubts whether he slept or was
merely drowsy, no wudu is due on him, though it is recommended that he perform
it.** [8] Doubt does not undo a wudu you know you had. That principle runs
through this whole chapter and there is a separate answer on it here.
