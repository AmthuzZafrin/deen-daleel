# Which school of thought should I follow?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

> Reviewer note: May explain what the schools are; must not pick one.

## Answer

**This site will not pick a school for you.** Not because the question is
improper — it is a perfectly good question — but because the answer turns
entirely on facts about your life that no website has: where you live, who is
available to teach you, which school your local scholars and mosque actually
work in, and what language you can learn in. What follows is what the schools
are, why there are four, and what the sources say the choice does and does not
depend on.

**First, whether you need to follow anyone at all.** Ibn Taymiyya states the
mainstream settlement: **"والذى عليه جماهير الأمة أن الاجتهاد جائز في الجملة
والتقليد جائز في الجملة لا يوجبون الاجتهاد على كل أحد ويحرمون التقليد ولا
يوجبون التقليد على كل أحد ويحرمون الاجتهاد وأن الاجتهاد جائز للقادر على
الاجتهاد والتقليد جائز للعاجز عن الاجتهاد"** — *what the great majority of the
community hold is that ijtihad is permissible in general and taqlid is
permissible in general; they neither oblige ijtihad on everyone and forbid
taqlid, nor oblige taqlid on everyone and forbid ijtihad. Ijtihad is permitted
to one capable of it, and taqlid is permitted to one incapable of it* [1].

The category you fall into is set by capacity, not by preference. He rejects
imposing independent reasoning on everyone for exactly that reason: **"وهذا ضعيف
لأنه لو كان طلب علمها واجبا على الأعيان فانما يجب مع القدرة والقدرة على معرفتها
من الأدلة المفصلة تتعذر أو تتعسر على أكثر العامة"** — *this is weak, because if
seeking that knowledge were an individual obligation, it would only be obligatory
alongside capacity — and the capacity to know these matters from the detailed
evidences is impossible or very difficult for most non-specialists* [2].

**The reasoning behind allowing taqlid at all.** The Hanbali manual *Sifat
al-Fatwa* grounds it in the verse "Ask the people of remembrance if you do not
know," and in the report of a wounded man who was told to perform a full washing,
did so, and died — and the Prophet's ﷺ response: **"ألا سألوا إذا لم يعلموا إنما
شفاء العي السؤال"** — *why did they not ask, when they did not know? The cure for
ignorance is only asking* [3]. And the practical argument: **"إذ لو منع كل الناس
من التقليد وكلفوا معرفة الأحكام بدليل تعين فرض العلم على الكافة وتعطلت المعايش
وفسد النظام والجهاد وكثير من أمر الدين والدنيا"** — *for if all people were
barred from taqlid and burdened with knowing the rulings by evidence, the
obligation of learning would fall on everyone, livelihoods would cease, and order
would break down, along with much of the affairs of religion and the world* [4].

The same work insists on the reverse: **"ولو جاز للكل التقليد بطل الاجتهاد وسقط
فرض التعلم والتعليم واندرس العلم"** — *and if taqlid were permitted to everyone,
ijtihad would be void, the obligation of learning and teaching would fall away,
and knowledge would be effaced* [5]. Some must go deep so that the rest can ask.
That is the structure.

**Why these four schools.** Ibn Taymiyya transmits an account of the criteria by
which those imams became the ones followed: **"لأنهم هم المقتدى بهم والمرجوع
شرقا وغربا إلى مذاهبهم ولأنهم أجمع لشرائط القدوة والإمامة من غيرهم وأكثر لتحصيل
أسبابها وأدواتها من جودة الحفظ والبصيرة والفطنة والمعرفة بالكتاب والسنة والإجماع
والسند والرجال والأحوال ولغات العرب ومواضعها والتاريخ والناسخ والمنسوخ والمنقول
والمعقول والصحيح والمدخول في الصدق والصلابة وظهور الأمانة والديانة ممن سواهم"** —
*because they are the ones taken as models, whose schools are referred to east and
west; and because they combine the conditions of exemplarity and leadership more
than others, and acquired more of its causes and instruments: excellence of
memory, insight, acuity, knowledge of the Book, the sunna, consensus, chains of
transmission, the narrators and their circumstances, the languages of the Arabs
and their usages, history, the abrogating and abrogated, the transmitted and the
reasoned, the sound and the defective — in truthfulness, firmness, and manifest
trustworthiness and religion, beyond others* [6]. And where one fell short in
some respect: **"وإن قصر واحد منهم في سبب منها جبر تقصيره قرب عصره من الصحابة
والتابعين لهم بإحسان"** — *if one of them fell short in one of those causes, his
shortfall was made up by the nearness of his era to the Companions and the
Successors who followed them well* [7].

Other schools existed and were followed. The account explains what happened to
them: **"ثم اندرجت مذاهبهم الآخرة تحت مذاهب الأئمة المعتبرة"** — *then their
later schools were absorbed under the schools of the recognised imams* [8]. Four
survived as living transmission systems; that is a historical fact about
continuity, not a claim that the others were wrong.

**And whether you must attach to one particular imam is itself disputed.** Ibn
Taymiyya reports it as an open question: **"ثم هل يجب على كل واحد اتباع شخص معين
من الأئمة يقلده في عزائمه ورخصه على وجهين"** — *then, whether it is obligatory on
each person to follow one specific imam, following him in his strict positions
and his concessions, is a matter of two positions* [9]. If the scholars did not
settle whether you must commit to one, a website is in no position to tell you
which one.

*(One note of disclosure: the passage cited above for [6]–[8] continues into a
sharp polemic condemning people who follow one imam in law while holding a
different school's creed. That material is not cited here. It is a
sixth-century argument about creed, not about how a Muslim today should choose a
school of fiqh, and reproducing it would misrepresent both the question and a
large portion of the Sunni tradition.)*

---

**Why the choice is yours and not this site's.**

The sources above give criteria for **who is qualified to be followed**. They do
not rank the four schools against each other, because the four are all held to
meet those criteria. What determines your answer is therefore not a scholarly
verdict but your own situation:

- **Which school do the scholars you can actually reach work in?** Following a
  school with no one nearby who can teach it to you leaves you with books and no
  one to ask — which is exactly the position the verse tells you not to be in.
- **Which school does your local mosque and community follow?** Practising
  differently from everyone around you is possible, but it is a real cost and
  should be chosen knowingly rather than by accident.
- **What can you learn in?** A school with good teaching material in a language
  you read is worth more to you than a school with better material in one you do
  not.
- **What did your family or the community you converted through practise?** Not
  binding — but it is usually the path with the most support attached.

Notice that none of those are questions about which imam was more learned. They
are questions about your life, and you are the only one holding the answers.

**What to do.** Ask someone locally — an imam, a teacher, a scholar in your city
— and tell them your circumstances rather than asking them which school is best.
That is a question they can answer for you and this site cannot. If you find no
one, the more general answer is set out under *Do I have to follow a madhhab at
all?* — the short version being that acting on a ruling you obtain is obligatory,
while binding yourself to one school is not.
