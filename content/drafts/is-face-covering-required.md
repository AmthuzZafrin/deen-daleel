# Do I have to cover my face?

## Answer

No — not as an obligation, on the position most of the scholars held. But the
sources also show that covering the face was a normal and respected practice
among the Companions' households, so a woman who chooses it is not doing
something the tradition finds strange. Both halves of that are worth seeing,
because this is a real disagreement and neither side is a modern invention.

**The strongest single argument is the pilgrimage, and it is decisive on the
narrow point.** Ibn Qudama: "the summary of it is that a woman is forbidden to
cover her face in her ihram, just as a man is forbidden to cover his head. We
know of no disagreement in this" [1]. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr reports the same as
consensus, with the detail: "they were agreed that her ihram is in her face and
not her head, and that she covers her head and covers her hair while in ihram"
[2]. Ibn al-Mundhir adds: "dislike of the burqu' is established from Sa'd, Ibn
'Umar, Ibn 'Abbas and 'A'isha, and we know of no one who differed on it" [3].

The reasoning matters more than the ruling. Ibn Qudama works through what
happens when the two requirements collide — the head must be covered, the face
must not, and you cannot cover the whole head without catching part of the face.
His resolution: "in that case covering the whole head takes precedence, because
it is stronger — since *it* is 'awra, its prohibition not being confined to the
state of ihram, whereas uncovering the face is otherwise" [4]. He is saying
plainly that the head is 'awra and the face is not. You cannot be commanded to
uncover something that is 'awra, and in ihram a woman is commanded to uncover
her face. That is the argument, and it is why the majority position — "all of
her is 'awra except the face and the hands; this is what most of the people of
knowledge hold" [5] — is where it is.

**And yet the same passages show women covering their faces.** Not despite the
rule, but through a recognised workaround. "As for when she needs to cover her
face because of men passing close by her, she draws the garment down from over
her head onto her face" [6] — and Ibn Qudama says he knows of no disagreement
about *that* either. The basis is 'A'isha: "riders used to pass by us while we
were in ihram with the Messenger of Allah ﷺ; when they drew level with us, one
of us would let her jilbab down from her head over her face, and when they had
passed we uncovered it" [7]. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr records it as agreed: "they were
agreed that she may let the garment down over her face from above her head, a
light letting-down, screening herself with it from men looking at her" [8], and
Ibn Hajar quotes Ibn al-Mundhir to the same effect [9].

Note what is actually forbidden in ihram, then: "the woman is only prevented
from the burqu' and the niqab and their like — what is *made* for covering the
face" [10]. The tailored veil, not the covering. And the justification Ibn
Qudama gives for permitting the sadl is striking: a woman has a need to cover
her face, "so covering it was not made forbidden to her absolutely, as the
'awra is" [11]. The need is treated as real and legitimate, in the middle of the
one rite where face-covering is prohibited.

There is also a straightforward report of women simply covering: Malik, from
Fatima bint al-Mundhir: "we used to cover our faces while in ihram, with Asma'
bint Abi Bakr al-Siddiq" [12] — her own grandmother. Both Ibn Qudama and Ibn
'Abd al-Barr suggest this may have been the sadl rather than a tied veil; that
is an interpretation, and they say so.

**The textual basis for the other view.** On the verse of the jalabib (33:59),
Ibn al-'Arabi records two readings side by side: "it is said its meaning is that
she covers her head with it above her khimar, and it is said that she covers her
face with it so that nothing shows of her but her left eye" [13]. The second
reading is a genuine part of the exegetical record, not a later addition. A
woman who veils is following it.

**One caution about a hadith often used here.** Jabir's report — that a woman
comes forward in the form of a shaytan — is on this file, and al-Nawawi draws
*two* conclusions from it, not one: "it is deduced from this that she should not
go out among men except for necessity, and that a man should lower his gaze from
her clothing and turn away from her entirely" [14]. The obligation runs both
ways, and the Prophet's ﷺ own response in the narration was to go to his own
wife — the burden he placed was on the man's response, not on the woman's face.

**So where does that leave you?** Not obligatory, on the majority reading, and
you are not falling short if you do not veil. Legitimate and well-founded if you
choose to, and you are following a real reading of a real verse and the practice
of women in the Prophet's ﷺ household. Which of the two applies to you often
turns on where you live and what your family and community expect — and that is
a conversation with a local scholar rather than a question this library can
close.
