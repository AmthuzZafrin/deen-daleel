# Do I have to take my shoes off to pray?

## Answer

**Religiously, no — you may pray in clean shoes, and the Prophet did.** In
practice, yes, take them off, because mosque carpets and prayer areas are a
different matter and the rule that governs them is cleanliness, not sanctity.

**The ruling.** Al-Nawawi lists among the benefits of the hadith: **that prayer in
clean footwear is permitted, and that walking in the mosque in footwear is
permitted.** [1] Al-Bahr al-Ra'iq goes further and reports the opposite
inclination: **Ibrahim al-Nakha'i used to dislike removing the sandals and held
that praying in them was better, because of the hadith of removing the sandals**
[2] — and concludes that **prayer with clean sandals and khuffs is closer to good
manners** [3].

**The hadith that fixes the condition.** The Prophet **removed his sandals during
his prayer, so the people removed theirs. When he finished he said: Jibril came to
me and told me there was filth on them. So when one of you comes to the mosque,
let him turn his sandals over — and if he sees filth on them, let him wipe them on
the ground.** [4]

Read what that establishes and what it does not. He did *not* say shoes must come
off; he said **check them**. The instruction is inspection, and the remedy is
wiping, not removal.

**And the ground itself cleans them.** Ibn Taymiyya cites the same instruction —
**when one of you comes to the mosque let him look at his sandals; if he finds
filth on them, let him rub them in the earth, for the earth is a purifier for
them** [5] — together with Umm Salama's question. She said she sometimes walks
over an unclean place and then a clean one, and was told: **the earth purifies
one part of it by another** [6], or in Ibn Taymiyya's wording, **what comes after
purifies it** [7].

So the Hanafis rule directly: **Abu Hanifa and Abu Yusuf held it preferable and
said it is purified by wiping on the ground** [8]. Walking on clean ground after
unclean ground cleans the sole.

**Why you still take them off at the mosque.** Because the concern shifted, and
the books say so. Al-Bahr al-Ra'iq: **whoever intends to enter the mosque should
check his sandals and khuffs for impurity and then enter, out of caution against
soiling the mosque** [9]. That was written when floors were earth and gravel.
Modern mosques are carpeted, and shoes worn on pavements carry in far more than
sandals worn on sand.

The elegant classical solution is Ali's, and it is exactly what a shoe rack does:
**it is related of Ali that he had two pairs of sandals — when he made wudu he
wore one of them to the door of the mosque, then took it off and wore the other,
and entered the mosque to his place of prayer.** [10] Indoor pair, outdoor pair.

**Practical summary**

| | |
|---|---|
| Mosque, carpeted prayer hall | **take them off** — this is what the racks are for |
| Praying at home, on a mat | shoes off, obviously |
| Praying outdoors — a park, a car park, a building site | **keep clean shoes on.** This is the case the permission is for |
| Praying at work on a clean floor | either; follow the room's convention |
| Shoes with visible dirt | wipe them, or take them off |

**One reassurance about after the fact.** If you pray and only afterwards notice
something on your shoe or your clothes, Al-Nawawi settles it: **if he gives the
salam from his prayer and then sees impurity on himself, and it is possible that
it was there during the prayer and possible that it occurred after it — his prayer
is valid, without disagreement.** [11] Repeating is recommended as a precaution,
not required. And there is a separate answer here on praying in clothes with a
mark you are unsure about.

**And a word to reverts.** The shoes-off custom is often the first thing you
encounter at a mosque and it can read as an elaborate purity rule you are bound to
get wrong. It is not. It is housekeeping, with a sound reason behind it — and the
underlying law, as above, is that clean footwear was never the problem.
