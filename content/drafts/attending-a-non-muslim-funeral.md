# Can I attend a non-Muslim funeral?

## Answer

Yes — and there is a hadith with a chapter heading written for exactly this.

**The Prophet ﷺ stood for a Jewish funeral.**

The chapter heading in the *Sahih* reads: "**one who stood for the funeral of a Jew**" — and Ibn Hajar glosses it: "**or the like of him among the People of the Covenant**" [1].

The narration: a funeral passed and he stood, and his Companions stood with him. "When we went to carry [the bier], it was said: **it is the funeral of a Jew** — [and] he said: **death is a terror**" [2]. In another version the objection was raised to him directly, and he answered: "**is it not a soul?**" [3].

Al-Qurtubi draws the inference: "its meaning is that death is something to be alarmed at, an indication of its gravity. And the purpose of the hadith is that a person should not persist in heedlessness after seeing death, since that would suggest making light of the matter of death — **and from this point it is the same whether the deceased is a Muslim or not a Muslim**" [4].

And the reason it matters to be present rather than indifferent: "**in it is an alert that whoever sees that state should be disquieted by it and shaken, and should not show unconcern and indifference**" [5].

**The fiqh goes further than attendance.**

Al-Nawawi, on washing a non-Muslim's body: "**it is not obligatory on the Muslims or on others to wash the disbeliever, without disagreement** — whether he is a protected person or otherwise, because he is not a person of worship nor a person of purification. **And it is permissible for Muslims and others to wash him**; and his disbelieving relatives have more right to him than his Muslim relatives" [6].

Note the shape: not required, expressly permitted, and the family's own claim comes first — which is exactly how you should think about their rites too.

And on the shrouding and burial of a protected person, if he leaves no wealth: "**the sounder [of the two views] is that it is obligatory** — in fulfilment of his covenant, **just as it is obligatory to feed and clothe him in his life**" [7].

A Muslim community was held responsible for burying, at its own cost, someone who had died outside its religion. That is what these books say when the question is asked directly.

**The one limit, and it is narrow.**

Al-Jassas, on the verse about those who mock God's revelations: "in it is an indication of **the obligation to reject the wrong against the one doing it**, and that part of rejecting it is showing dislike when he cannot remove it, and leaving the company of the one doing it and rising from him **until he stops and comes into some other state**" [8].

That is the whole restriction: you do not take part in an act of worship you do not hold, and you do not affirm what you do not believe. It attaches to the moment of the rite, and it lifts when the moment passes.

**Practically, for the funeral of someone you loved.**

- **Go.** The Prophet ﷺ stood for a stranger's funeral because a soul had died. You are being asked about a father, a friend, a neighbour.
- **Be present as a mourner, not a participant in the rite.** Sit, stand when others stand out of ordinary courtesy, be silent during prayers and hymns. Do not recite creeds you do not hold, take communion, or say *amen* to a prayer addressed elsewhere.
- **Do the practical things.** Carry, drive, cook, sit with the family, answer the door. This is where a Muslim should be most visible and it costs nothing in doctrine.
- **You may help with the body**, if the family ask and their own relatives do not object — al-Nawawi permits it in terms.
- **Bury them according to their family's wishes.** Their relatives have the prior right; that is stated.
- **Say what you can honestly say.** *I am so sorry. He was good to me. I am here for whatever you need.* You do not have to say anything about the afterlife, and nobody at a funeral is asking you to.
- **Do not stay away out of fear of appearances.** The alternative — a Muslim who does not come when a non-Muslim relative dies — is the exact opposite of what these texts describe, and everyone present will read it as your religion's verdict on their dead.
