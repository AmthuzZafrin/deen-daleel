# What am I supposed to say in everyday situations?

## Answer

**Most of these phrases are not etiquette. They are short remembrances the books attach to particular moments, and once you know which moment each belongs to they stop being a code you are failing to speak and become simply what you say.** Here is the everyday set, with where each one comes from.

The general principle sits behind all of them: `ومن كان يؤمن بالله واليوم الآخر فليقل خيرا أو ليصمت` [1] — whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let them say something good or keep silent.

---

**In shā' Allāh — "if God wills."**

Say it whenever you state something you intend to do later. The instruction is a verse: *"And say not of anything: Lo! I shall do that tomorrow"* [2] — the clause that completes it being *unless God wills*.

Al-Qurtubi explains what the phrase is actually doing, and it is not superstition: `وأمر في هذه الآية ألا يقول في أمر من الأمور إني أفعل غدا كذا وكذا إلا أن يعلق ذلك بمشيئة الله عز وجل حتى لا يكون محققا لحكم الخبر فإنه إذا قال : لأفعلن ذلك ولم يفعل كان كاذبا وإذا قال لأفعلن ذلك إن شاء الله خرج عن أن يكون محققا للمخبر عنه` [3] — he is commanded not to say *I will do such-and-such tomorrow* without tying it to God's will, so that he is not asserting the report as settled fact; for if he says *I will certainly do it* and then does not, he has spoken falsely, whereas if he says *I will do it if God wills*, he is no longer asserting it as settled.

So it is a truthfulness device. You do not actually know that you will be there tomorrow, and the phrase stops you from claiming that you do. It is not a polite way of saying no, and using it that way empties it.

**Mā shā' Allāh — "what God has willed."**

Two related formulas, and al-Nawawi gives both with their occasions: `وإذا أصابه شئ فليقل قدر الله وما شاء الله فعل وليقل لدفع الآفات ما شاء الله لا قوة الا بالله` [4] — when something befalls him let him say *God decreed, and what He willed He did*; and to ward off harms let him say *what God has willed; there is no power except by God.*

That second one is the phrase people say on seeing something good — a new baby, someone's success, a house, a piece of work. Its function in the books is protective: you acknowledge that what you are admiring came from God rather than from itself, and you disclaim any power of your own over it. Saying it when you admire something is exactly the use the formula was given for.

**Al-ḥamdu lillāh — "praise be to God."**

The default response to anything good, and the response to a great deal that is not. It appears below at meals, on waking, and after a sneeze; al-Nawawi also has it for a good dream: `وإذا رأى في منامه ما يحب فليحمد الله ويحدث بها من يحب ولا يحدث من لا يحب` [5] — let him praise God and tell it to someone he loves, and not to someone he does not.

---

**Eating.**

`بسم الله` before, and if you forget, there is a repair: `إذا أكل أحدكم فليذكر اسم الله، فإن نسي أن يذكر اسم الله في أوله فليقل: بسم الله أوله وآخره` [6] — if he forgets to mention God's name at the start, let him say *in the name of God, at its beginning and its end*. Worth knowing, because forgetting is the normal case and the tradition provides for it rather than treating it as a loss.

**Waking up.**

`السنة أن يذكر الله تعالى إذا استيقظ من نومه وأن يقول الحمد لله الذي أحيانا بعد ما أماتنا واليه النشور` [7] — praise be to God who gave us life after He caused us to die, and to Him is the resurrection.

**Going to sleep.** Al-Nawawi notes it as a thing not to skip: `ويكره أن يضطجع بلا ذكر` [8] — it is disliked to lie down without remembrance.

**Leaving the house.**

`وإذا خرج من بيته قال بسم الله توكلت على الله ولا حول ولا قوة الا بالله` [9] — in the name of God, I rely on God, and there is no power nor strength except by God.

**Morning and evening.**

The longer one, worth learning slowly: `ويقول عند الصباح والمساء اللهم أنت ربى لا إله إلا أنت خلقتني وانا على عهدك ووعدك ما استطعت أبوء لك بنعمتك وأبوء لك بذنبي فاغفر لي فإنه لا يغفر الذنوب الا أنت` [10]. And a short one that stands on its own if that is too much at first: `وأيضا رضيت بالله ربا وبالإسلام دينا وبمحمد صلى الله عليه وسلم نبيا رسولا` [11] — I am content with God as Lord, with Islam as religion, and with Muhammad as prophet and messenger.

**Setting out on a journey — including getting into a car.**

`سبحان الذي سخر لنا هذا وما كنا له مقرنين وإنا إلى ربنا لمنقلبون اللهم إني أسألك في سفري هذا البر والتقوى ومن العمل ما ترضى اللهم هون علينا السفر واطو عنا بعده اللهم أنت الصاحب في السفر والخليفة في الأهل` [12] — *Glory to Him who subjected this to us, for we could not have done it ourselves, and to our Lord we are returning* — then asking for righteousness on the journey, that its distance be folded up, and that God be the companion on the road and the guardian of those left at home.

---

**Sneezing — the one people most often get wrong, because it has three parts and a condition.**

The sneezer speaks first: `إذا عطس أحدكم فليقل الحمد لله` [13]. Then the response, `يرحمك الله` — may God have mercy on you. Then the sneezer replies, `يهديكم الله ويصلح بالكم` — may God guide you and set your affairs right.

The condition matters: `إذا عطس أحدكم فحمد الله فشمتوه وإذا لم يحمد الله فلا تشمتوه` [14] — if he praised God, respond to him; if he did not, do not. Two men sneezed in the Prophet's presence and only one was answered, for exactly that reason.

There is also a recorded case of the third formula used on its own. Some people would sneeze near the Prophet hoping to be blessed, and `رجاء أن يقول يرحمكم الله فكان يقول يهديكم الله ويصلح بالكم` [15] — hoping he would say *may God have mercy on you*, he would say instead *may God guide you and set your affairs right.* That wording is the one commonly used when the person sneezing is not a Muslim, and it has this precedent behind it.

And the manners of it: `ومن أدب العطاس أن يضع العاطس يده على فيه ويخفض بالعطسة صوته ويقول الحمد لله على كل حال` [16] — cover the mouth, keep the sound down, and say *praise be to God in every state*.

---

**A few notes on using these.**

They are described in these books as `السنة` and as recommended — things to do, not conditions of validity. Missing one is not a sin, and the eating formula above shows the tradition building in a repair for forgetting rather than a penalty.

Learn them a few at a time and attach each to its moment rather than memorising a list; the moment is what brings the phrase back. And notice that almost every one of them is short. The longer morning and evening formulas are worth working towards, but *al-ḥamdu lillāh*, *bismillāh*, *in shā' Allāh* and *mā shā' Allāh* — placed where they belong — already cover most of an ordinary day.
