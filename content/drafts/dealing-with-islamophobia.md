# How do I deal with people being hostile about my religion?

## Answer

First, the Qur'an tells you this is coming, so that it does not arrive as evidence that you chose wrong.

*"Assuredly ye will be tried in your property and in your persons, and ye will hear much wrong from those who were given the Scripture before you, and from the idolaters. But if ye persevere and ward off (evil), then that is of the steadfast heart of things"* (3:186) [1].

*Much wrong* — not a little, not occasionally. It is named in advance and put in the same sentence as your property and your body: a real cost, expected, and survivable.

**The instruction.**

*"The good deed and the evil deed are not alike. Repel [the evil deed] with one which is better, then lo! he, between whom and thee there was enmity, [will become] as though he was a bosom friend"* (41:34) [2].

Ibn 'Abbas explains what *the one which is better* is: "**God commanded the believers to patience at the moment of anger, and to forbearance and pardon at the moment of ill-treatment. And when they do that, God protects them from Satan, and their enemy submits to them — as though he were a close friend**" [3].

'Ata' and Mujahid give it a concrete form that is almost startling in its smallness. Asked what *repel with what is better* means, 'Ata' said: "**with the salam**" [4]. And Mujahid: "**peace be upon you — when you meet him**" [5].

Al-Tabari on the outcome: "do what I have commanded you, of repelling the evil-doer's evil by your good treatment which I commanded you toward him — **so that the one who wronged you, between whom and you there was enmity, becomes, through his gentleness to you and his kindness, like a relative of yours from among your cousins, close in lineage to you**" [6].

**And how far it goes.**

Ibn al-'Arabi draws the rule out of the verse: "**the understanding of the verse is: travel the road of the noble, and do not look to the side of requital. Repel without recompense, and do not travel the road of trading.** And there enters into it: **greet with salam one who does not greet you** — the examples are many and the intent is understood, so travel it" [7].

*Do not travel the road of trading* is the whole discipline. Not returning like for like is the point; a transaction is the thing being refused.

He also passes on his teacher's rule for when the two pull against each other: "my teacher Abu Bakr al-Fihri said to me: **whenever two matters come together for you, one of them for the world and the other for God, put God's first — for both will then come to you; and if you put the world first, perhaps both are lost**... and I have tried it and found it so" [8].

**Practically, when it happens.**

- **Expect it, and do not read it as a verdict on your choice.** The Qur'an put this in a sentence about steadfastness, not about mistake.
- **The first response is silence and composure.** Ibn 'Abbas's gloss is patience *at the moment of anger* — the specific moment when you most want to answer.
- **Then a greeting, if you can.** Two of the earliest exegetes read *repel with what is better* as literally saying *peace be upon you*. It is a very small act and it is the one they named.
- **Do not trade.** The point is not to lose the exchange gracefully; it is to refuse to be in an exchange. Ibn al-'Arabi's *repel without recompense* is the whole method.
- **This is not a rule against protecting yourself.** Nothing here asks you to stay somewhere unsafe, to decline to report a crime, or to absorb an assault. Patience at insult and refusal to seek protection are different things, and only the first is being described.
- **Do not let it change how you treat the next person.** The verse's promise is that the enemy becomes *like a close relative* — which only happens if you are still someone a person could become close to.
- **Tell someone.** The Qur'an names this as a shared trial rather than a private one, and carrying it alone is not what steadfastness means.

**One last thing.** The people most often on the receiving end of this are the visibly Muslim — women in hijab, men at the mosque door, children at school. If that is not you, the material above is not addressed to you as advice; it is addressed to you as a description of what someone near you is absorbing. Ibn al-'Arabi's line about the road of the noble was written for the person who *has been* wronged. Standing beside them is a separate obligation and the sources are not quiet about it.
