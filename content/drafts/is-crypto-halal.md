# Is crypto halal?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

## Answer

**No book here mentions cryptocurrency, and this page will not rule on it. But the question decomposes into three older ones that these books argue about at length, and once you see which three, you can follow what contemporary scholars are actually disagreeing about — because they are disagreeing about these, not about anything new.**

**First: is it property at all?**

This sounds abstract and is not. If a token is not `مال` — property recognised by the law — then it cannot be validly sold, inherited, or owed, and every later question falls away.

The definition the jurists work with is functional rather than physical. Al-Sarakhsi: `والمال اسم لما هو مخلوق لإقامة مصالحنا به ولكن باعتبار صفة التمول والاحراز` [1] — wealth is a name for what is created so that our interests may be established through it, by virtue of being **capable of being treated as property and secured**. He unpacks the second half: `وبيانه أن صفة المالية للشئ إنما تثبت بالتمول والتمول صيانة الشئ وادخاره لوقت الحاجة` [2] — the quality of being property is established by `تمول`, and `تمول` is keeping a thing and storing it against a time of need.

Ibn Hajar reports the majority position in the same direction: `ونهى عن إضاعة المال وهو يتناول كل ما يتمول` [3] — the prohibition of wasting wealth takes in everything that can be treated as property; and the sounder reading is `موافقة الجمهور وان المال يطلق على كل ما يتمول` [4] — the majority hold that `مال` applies to everything that can be so treated.

That is a test about function — can it be held, stored, and used to serve real interests — not about whether a thing is metal or paper or a ledger entry. Applied honestly, it does not obviously exclude a token that people hold, transfer, and price. But notice it does not obviously include one either: something no one can secure, or that serves no interest beyond the hope of selling it on, sits awkwardly against `ادخاره لوقت الحاجة`.

**Second: if it works as money, do the currency-exchange rules apply?**

Here the corpus has a precedent much closer than people expect: `فلوس`, the copper coinage whose value came from circulation rather than from the metal. That is a money whose worth is conventional — exactly the feature people think is unprecedented about digital currency.

And the jurists split over it. Ibn Taymiyya: `وأما بيع الفضة بالفلوس النافقة هل يشترط فيها الحلول والتقابض كصرف الدراهم بالدنانير فيه قولان` [5] — whether selling silver for circulating `فلوس` requires immediacy and mutual possession, as in exchanging dirhams for dinars, there are two views. He gives both:

`إحداهما لابد من الحلول والتقابض فإن هذا من جنس الصرف فإن الفلوس النافقة تشبه الأثمان فيكون بيعها بجنس الأثمان صرفا` [6] — it does, because circulating `فلوس` **resemble prices**, so selling them for a price-kind is a currency exchange.

`ولأن الفلوس هي في الأصل من باب العروض والثمنية عارضة لها` [7] — or it does not, because `فلوس` **are originally in the category of commodities, and price-ness is accidental to them.**

That second sentence is the whole modern argument in nine words, written seven centuries before anyone needed it. Is a thing money because of what it is, or because of what people currently do with it — and if the latter, does an accidental function drag the full weight of the exchange rules with it?

He records that this is not a marginal quarrel: `هذه المسألة فيها نزاع مشهور بين العلماء وهو صرف الفلوس النافقة بالدراهم هل يشترط فيها الحلول أم يجوز فيها النسأ على قولين مشهورين` [8].

The same pressure shows up inside the dispute about the operative cause. Al-Shafi'i's position is that `الذهب والفضة فالعلة عند الشافعي فيهما كونهما جنس الأثمان غالبا وهذه عنده علة قاصرة عليهما لا تتعداهما إذ لا توجد في غيرهما` [9] — the cause is that they are the class of prices, and it does not extend beyond them. His opponents pressed exactly the case at issue here: `وقد يوجد الحكم ولا علة كالفلوس بخراسان وغيرهما فإنها أثمان ولا ربا فيها عندكم` [10] — the `فلوس` of Khurasan **are** prices, and yet on your view there is no usury in them.

So: whether a non-precious, conventionally valued medium of exchange falls under the currency rules was already contested, on named grounds, by named authorities. Nobody today is starting from scratch, and nobody today can claim the tradition speaks with one voice.

**Why this matters practically.** If a token counts as `ثمن`, then swapping it for currency is `صرف`, and immediacy and mutual possession are required — which bears directly on deferred settlement, margin, and lending arrangements. If it is `عرض`, a commodity, those constraints do not apply in the same way. Contemporary scholars who reach opposite conclusions are usually differing on this classification, not on anything about blockchains.

**Third: is the trading itself `غرر` or `قمار`?**

This is separate from what the thing is, and it is where much of the real objection lives. Ibn Taymiyya's definition: `والغرر ما لا يقدر على تسليمه سواء كان موجودا أو معدوما` [11] — `غرر` is what one cannot deliver, whether it exists or not; and the vice is that the contract becomes a wager in which one side's gain is the other's loss.

That test bites hardest not on holding a token but on the structures built around it — leveraged positions, contracts settled in cash differences, and tokens whose entire proposition is that someone later will pay more. A purchase of something real, held and transferable, is a different act from a bet on a price movement, and the tradition has always distinguished them.

**What is genuinely absent.**

No text here contemplates a thing with no issuer and no obligor, whose supply is fixed by a protocol and whose value rests on nothing but collective acceptance. Nothing addresses mining, or whether computational effort creates a property right. Nothing weighs volatility as a defect in something claimed to be money — and `ادخاره لوقت الحاجة` is a phrase worth sitting with there. Nothing addresses tokens created explicitly as jokes or as vehicles for speculation, which is a large share of what exists.

**What to do.**

Ask about the specific asset rather than "crypto," because the categories above sort them very differently, and put these questions:

- Is this a token with a real function that can be held and used, or is its only proposition resale at a higher price?
- Are you buying and holding the thing itself, with immediate settlement — or trading contracts, using leverage, or settling differences in cash?
- Does the arrangement pay you a fixed return for lending or staking your holdings? If so the questions about loans and stipulated increase apply, whatever the platform calls it.

On the first classification question — money or commodity — do not expect a settled answer, and be suspicious of anyone who offers one briskly in either direction. The dispute is old, it is recorded above under a different name, and it was never resolved.
