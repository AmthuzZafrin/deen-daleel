# Can I watch films and TV?

## Answer

No source here discusses drama, cinema or a screen. What the tradition worked out in great detail is a closely related problem — **being present where something wrong is happening** — and its answer is more careful and more humane than the version usually repeated.

**The default position.**

Ibn Taymiyya: "it is not for a person to attend places where wrongs are witnessed and he is unable to object — **except for a legal warrant**, such as some matter he needs for the good of his religion or his worldly life that requires his presence, or his being compelled. As for **his attending merely for the spectacle**, and bringing his wife along to watch that — this is something that impugns his uprightness and his propriety, **if he persists in it**" [1].

Two things are worth noticing there. *Merely for the spectacle* is the case he singles out, and *if he persists in it* is the condition he attaches. He is describing a habit, not a lapse.

Ibn Hajar reports the Shafi'i view on being invited somewhere with wrongdoing: one view is "**the prohibition of attending, because it is like consenting to the wrong** — and the scholars of Marw held it sound. If he did not know until he had arrived, let him forbid them; and if they do not stop, **let him leave** — unless he fears for himself in that. The Hanbalis proceeded on the same" [2]. And Malik held that "one of standing should not attend a place where there is amusement at all" [3].

**But the tradition's most careful treatment is al-Jassas's, and it complicates all of that.**

He begins with the verse *you would then be like them*, and gives its two readings: "one, in the disobedience, even if their disobedience does not reach the rank of disbelief; the second, that you are like them in being pleased with their state, in the outward of your affair — and being pleased with disbelief and mockery of God's signs is disbelief. **But whoever sits with them detesting that state of theirs has not disbelieved**, even though it is not permitted for him to sit with them" [4].

So the operative thing is *ridan* — being pleased with it. Detesting it changes the ruling on the person, though not on the sitting.

Then: "in this verse is an indication of the obligation to reject the wrong against the one doing it, and that **part of rejecting it is showing dislike when he cannot remove it**, and abandoning the company of the one doing it and rising from him until he stops and comes into some other state" [5].

**And then he asks exactly the question you are asking, and answers it with a condition.**

*Must a person get up and go far enough that he neither sees nor hears?*

"It has been said regarding this that he ought to do that — **provided that his moving away and abandoning listening does not involve his abandoning a duty upon him**: such as abandoning prayer in congregation because of a sound of singing and instruments he hears; or abandoning attendance at a funeral because of the wailing with it; or abandoning attendance at a wedding feast because of the amusement and play there. **And when there is nothing of that, moving away from them is better**" [6].

That is a rule with a hinge in it. Where staying is required by a duty, the wrong in the room does not cancel the duty. Where nothing is required of you, leaving is better.

He gives two illustrations. Abu Hanifa, on a man at a wedding feast where amusement and play arise: "**it is not proper for him to leave** — and he said: I was tested with it once" [7].

And al-Hasan al-Basri and Ibn Sirin attended a funeral where there was wailing. Ibn Sirin left; al-Hasan stayed, and said: "**if, whenever we saw something false, we abandoned a right — that would work fast in our religion, and we would not come back**" [8].

Two men of the first rank, present at the same wrong, doing opposite things, and neither of them accused. That is the tone the sources actually have on this question.

At the other end, Ibn 'Umar: "he heard a pipe, and **put his two fingers in his ears and moved away from the road**, and said: Nafi', do you hear anything? I said no" [9].

**The other transferable rule: the gaze.**

Al-Qurtubi: "**sight is the greatest door to the heart, and the most trafficked of the paths of the senses to it** — and accordingly falls from its direction are many, so warning against it is obligatory. And lowering it is obligatory from all the forbidden things, **and from everything on account of which temptation is feared**" [10].

And the Prophet ﷺ to 'Ali: "**do not follow the glance with a glance — the first is yours, and the second is not**" [11].

That distinction is exactly usable. Something appears on screen that should not; the first is not counted against you, and the choice about the second is.

**Where the analogy holds and where it does not.**

Everything above is about *a gathering* — a room with people in it doing something. A screen differs in three ways that matter to the reasoning: nobody present is wronged by your being there, no one takes your presence as approval, and you can stop it, which is a power no one at a wedding feast had.

That last one cuts both ways. It removes the excuse of being stuck in the room, and it makes the *ridan* — the being-pleased-with-it — the only thing left. You chose this; nobody put it in front of you.

**Practically.**

- **Watching is not addressed by any of these texts**, and anyone quoting them at you as though it were is skipping a step. What they give is a way of reasoning, not a ruling.
- **Content decides it.** Nakedness, mockery of religion, the glamorising of cruelty — these are judged as themselves, and there is no room in a home for them under the reasoning above.
- **The first glance is not counted; the second is a choice.** That is the actual rule for an unexpected scene, and it is more forgiving than people assume and more demanding than they practise.
- **A duty is not cancelled by wrong in the room.** Al-Jassas's condition means you do not skip the family gathering, the funeral, or the wedding because a screen is on. Al-Hasan's line is the one to remember.
- **Where nothing is owed, leaving is better.** Nothing is owed to a streaming service.
- **Ibn Taymiyya's real target is the habit**, not the incident — attending "merely for the spectacle", *if he persists in it*. That is the honest question to put to your own viewing, and it is a question about a pattern rather than an evening.
- **Being pleased with the wrong is the pivot.** You can watch a film that portrays a wrong; whether you are entertained *by* the wrong is a different matter, and it is the one al-Jassas puts at the centre.
