# Can I sing or listen to nasheeds?

> Reviewer note: Same school-skew caution as music.

## Answer

Yes — and this is the part of the music question where the sources are not divided. The unaccompanied human voice reciting poetry has a licence that Ibn 'Abd al-Barr says he knows of no disagreement about.

**The class of singing that is agreed permitted.**

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, after collecting everything narrated against singing, marks off the part that is not in dispute: "this class of singing the scholars have permitted, and reports have come from the *salaf* permitting it. It is called the singing of the riders, and the singing of *nasb*, and the *hida'* — **these kinds of singing, there is no disagreement about their permissibility among the scholars**" [1].

*Hida'* is the caravan chant; *nasb* is a sung poem with drawn-out notes; *ghina' al-rukban* is what travellers sang on the road. All three are voice alone, carrying poetry.

He then names who did it. 'Umar b. al-Khattab "said: singing is among the provisions of the rider — or he said, of the traveller"; and in another chain, "'Umar said: how excellent a provision for the rider is singing, in the *nasb* style" [2]. Usama b. Zayd was seen singing at the door of his room. And there is this, which is worth sitting with: 'Ubaydullah b. 'Abdullah reported that his father "heard 'Abdullah b. al-Arqam raising his voice singing. 'Abdullah b. 'Utba said: **by God, I have never seen a man more fearful of God than 'Abdullah b. al-Arqam**" [3].

The point of that report is its juxtaposition. The narrator did not experience the singing as sitting oddly beside the piety; he recorded them in the same breath.

'Ata' b. Abi Rabah, asked directly about *hida'*, poetry and singing — the questioner clarifying that he meant the singing of the riders — "said: there is no harm in it, so long as it is not obscene" [4].

**It was done in the Prophet's presence.**

The Prophet ﷺ had the *hida'* chanted for him on journeys. Anjasha's chanting once made the camels quicken with the women riding, and the Prophet ﷺ said: "gently, Anjasha — go easy in driving the glass vessels" [5]. The correction was to the *pace*, not to the singing.

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr's conclusion states both halves at once: "this is something in which **I know of no disagreement among the scholars**, when the poetry is free of obscenity and foulness. As for the singing the scholars disliked, that is this singing that chops up the letters and ruins the metre of the poem and draws it out in pursuit of amusement and rapture, departing from the ways of the Arabs" [6].

So the line he draws is not voice versus silence. It is a line about *what is being sung and why*.

**The voice raised in Qur'an is more than permitted.**

Abu Hurayra reported the Prophet ﷺ saying: "God has not listened to anything as He has listened to a prophet of beautiful voice chanting the Qur'an aloud" [7]. And to Abu Musa al-Ash'ari: "had you seen me last night as I listened to your recitation — you have been given a pipe from the pipes of the family of David" [8].

Ibn Hajar records the exegetical dispute about the word *yataghanna* — whether it means to make melody or to be content — and alongside it, from Ibn 'Abbas: "David used to recite the Psalms in seventy melodies, and recited a recitation from which the fevered man would be moved to rapture" [9].

That is the tradition's own vocabulary for a beautiful voice: it uses the language of music without apology.

**Where nasheed sits.**

By every definition above, nasheed is inside the licence. Al-Nawawi puts it plainly: "the Arabs call recitation *ghina'*, and it is not the singing that is disputed; rather it is permitted. The Companions permitted the singing of the Arabs, which is mere recitation and humming, and they permitted the caravan chant, and did it in the presence of the Prophet ﷺ... and this and its like is not forbidden" [10].

Al-Fatawa al-Hindiyya adds a content test that is useful for choosing what to listen to: "if the poem contains a wise saying, or admonition, or fiqh, it is not disliked... and reciting permissible poems, there is no harm in it" [11]. Against which: "reciting a literary man's verse, when it contains mention of licentiousness and wine and the boy, is disliked" [12].

That is a test you can apply to a track list this afternoon.

**The honest complication: the Hanafi school is stricter about bare singing.**

Al-Fatawa al-Hindiyya reports three positions inside the school on *taghanni mujarrad* — singing as such: "they disagreed about bare singing. Some said it is forbidden absolutely, and listening to it is disobedience — and this is the choice of Shaykh al-Islam; though if one hears it suddenly there is no sin on him. And some said there is no harm in singing in order to gain from it the composition of rhyme and eloquence. And some said singing is permitted to dispel loneliness when one is alone, and not by way of amusement — and to this Shams al-A'imma al-Sarakhsi inclined" [13].

Notice that even the strictest of the three excuses you for hearing it unexpectedly, and that the other two permit it outright for a purpose. And Ibn Hajar's survey of the wider disagreement shows this is a real spread and not a fringe: "al-Mawardi said: they disagreed about it. A group permitted it absolutely; a group forbade it absolutely; Malik and al-Shafi'i — in the sounder of two views — disliked it; prohibition is transmitted from Abu Hanifa, and likewise most Hanbalis; and Ibn Tahir transmitted in his *Kitab al-Sama'* the permission from many of the Companions" [14].

**The argument the permitters made.**

Ibn Hajar reports it fairly: "those who permitted it argued that in it is a refreshing of the soul — **so if he does it in order to strengthen himself for obedience he is obedient, or for disobedience he is disobedient; and otherwise it is like strolling in a garden or watching the passers-by**" [15].

And al-Ghazali's argument reduced to its core: "the *hida'* with *rajaz* and poetry never ceased being done in the Prophetic presence, and sometimes it was requested — and it is nothing but poems measured out with pleasing voices and measured melodies; and likewise singing is measured poems delivered with delightful voices and measured melodies" [16].

That is, the two things people place on opposite sides of the line are the same thing described twice.

**One last detail, and it is the practical one.**

The Ansari women were singing at a wedding and included a line claiming knowledge of tomorrow. The Prophet ﷺ said: *leave that one* — and told them to go back to what they had been saying. Ibn Hajar draws the inference: "in it is an indication of the permissibility of listening to praise and elegy, in what contains no exaggeration leading to excess" [17].

He stopped a *line*. He did not stop the singing.

**So, practically.** Nasheed, chant, *a cappella* poetry, recitation with a beautiful voice — permitted, and on Ibn 'Abd al-Barr's report without disagreement. Sing in the car, sing to your children, sing on a journey. Choose by content: what carries remembrance, wisdom or admonition is encouraged; what dwells on sex, drink and contempt is what the sources actually object to. If your school holds the stricter Hanafi position on singing for its own sake, follow it — and note that even there, hearing something unbidden carries no sin.

Instruments beyond the *duff* are a separate and genuinely disputed question; that dispute does not reach the voice.
