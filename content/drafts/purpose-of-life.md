# What does the Qur'an say about the purpose of human life?

## Answer

**The Qur'an answers this in one sentence.** 51:56: **"I created the jinn and
humankind only that they might worship Me."** [1]. Everything else here is
unpacking what that sentence does and does not mean, because the English word
"worship" carries the wrong weight.

**What "worship" is doing in that verse.** Ibn Hajar reports the commentators
grappling with an obvious objection — if that is why people were created, why do
many not do it? He gives two readings. The first takes the general wording to
mean something specific: **"وحاصل التأويلين أن الأول محمول على أن اللفظ العام
مراد به الخصوص"** — *the first is that the general expression is meant
specifically* [2]. The second keeps it general but reads it as capacity rather
than outcome: **"والثاني باق على عمومه لكن بمعنى الاستعداد أي خلقهم معدين لذلك
لكن منهم من أطاع ومنهم من عصى"** — *the second keeps its generality but in the
sense of preparedness: He created them equipped for it, but some obeyed and some
disobeyed* [3].

And he states the distinction that resolves it:
**"أن المراد بالخلق خلق التكليف لا خلق الجبلة فمن وفقه عمل لما خلق له ومن خذله
خالف"** — *what is meant is the creation of moral responsibility, not the
creation of innate nature; whoever is granted success acts according to what he
was created for, and whoever is forsaken goes against it* [4].

That is the key. The verse is not describing what people automatically do; it is
describing what they were *equipped and made accountable for*. Purpose here is a
vocation, not a mechanism.

**The Qur'an also gives the purpose in the form of a test.** 18:7: **"Lo! We have
placed all that is on the earth as an ornament thereof that We may try them:
which of them is best in conduct."** [5]. Read the two verses together and the
picture is coherent: the world is furnished attractively *on purpose*, and the
attraction is part of what is being tested. Not a trap — a test needs something
at stake.

**What that makes of ordinary life.** 57:20: **"Know that the life of the world
is only play, and idle talk, and pageantry, and boasting among you, and rivalry
in respect of wealth and children"** [6], with the image of a crop:
**"whereof the growth is pleasing to the husbandman, but afterward it drieth up
and thou seest it turning yellow, then it becometh straw"** [7]. And 18:46:
**"Wealth and children are an ornament of the life of the world. But the good
deeds which endure are better in thy Lord's sight for reward, and better in
respect of hope."** [8].

Notice what these verses do *not* say. They do not say wealth and children are
bad; both are called an ornament, which is a positive word. What is denied is
that they are what lasts. The contrast at [8] is between ornament and what
endures, not between evil and good.

**Putting it plainly.** On this material, the purpose of a human life is to know
and serve Allah [1], with the world deliberately made attractive so that what a
person actually chooses means something [5], and with the specific verdict being
about conduct — *which of them is best in conduct* — rather than about belief
alone. Success is defined at [4] as acting in line with what you were made for.

**And what it does not mean.** It does not mean life should be spent in the
mosque and nothing else. On the reading of "worship" implied by [5] — where the
test is conduct amid ornament — the ordinary content of a life is the material
the test is made of: work, family, money, how you treat people. The question the
verses pose is not whether you have those things but what you are doing with
them, and whether you know which of them survive [8].

If you are new to Islam and looking for something to *do* with this: the honest
answer from these verses is that it is less a matter of finding a special
purpose than of reorienting the life you already have. The prayers exist partly
to keep re-asking the question five times a day.

**On the language above:** "'ibada" is worship or service — the root means
servitude, and in the sources it covers far more than ritual; "taklif" is moral
responsibility, the term at [4]; "dunya" is the near world, this life;
"al-baqiyat al-salihat" is "the good deeds which endure" at [8].

**A note on scope.** This is a large question and the classical tradition
approaches it from several directions — theology, tafsir, and the literature on
the heart each frame it differently. The passages here are Qur'anic verses and one
commentator's discussion of the central one. If you want to go further, that
discussion at [2]–[4] is the natural place to start.
