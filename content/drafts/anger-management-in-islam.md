# What does Islam say about controlling anger?

## Answer

A man came to the Prophet ﷺ and said: *Advise me.* He said: **"Do not become angry."** The man asked again, and again — and got the same three words each time.

Al-Nawawi: "for this reason the Prophet ﷺ said to the one who said to him *advise me*: Do not become angry. And he repeated it several times — he said: Do not become angry. So he added nothing to the advice beyond *do not become angry*, despite his repeating the request. And this is a clear proof of the enormity of the harm of anger" [1].

**But you are not commanded to stop feeling it.**

This matters, because people read "do not become angry" and conclude that the anger rising in them is itself a sin. The commentators say otherwise, and they say it plainly.

Al-Khattabi: "the meaning of his words *do not become angry* is: avoid the causes of anger and do not expose yourself to what brings it on. As for anger itself, forbidding it is not feasible, because it is a natural matter that does not depart from one's constitution" [2]. Another: "what is of the category of animal nature cannot be repelled, so it does not enter into the prohibition, because that would be obligating the impossible; and what is of the category acquired by discipline — that is what is meant" [3].

Ibn Hibban is the most direct: "he meant: *do not do anything you have been forbidden after becoming angry* — not that he forbade him something he was created with and has no means of repelling" [4]. And a third reading given by Ibn Hajar: "its meaning is: do not do what anger commands you to do" [5].

So the target is the *action*, and the *cultivated habit* of flying off — not the feeling itself. If you feel a surge of anger and say nothing and do nothing, you have obeyed the hadith completely.

**Why it was the whole of the advice.**

The man who received it thought about it afterwards: "I reflected on what he said, and behold — anger gathers all evil" [6]. Ibn al-Tin: "in his words *do not become angry* he ﷺ gathered the good of this world and the next, because anger leads to mutual severing — and from it comes [the loss of] gentleness — and it may lead to harming the one you are angry at, which then diminishes one's religion" [7].

One diagnosis of the root is worth sitting with: "it is said its meaning is *do not become angry*, because the greatest thing anger arises from is arrogance — since it occurs when something he wants is opposed, and arrogance carries him to anger. So the one who humbles himself until the self-importance leaves him is safe from the evil of anger" [8].

That is an uncomfortable but useful test. Ask what the anger is protecting. Very often it is not justice; it is the assumption that things should go your way.

**Strength is redefined.**

"The strong one is not the one who throws people down in wrestling; the strong one is the one who controls himself when angry" [9]. Al-Nawawi: "rather, he is the one who controls himself when angry — this is the excellent and praised one, whom few are able to take on as a character or share in his merit" [10].

**Four things to do when it rises.**

*Say the words.* Mu'adh reported: "Two men reviled each other in the presence of the Prophet ﷺ until anger showed in the face of one of them, and the Prophet ﷺ said: I know a word which, if he said it, his anger would leave him — *a'udhu billahi min al-shaytan al-rajim*" [11]. Al-Nawawi: "in it is that anger for other than Allah is from the incitement of Shaytan, and that the angry person should seek refuge and say *a'udhu billahi min al-shaytan al-rajim*, and that it is a cause for the removal of anger" [12].

Sulayman b. Surad's version of the same incident records what happened next, and it is instructive. The man was told what the Prophet ﷺ had said [13] — and he snapped back: "Do you think me mad?" [14]. Al-Nawawi's verdict on the retort: "this is the speech of one who has no understanding in the religion of Allah and has not been refined by the lights of the noble Law, and who supposed that seeking refuge is specific to the insane" [15].

Take the point: the moment you most need this is the moment you will most resent being told it.

*Change your posture.* Abu Dharr reported: "When one of you becomes angry while standing, let him sit down; and if the anger leaves him, [well and good] — otherwise let him lie down" [16].

*Make wudu.* Ibn Hajar lists the remedies together: "what helps in leaving anger is calling to mind what has come concerning the merit of restraining rage, and what has come concerning the outcome of anger's fruit of warning; and that he seek refuge from Shaytan, as preceded in the hadith of Sulayman b. Surad; and that he perform ablution" [17].

*Remember who is actually acting.* Al-Tufi's remedy is the deepest one here: "the strongest of things in repelling anger is calling to mind real *tawhid* — namely that there is no doer except Allah, and every doer other than Him is an instrument for Him. So whoever has something disliked directed at him from the direction of another, and calls to mind that had Allah willed, He would not have enabled that other to do it — his anger is repelled" [18].

That reframes the object. The person in front of you is not the ultimate cause of what has happened to you.

**A physical observation worth keeping.**

Ibn Hajar passes on a long description of what anger does to the body and then this: "so that if the angry man saw himself in the state of his anger, his anger would subside out of shame at the ugliness of his form and the alteration of his make" [19].

**What it is worth.**

"There is no draught a servant swallows better for him or greater in reward than a draught of rage [swallowed] for Allah" [20]. And: "Whoever restrains a rage while able to carry it out, Allah will call him before the heads of all creation on the Day of Resurrection until He gives him his choice of whichever of the *hur* he wishes" [21] — al-Tirmidhi graded that *hasan gharib*.

Anas relates a short exchange that puts the whole thing in place: "Messenger of Allah, what is severest of all things? He said: *the anger of Allah*. He said: What saves from the anger of Allah? He said: *Do not become angry*" [22].

**One exception.**

Ibn Hajar, after all of the above: "all of this concerns worldly anger, not religious anger" [23]. Anger at genuine injustice, at cruelty, at what Allah has forbidden — that is a different thing, and the Prophet ﷺ had it. The test is whether the anger is on behalf of a wronged party or on behalf of your own standing.

**In practice.** When it rises: stop talking. Say *a'udhu billahi min al-shaytan al-rajim*. Sit if standing, lie down if sitting. Go and make wudu. Do not send the message, do not make the decision, do not say the sentence — anger passes and words do not. And afterwards, when you are calm, ask the harder question the commentators pointed at: was that about the wrong done, or about me?

قال البخاري: حدثنا عثمان بن أبي شيبة، حدثنا جرير، عن الأعمش، عن عدي بن ثابت، قال: قال سليمان بن صرد: استب رجلان عند النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم، ونحن عنده جلوس، فأحدهما يسب صاحبه مغضبا قد احمر وجهه، فقال النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم: " إني لأعلم كلمة لو قالها لذهب عنه ما يجد،
