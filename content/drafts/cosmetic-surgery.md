# Is cosmetic surgery allowed?

## Answer

**The corpus draws a line here, and it is a real one: restoring what was lost or removing what harms is permitted — there is a decided case on it — while altering a sound body for the sake of beauty falls under an explicit prohibition. Most actual questions sit near the line rather than at either end, and this page will show you where the line is drawn and why.**

**The prohibition.** The text everything rests on: `لعن الله الواشمات والمستوشمات والنامصات والمتنمصات والمتفلجات للحسن المغيرات خلق الله` [1] — a curse on those who tattoo and are tattooed, who pluck facial hair and have it plucked, and who file gaps between their teeth **for beauty**, altering God's creation. Ibn al-'Arabi cites the same words with the same two operative phrases: `والمتفلجات للحسن المغيرات خلق الله` [2].

Two things are doing the work. `للحسن` — for beauty — and `المغيرات خلق الله` — altering the creation of God. Filing teeth is singled out precisely because it was done by older women to imitate the gap that young girls have naturally: appearance changed on a sound body, to seem other than one is.

**The exception, stated outright.** Ibn Hajar, having said that lengthening or thickening hair with another's hair all falls under the prohibition as altering God's creation, immediately continues: `فكل ذلك داخل في النهي وهو من تغيير خلق الله تعالى قال ويستثنى من ذلك ما يحصل به الضرر والأذية كمن يكون لها سن زائدة أو طويلة تعيقها في الاكل أو إصبع زائدة تؤذيها أو تؤلمها فيجوز ذلك والرجل في هذا الأخير كالمرأة` [3] — **excepted from this is whatever causes harm or injury**: an extra or overlong tooth that impedes eating, an extra finger that injures or pains her — that is permitted; and a man is like a woman in this last point.

So the category is not "surgery" and not "changing how you look." It is *harm*. Where a feature harms, removing it is outside the prohibition entirely.

**And there is a decided case, which is worth more than any amount of analogy.** 'Arfaja b. As'ad lost his nose in battle: `أن جده عرفجة بن أسعد قطع أنفه يوم الكلاب فاتخذ أنفا من ورق، فأنتن عليه، فأمره النبي - صلى الله عليه وسلم - فاتخذ أنفا من ذهب` [4] — he took a nose of silver, it stank, and the Prophet ordered him to take one of gold.

Look at what that decision costs. Gold is prohibited to men; the instruction overrides the prohibition rather than working around it. Al-Kasani draws out the rule: `وكذا لو جدع أنفه فاتخذ أنفا من ذهب لا يكره بالاتفاق لان الانف ينتن بالفضة فلا بد من اتخاذه من ذهب فكان فيه ضرورة فسقط اعتبار حرمته` [5] — by agreement it is not disliked, because the nose putrefies with silver so gold becomes necessary; **there was necessity, so the consideration of its being forbidden fell away.**

A man given a prosthesis to replace what he lost is the paradigm of reconstructive surgery, and the tradition settled it in his favour at the cost of a standing prohibition.

**The tradition also treats visible disfigurement as a real harm, not a vanity.** This shows up where you would least expect it — in the rules for removing a tattoo one is obliged to remove. Al-Nawawi: `فان أمكن ازالته بالعلاج وجبت ازالته وان لم يمكن الا بالجرح فان خاف منه التلف أو فوات عضو أو منفعة عضو أو شيئا فاحشا في عضو ظاهر لم تجب إزالته` [6] — if it can be removed by treatment, removal is obligatory; if only by wounding, then where destruction is feared, or the loss of a limb, or the loss of a limb's function, **or something grossly disfiguring in a visible limb**, removal is not obligatory.

Gross disfigurement of a visible part sits in that list beside losing a limb. The reasoning weighs it as an actual injury.

**Now the hard edge, which honesty requires stating plainly.** The most sympathetic cosmetic case in these books was refused. A woman came about her daughter: `فأصابتها الحصبة أو الجدري فسقط شعرها وقد صحت وزوجها يستحثنا وليس على رأسها شعر أفنجعل على رأسها شيئا نجملها به` [7] — measles or smallpox, her hair fell out, she has recovered, her husband is pressing, there is no hair on her head; may we put something on it to make her look well? In another wording: `وفى رواية أنها مرضت فتمرط شعرها وفى رواية فاشتكت فتساقط شعرها وأن زوجها يريدها` [8]. The answer was a curse on the one who joins hair and the one who has it joined, and al-Nawawi treats the ruling as unambiguous: `وهذه الأحاديث صريحة في تحريم الوصل ولعن الواصلة والمستوصلة مطلقا وهذا هو الظاهر المختار` [9].

Illness, hair loss, a new marriage, a husband urging it — and still no. Whatever the line is, it is not "permitted whenever the motive is understandable." Note also the additional reason given: `ولأنه يحرم الانتفاع بشعر الآدمي وسائر أجزائه لكرامته بل يدفن شعره وظفره وسائر أجزائه` [10] — it is forbidden to make use of a human being's hair or any other part, out of the honour due to them; hair and nails are buried. Al-Kasani applies the same to teeth: `ولو سقط سنه يكره أن يأخذ سن ميت فيشدها مكان الأولى بالاجماع` [11] — taking a dead person's tooth is disliked by consensus. If a procedure uses tissue from another person, that is a separate question from the cosmetic one, with its own answer here.

**And the line is not "never change your appearance."** Al-Nawawi, on the same hadith that curses plucking: `وهذا الفعل حرام الا إذا نبتت للمرأة لحية أو شوارب فلا تحرم إزالتها بل يستحب عندنا` [12] — the act is forbidden, except that where a beard or moustache grows on a woman, removing it is not forbidden but **recommended** in our school. An appearance-altering removal, positively encouraged, because what is being removed is out of place rather than merely unadmired.

There is a genuine dissent worth knowing. Ibn Jarir held to the strictest reading: `وقال ابن جرير لا يجوز حلق لحيتها ولا عنفقتها ولا شاربها ولا تغيير شئ من خلقتها بزيادة ولا نقص` [13] — no alteration of her created form at all, by addition or subtraction. Al-Nawawi records it and does not follow it, but it is in the books.

**Putting it together.**

Taken as a whole, the material supports a distinction, and it is one you can apply:

- **Restoring function or form lost to injury, illness or a birth defect** is on the permitted side. It has a decided case behind it, the exception for harm covers it, and the reasoning treats visible disfigurement as a genuine injury. Reconstruction after burns, accident, mastectomy or cleft palate is this case.
- **Removing something that harms or impedes** — the obstructing tooth, the painful extra digit — is expressly excepted, for men as for women.
- **Altering a sound body to be more admired** is what the prohibition is aimed at, in its own words: `للحسن`, for beauty. Nothing here carves out an exception for it, and the closest sympathetic case was refused.

**Where it genuinely runs out.** The corpus never asks whether severe distress about one's appearance is itself a `ضرر` of the kind that opens the exception — which is the crux of most modern cases, and the reason a nose that a person cannot bear to see sits differently from a nose that does not breathe. It has nothing on surgical risk, on anaesthesia, or on procedures that trade a permanent alteration for a temporary appearance. And where a procedure is partly reconstructive and partly not, no text divides it for you.

**What to do.** Ask which side of the harm line your case sits on, and be honest in answering: is this restoring something lost or removing something that hurts, or is it improving something that works? If it is clearly the first, the material above is on your side and you do not need to feel uneasy about it. If it is clearly the second, that is what the prohibition names. If you genuinely cannot tell — and many real cases cannot — that is exactly when to put the specifics to a scholar you can talk to, along with what the surgeon has told you about the risk, which no book here can weigh for you.
