# Can I be friends with the opposite gender?

## Answer

**Short answer: yes, you can have colleagues, classmates, neighbours and
professional relationships across genders — the sources make explicit room for
that. What they close off is the private version: being alone together, the
sustained look, the relationship that is a friendship in name and a courtship in
substance.**

**Start with the room the sources make.** Al-Nawawi, having stated the general
prohibition on looking at someone you are not related to, immediately excepts
ordinary dealings: **"كانت حاجة شرعية فيجوز النظر كما في حالة البيع والشراء
والتطبب والشهادة ونحو ذلك ولكن يحرم النظر في هذه الحال بشهوة"** — *where there
is a lawful need, looking is permitted, as in buying and selling, medical
treatment, giving testimony and the like; but looking with desire remains
forbidden even then* [1]. Buying, selling, treating a patient, testifying — the
list is of ordinary transactions between people, and the carve-out is real.
Al-Nawawi elsewhere permits a man to be in company with several women:
**"وكذا لو اجتمع رجال بامرأة أجنبية فهو حرام بخلاف ما لو اجتمع رجل بنسوة أجانب
فإن الصحيح جوازه"** — *several men with one woman is forbidden; a man with
several women is, on the sound view, permitted* [2]. And necessity overrides:
**"ويستثنى من هذا كله مواضع الضرورة بأن يجد امرأة أجنبية منقطعة في الطريق أو
نحو ذلك فيباح له استصحابها"** — *cases of necessity are excepted from all of
this, as when he finds a woman stranded on the road* [3]. So work, study,
medicine, business, a group setting, helping someone in trouble: these are
inside the permission, not exceptions grudgingly made.

**What is closed off, first, is being alone together.** Al-Nawawi:
**"وأما إذا خلا الأجنبي بالأجنبية من غير ثالث معهما فهو حرام باتفاق
العلماء"** — *a man alone with an unrelated woman with no third person present
is forbidden by the agreement of the scholars* [4]. He heads a chapter of his
Muslim commentary **"باب تحريم الخلوة بالأجنبية والدخول عليها"** [5]. The hadith
is in Sahih Muslim from Ibn 'Abbas: **"لا يخلون رجل بامرأة إلا ومعها ذو محرم"**
[6], and Al-Hidaya gives the version naming the reason: **"ألا لا يخلون رجل
بامرأة ليس منها بسبيل فإن ثالثهما الشيطان"** — *the third of them is Shaytan*
[7]. Ibn Hajar records **"لا تدخلوا على المغيبات فان الشيطان يجرى من ابن آدم
مجرى الدم"** — *do not enter upon women whose husbands are away, for Shaytan
runs in the son of Adam as blood runs* [8], where the stated reason is what the
situation does to people rather than what either of them intends.

**And second, the sustained look.** Al-Nawawi distinguishes the accidental
glance from the choice to keep looking: **"معنى نظر الفجأة أن يقع بصره على
الأجنبية من غير قصد فلا إثم عليه في أول ذلك ويجب عليه أن يصرف بصره في الحال فان
صرف في الحال فلا إثم عليه وان استدام النظر أثم"** — *the sudden glance is when
his eye falls on her unintentionally; there is no sin in that first moment, and
he must look away at once; if he does, no sin, and if he keeps looking, he sins*
[9]. He notes what that puts on whom: **"ويجب على الرجال غض البصر عنها في جميع
الأحوال"** — *men are obliged to lower their gaze in all circumstances* [10]. And
the obligation runs both ways: Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, **"وكما لا يجوز للرجل أن ينظر
إلى المرأة فكذلك لا يجوز للمرأة أن تنظر إلى الرجل"** [11], grounded in the
address to believing women — **"And tell the believing women to lower their gaze
and be modest"** [12].

**So what does that leave, practically?** Working alongside someone, speaking
with them about the work, being in a class or a meeting or a group with them,
helping them, being decent to them — inside the permission at [1], [2] and [3].
Meeting one-to-one behind a closed door, a lift home alone at night, a private
messaging thread that runs late and gets personal, an emotional dependence that
your spouse or theirs would be hurt to read — these are the things the material
at [4] to [8] is aimed at, and the reason given for the rules is not that
anything has happened but that the situation makes it likelier.

**A note on the modern shape of this.** None of these texts addresses direct
messages, a group chat, or a video call. The physical element the hadiths
describe — a closed room, a house entered — is absent from a text thread; the
element the reasoning points at, two people alone in something private, is not.
Scholars differ on how the rules apply there, and this answer does not settle it.
What the sources do give you is the test they were reasoning with: is there a
third party to this, and would you be content for there to be?

**On the honest difficulty.** If you converted, or moved into a Muslim community
from outside it, some of your existing close friendships are with people of the
opposite gender and the reading above changes what those look like. That is a
real loss and it is worth naming rather than glossing. What the texts here
restrict is a specific shape — private, one-to-one, sustained — not the person
and not basic decency toward them. Group settings, public settings, and keeping
your spouse or their spouse in the picture usually preserve most of what
mattered about the friendship.

**On the language above:** "khalwa" is being alone together, which these texts
prohibit; "ajnabi"/"ajnabiyya" means someone you are not related to in a way that
bars marriage; "mahram" is such a relative; "nazar al-faj'a" is the sudden
unintended glance; "hajah shar'iyya" is a lawful need, the ground on which
ordinary dealings are permitted.

**A caution.** These are the classical positions on the situations the classical
texts took up. Whether a particular workplace arrangement, an online friendship,
or a study group falls inside the permission or outside it is a judgement, and
the schools and contemporary scholars differ. If a specific relationship in your
life is the real question, put it to a scholar or imam who can hear the details.
