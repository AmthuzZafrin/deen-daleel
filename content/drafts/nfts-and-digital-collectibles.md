# Is buying and selling NFTs allowed?

> Reviewer note: The classical levers are gharar and whether the thing sold is property at all — mal mutaqawwam — and whether it can be delivered.


## Answer

**The objection you hear most often — "an NFT isn't a real thing, so it can't be sold" — is the weakest one available, and the tradition says so explicitly.** Clearing that out of the way first is worth doing, because it leaves the objections that actually bite.

**The rule people reach for, and what it was about.**

Hakim b. Hizam asked the Prophet ﷺ about a man coming to him wanting something he did not have, which he would then go to the market to buy and deliver. The answer was `لا تبع ما ليس عندك` [1] — do not sell what you do not have. Ibn Qudama explains the defect: `ولأنه باع ما لا يقدر على تسليمه، فأشبه الطير في الهواء` [2] — because he has sold what he cannot deliver, so it resembles a bird in the air.

Note the reason. Not that the bird is unreal. That he cannot hand it over.

The paradigm of a forbidden sale is in the same vein: `نهى عن بيع حبل الحبلة، وحبل الحبلة أن تنتج الناقة ما في بطنها ثم تحمل التي نتجت` [3] — the sale of the offspring of the offspring, where a she-camel must first bear, and then what she bore must itself conceive. Two layers of maybe.

**Ibn Taymiyya states the principle exactly, and it settles the naive objection.**

`وليست العلة في المنع لا الوجود ولا العدم بل الذي ثبت في الصحيح عن النبى انه نهي عن بيع الغرر` [4] — the cause of the prohibition is neither existence nor non-existence; what is established is the prohibition of *gharar*. And his definition: `والغرر ما لا يقدر على تسليمه سواء كان موجودا أو معدوما` [5] — gharar is what cannot be delivered, whether the thing exists or not. He says it again to be unmistakable: `وهكذا المعدوم الذي هو غرر نهى عن بيعه لكونه غررا لا لكونه معدوما` [6] — the non-existent thing is forbidden *for being gharar, not for being non-existent*.

By that test a token on a public ledger is in a stronger position than the argument assumes. It can be delivered; the transfer is the whole mechanism. Whatever is wrong with NFTs, "it isn't a physical object" is not where the classical objection lies.

**What does bite, and it is sharper than the first objection.**

Ibn Taymiyya's account of *why* undeliverable sales are forbidden is the part that reaches speculation: `فان موجب البيع تسليم المبيع والبائع عاجز عنه والمشترى انما يشتريه مخاطرة ومقامرة` [7] — the buyer is purchasing by way of risk-taking and gambling. And then the symmetry that names the structure: `فان امكنه اخذه كان المشترى قد قمر البائع وان لم يمكنه أخذه كان البائع قد قمر المشترى` [8] — if he can take it, the buyer has gambled the seller out of it; if he cannot, the seller has gambled the buyer out of his money.

His conclusion on a sale whose object is of unknown measure and description — `واذا حمل فالمحمول لا يعرف قدره ولا وصفة` — is `فهذا من القمار وهو من الميسر الذي نهى الله عنه` [9].

Read that against a market where the item has no use, produces nothing, and is bought only because someone later may pay more. Every gain is exactly someone else's loss; nothing is produced in between. That is the shape Ibn Taymiyya is describing, and it is the serious question for NFTs — not their immateriality but their zero-sum speculation.

**A third strand: the sale of what you have not taken possession of.**

`من ابتاع طعاما فلا يبعه حتى يستوفيه` [10] — whoever buys food must not sell it on until he takes possession — with Ibn 'Abbas adding that he reckoned everything else was like it. Al-Nawawi records that the Companions were struck for reselling a bulk purchase before moving it. Whether a ledger entry is *qabd*, possession, is a real question that these texts pose and cannot answer, because none of them was written about a ledger.

**What these books do not settle.**

Whether a token is `مال متقوم` — property with recognised value — at all. That is the foundational question and no author here was in a position to consider it. Nothing addresses the enormous variation the word "NFT" covers: a receipt for a real, enforceable right (a ticket, a membership, a deed to something existing) is a completely different transaction from a purely speculative collectible with no claim attached, and the material above would treat them differently. Nothing addresses whether the underlying network's own mechanics raise separate issues. Nothing addresses the images themselves, which where they depict living beings raise the questions covered in the answer on generated images.

**What to ask.**

- Does the token carry an actual, enforceable right to something — access, membership, ownership of a real asset? If yes, ask about *that* underlying thing. If no, you are asking about a speculative counter.
- Would you hold this if its price could never rise? That question separates a purchase from a wager, and on the reasoning at [7] and [8] it is close to the heart of the matter.
- Can it be delivered, and does transfer give you real control?
- Is the price known and the thing described, or are both floating?
- Is the image of a living being? A separate question with its own answer.

Take those distinctions to a scholar rather than the word "NFT", which covers transactions the tradition would rank very differently. And do not accept the argument that it is fine because it is only digital, nor that it is forbidden because it is only digital. On the material above, neither of those is the question.
