# What does keeping family ties actually require?

## Answer

*Silat al-rahim* gets quoted at people constantly and defined for them almost never. The sources do define it, and the definition is more concrete and more flexible than the slogan suggests.

**The shortest definition on this sheet is Ibn Abi Jamra's**, quoted by Ibn Hajar, and it is worth having in full because it names the five things and then gives the principle underneath them: "Keeping ties of kinship is by wealth, by help with a need, by warding off harm, by a cheerful face, and by supplication — and the comprehensive meaning is conveying whatever good one can and repelling whatever harm one can, **according to capacity**" [1].

Every part of that is doing work. Money is only the first item, not the whole of it. A cheerful face when you see them is on the list as a discharge of the duty. *Du'a* is on it — something you can do for a relative you cannot reach at all. And the closing phrase, *bi-hasab al-taqa*, according to capacity, is a limiting clause in the definition itself, not a concession bolted on afterwards.

Al-Qurtubi, on the same page, sorts it into two registers: "the kinship that is kept is general and particular. The general is the kinship of religion, and keeping it is obligatory by mutual affection, mutual sincere counsel, justice and fairness, and fulfilling the obligatory and recommended rights. As for the particular kinship, it adds maintenance of the relative, attending to their circumstances, and overlooking their slips — and the degrees of their entitlement to that vary, as in the first hadith of the Book of Manners: the nearest, then the next nearest" [2]. So the specifically *family* portion is three things beyond ordinary decency: **material support, attention to how they are doing, and letting small offences go** — graded by closeness.

**The obligation is not in dispute.** Ibn al-'Arabi states it as agreed: "the community has agreed that keeping ties with relatives is obligatory and that severing them is forbidden" — and he notes it holds across religious lines, citing Asma' asking about her idolater mother and being told "keep ties with your mother", from which "the merit entered into keeping ties with the disbelieving relative" [3]. The reason for the weight is a play on the word itself: "I am al-Rahman, and it is *al-rahim*; I derived a name for it from My name. Whoever keeps it, I keep him; and whoever severs it, I sever him" [4]. And in the phrasing of another narration: "Those who show mercy, the Merciful shows mercy to them. Show mercy to those on earth, and He who is in heaven will show mercy to you. Kinship is a branch from the Merciful: whoever keeps it, God keeps him, and whoever severs it, God severs him" [5].

**The famous promise attached to it is not what it sounds like.** "Whoever is pleased to have his provision expanded and his term extended, let him keep ties of kinship" [6] — which raises the obvious objection that lifespans are fixed. Al-Nawawi gives what he calls the soundest answer: "this increase is by blessing in his lifetime, being enabled to obey, filling his time with what benefits him in the hereafter, and preserving it from being wasted in other things" [7]. Ibn Hajar reports the same for the material half: "the scholars said the meaning of the expansion in provision is blessing in it ... because keeping ties with his relatives is charity, and charity increases wealth and makes it grow" [8]. So the promise is about the quality of what you have and the use you make of your time, not about a larger balance or a later date.

That last observation — that *sila* is a form of *sadaqa* — is where the fiqh works pick it up. Al-Sarakhsi: "gift and charity may be to strangers and may be to relatives, and the latter is better, because of the keeping of kinship in it — and to this the Prophet pointed when he said: the best charity is to a hostile relative" [9]. He then draws a legal consequence from it: 'Umar ruled that "whoever gives a gift to a *mahram* relative and the relative takes possession of it, he has no right to take it back" [10], where a gift to a stranger could be revoked. The tie changes the legal character of the transfer.

**And the hardest case has an answer.** A man came to the Prophet saying he kept ties with relatives who cut him off, was good to them while they were bad to him, and forbore while they behaved badly. The answer: "If you are as you say, it is as though you are feeding them hot ashes, and a helper from God will not cease to be with you against them as long as you continue that" [11]. Al-Nawawi's gloss makes plain who carries the loss: "its meaning is as though you are feeding them hot ashes — a likening of the pain that reaches them to what reaches one who eats hot ashes; **and there is nothing upon this one who does good; rather the enormous sin falls on them, for cutting him off and bringing harm upon him**" [12].

Note what that does and does not say. It does not say the one-sided relationship is comfortable, or that it must be maintained at the same intensity, or that the harm being done to him has stopped being harm — the sin of it is stated explicitly. It says that continuing to convey good, at whatever level he can, leaves the fault entirely on the other side. Read together with Ibn Abi Jamra's *according to capacity*, that is a workable position rather than an impossible one.

**So, concretely, what is required?** Convey what good you can and keep harm away, in proportion to what you can actually do; support them materially where they need it and you are able; know how they are; let small things go; greet them; pray for them. What is required of a person with money and safety is not what is required of a person with neither, and the sources say so in the definition rather than as an exception to it.
