# I keep repeating the same sin — is my repentance even accepted?

## Answer

It is accepted. And the position that says otherwise — that relapsing shows the
first repentance was never real — has a name attached to it, and the standard
commentary on al-Bukhari says it is wrong and names the evidence.

**The hadith that settles it.** In Sahih Muslim: "a servant committed a sin and
said: *O Allah, forgive me my sin*. And Allah, Blessed and Exalted, said: My
servant has committed a sin, and he knew that he has a Lord who forgives sin and
takes to account for sin. Then he returned and sinned, and said: *my Lord,
forgive me my sin*..." — the cycle repeats a third time, and then: "**then he
returned and sinned and said: my Lord, forgive me my sin. And Allah, Blessed and
Exalted, said: My servant has committed a sin, and he knew that he has a Lord who
forgives sin and takes to account for sin — do as you wish, for I have forgiven
you**" [1].

Read what is actually being praised there. Not that he stopped. That each time,
he *knew he had a Lord who forgives sin and takes to account for sin* — and
turned back on that knowledge. The forgiveness is granted after the third
relapse, not before the first.

**The disputed condition, and its rejection.** Ibn Hajar lists conditions later
scholars added to repentance: "some of those we met added other things to the
conditions of repentance — among them... **that he not return to that sin, for if
he returns to it, it becomes clear that his repentance was void**" [2]. Then he
rules on it: "the first is recommended, the second and third fall within the
limits of moral responsibility, and **the fourth and last was attributed to
al-Qadi Abu Bakr al-Baqillani — and the hadith coming twenty chapters later
refutes it**" [3]. The hadith he is pointing forward to is the one above.

So the claim you are afraid of is a real classical position, held by a real
authority, and it was answered.

**Al-Qurtubi states the principle directly.** On the same material: "in it is
evidence for the validity of repentance after it has been broken by returning to
the sin — **because the first repentance was an act of obedience, and it
concluded and was valid**" [4].

That sentence is worth keeping. The repentance you made last time was a good deed
that was completed and accepted at the moment you made it. Sinning again is a new
sin, not a retroactive cancellation of an old act of worship. You do not owe the
old one back.

**And the effect of each new turning.** Ibn Taymiyya: "what is meant is seeking
forgiveness with the heart along with the tongue — for **one who repents of a sin
is like one who has no sin**" [5].

**Where the real danger sits.** Not in relapsing, but in *israr* — persisting.
Al-Qurtubi, glossing the verse's clause about those who "will not knowingly
repeat" what they did: "Ibn 'Abbas, al-Hasan, Muqatil and al-Kalbi said: **and
they know that persisting is harmful, and that leaving it is better than
continuing**" [6]. And on the same phrase: "it is said — and they know that I
punish for persisting" [7].

Persisting is a stance towards the sin, not a count of how many times you fell.
Someone who keeps failing and keeps turning back has not adopted it. And on the
same page: "'Abdullah b. 'Ubayd b. 'Umayr said: and they know that if they repent,
Allah will turn to them in forgiveness" [8].

**Two things worth doing differently.**

First, look at what you are repenting *from*. Ibn Taymiyya notes the common gap:
"many people, at the time of repentance, bring to mind only some of the things
connected with indecency or its preliminaries, or some wrongdoing with the tongue
or the hand" [9] — and leave out the rest, especially what is owed to other
people. A repentance that keeps failing sometimes has an unaddressed piece in it.

Second, the practical signs the earlier Muslims looked for. Fath al-Mawsili:
"its signs are three — opposing one's desire, weeping much, and enduring hunger"
[10]. Not feelings, but three changed habits. Al-Qurtubi's own description of the
turning point is not a grand resolution either — a person looks at himself, finds
regret rising for what he neglected, and leaves the like of what came before out
of fear of Allah: "then it is true of him that he is one who repents" [11].

**So.** Repent again. Then again. The hadith describes a servant doing exactly
that and ends with forgiveness, and the only view that would condemn you was
identified by name and refuted. What would put you in danger is not the falling —
it is deciding that turning back is no longer worth doing.
