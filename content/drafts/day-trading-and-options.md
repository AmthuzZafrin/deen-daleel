# Is day trading or options trading allowed?

> Reviewer note: Distinct from the stocks-and-shares answer, which is about owning a company. Here the levers are selling what you do not possess, gharar, and interest on margin.


## Answer

**Owning shares in a company and trading contracts on a screen are not the same question, and this page is about the second.** There is a separate answer on stocks and shares, which is about becoming part-owner of a business. What follows is about the activity: buying to sell within minutes or days, selling what you have not got, and trading contracts whose value derives from something you never intend to own. The classical material speaks to all three, and it speaks about *risk-bearing* rather than about screens.

**The first rule: do not sell what you do not have.**

`لا تبع ما ليس عندك` [1] — said to a man who wanted to sell first and buy afterwards. Ibn Qudama gives the defect: `ولأنه باع ما لا يقدر على تسليمه، فأشبه الطير في الهواء` [2] — he has sold what he cannot deliver, so it resembles a bird in the air.

Short selling is that transaction with the order of operations unchanged: sell now, acquire later, hope the price falls in between. Whether borrowing the asset first cures the defect is precisely the question to put to a scholar, because borrowing is how the modern practice answers the classical objection, and it is not obvious that it does.

**The second rule, and the one that reaches furthest.**

`نهى عن ربح ما لم يضمن` [3] — he forbade profit on what one does not bear the liability for, and of it: `قال الترمذي حديث صحيح` [11].

Al-Sarakhsi shows how the test works in practice: `انه اشترى على ما ليس في ضمانه ونهى رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم عن ربح ما لم يضمن` [4], and where a gain arrives without the risk having sat with the person, `فهذا ربح حصل لا على ضمانه` [5] — this is a profit obtained not on his own liability.

That is the sharpest instrument in this chapter. It asks: during the time you stood to gain, were you exposed to loss on the thing itself? Not on the price bet — on the thing. It is why a merchant's profit is clean and why a purely financial spread often is not.

Ibn Taymiyya applies it to a debt sold on: it may be transferred, but `ولكن لا يجوز بربح بل لا يباع إلا بالقيمة لئلا يربح المسلف فيما لا يضمن` [6] — not at a profit, only at value, lest he profit on what he does not bear.

**The third rule: possession before resale — and here the schools genuinely differ.**

`من ابتاع طعاما فلا يبعه حتى يستوفيه` [7] — whoever buys food must not sell it on until he has taken it in full. Ibn al-Mundhir reports agreement: `أجمع أهل العلم على أن من اشترى طعاما فليس له أن يبيعه حتى يستوفيه` [8].

Two things then matter for a modern portfolio. First, the scope is contested: Ibn 'Abd al-Barr reports `الأصح عن أحمد بن حنبل أن الذي يمنع من بيعه قبل قبضه هو الطعام` [9] — the soundest position from Ahmad restricts the ban to foodstuffs, with the implication that other goods may be resold before possession. Second, and more usefully, Ibn Qudama gives the underlying test: `ولو دخل في ضمان المشتري، جاز له بيعه والتصرف فيه، كما بعد القبض` [10] — once the risk has passed to the buyer, he may sell and deal in it as though he had taken possession.

So the question about rapid trading is not "did you hold it long enough". It is whether the risk of the thing was genuinely yours while you held it.

**What these books do not reach.**

No author here considered an option — a contract to buy a right to buy — or a future, or a contract for difference, or leverage supplied by a broker at interest. Nothing addresses settlement periods, margin calls, or whether an entry in a broker's register is `قبض`. The interest on a margin loan is a separate matter with its own answer and is not analysed here. And nothing addresses index funds or long-term holding, which is the subject of the stocks answer rather than this one.

**What to ask.**

Bring a scholar the mechanics, since "trading" covers transactions the tradition would rank very differently:

- Do you own the underlying asset, or a contract about its price? If the second, ask about that contract specifically, because none of the material above was written about it.
- Are you selling something you do not yet own?
- While you stood to gain, could the thing itself have been lost or damaged at your expense? That is the test at [3] and [5], and it is the one that separates trade from a bet on a number.
- Is any part of it financed by an interest-bearing margin loan?
- Is the contract settled in cash only, with neither side ever intending delivery?

If the honest description of what you are doing is that you never intend to own anything and are taking a position on a price movement, say that plainly when you ask. The answer you get will be more useful than one given about "investing".
