# How do I dispose of a damaged Quran?

## Answer

There is a clear precedent here, it is one of the best-attested events in early Islamic history, and it points somewhere most people find surprising: **the standard classical answer is burning.**

**The precedent.** When 'Uthman had the standard *mushaf* copied, he ordered every other written copy destroyed. Ibn Kathir: "he ordered that everything besides that, of the people's *masahif*, be burned, so that people's readings would not differ across the lands — **and the Companions of his time agreed with him on that, and not one of them objected to it**" [1]. 'Ali defended it in the strongest terms, according to the report al-Qurtubi cites: "Fear God, and beware of excess concerning 'Uthman and your saying *the burner of the masahif*. By God, he burned them only by the counsel of a body of us, the Companions of Muhammad" — and, from another route, "**had I been the ruler at 'Uthman's time, I would have done with the *masahif* the same as 'Uthman did**" [2].

Ibn Battal drew the general ruling out of it, and the phrasing is the point: "in this hadith is the permissibility of burning by fire books that have God's name in them, **and that this is an honouring of them and a preservation from their being trodden underfoot**" [3]. The reasoning is not that burning is a lesser evil — it is that leaving pages where they will be walked on is the worse outcome.

**But the method was already disputed, and the sources record how.** Ibn Hajar on the wording of the reports: "*erasure* is more general — it may be by washing or by burning; and most of the narrations are explicit about burning, so that is what occurred; and it is possible that each of the two happened, according to what whoever had any of it in his hand thought best. 'Iyad was categorical that they washed them with water and then burned them, as a further step in doing away with them" [4].

And a named dissent, in the very next line: "'Abd al-Razzaq narrated through Tawus that he used to burn letters containing the *basmala* when they accumulated, and 'Urwa did likewise — **and Ibrahim disliked it**" [5].

**Then Ibn Hajar does something worth pausing on.** Having established that burning was what happened, he says it should not simply be copied: "**this ruling is what occurred at that time; as for now, washing is preferable**, given what has made removal necessary" [6]. A jurist stating in one sentence that a well-attested precedent settles what was done then, not what is best now.

**And there is a straightforward contrary position.** Ibn Qudama, discussing a case where a man's baggage is ordered burned as a penalty, exempts one thing: "**as for the *mushaf*, it is not burned — because of its sanctity**" [7]. He offers an alternative disposal instead: "it is possible that the *mushaf* be sold and the price given in charity" [8]. He extends the exemption further: "and if he has with him anything of the books of hadith or of knowledge, they too should not be burned, because the benefit of that returns to the religion" [9].

So the corpus holds two things at once: 'Uthman burned *masahif* with the Companions' agreement, and Ibn Qudama says a *mushaf* is not burned because of its sanctity. Both are on the page. Neither is a slip.

**On burial and water, the sheet gives a method — though for a related case.** Al-Qurtubi, listing what the *mushaf*'s sanctity requires, describes what to do with water that has washed Qur'anic writing off: it should not be poured "on a rubbish heap, nor in a place of impurity, nor on a place that is trodden — but rather in a corner of the ground, in a spot people do not tread; or he digs a hollow in a pure place ... then fills it in; or in a large river, where it mixes with its water and flows away" [10]. That is the burial and the running-water option, set out with the reasoning attached: what is being avoided is the pages ending up underfoot or among rubbish.

The same passage gives the principle behind all of it. The Prophet, seeing Qur'an written on the ground, said: "**God curse whoever did this. Do not place God's Book anywhere but in its proper place**" — and 'Umar b. 'Abd al-'Aziz struck a son of his for writing Qur'an on a wall [11]. Among the same list: "part of its sanctity is that it not be written on the ground nor on a wall" [12].

**So, practically.**

Every option people are usually offered turns out to have classical footing, and the sources disagree about which is best rather than about whether the thing may be done at all:

- **Burning** is the *Uthmanic precedent, endorsed by 'Ali and, according to Ibn Kathir, uncontested among the Companions — and read by Ibn Battal as an act of honouring. Ibrahim al-Nakha'i disliked it, and Ibn Qudama excludes the *mushaf* from burning on grounds of sanctity.
- **Burying** in a clean place people do not walk over is described in al-Qurtubi's list of what the sanctity requires.
- **Water** — a large flowing body where the writing dissolves — is in the same list, and Ibn Hajar preferred washing in his own day over burning.
- **Passing it on** is the one Ibn Qudama actually proposes: sell it, give the money away. If the copy is still usable, giving it to someone who will read it needs no ruling at all.

What runs through every option is a single concern, stated plainly in each source: the pages must not end up trodden on, in the rubbish, or in a place of filth. That, not the method, is what the sanctity attaches to. **Modern recycling is not addressed anywhere in these works**, and I am not going to extend the reasoning to it and present the extension as their ruling — but you can see clearly enough what question a scholar would be asking about it.
