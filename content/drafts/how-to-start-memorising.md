# How do I start memorising Quran?

## Answer

The sources give a surprising amount of practical method here, and the two most useful things they say both cut against how people usually go about it.

**Start with the promise, which is repeated four times in one *sura*:** "And in truth We have made the Qur'an easy to remember; but is there any that remembereth?" [1]

**The first practical instruction is not about learning. It is about review.** Ibn Mas'ud's report: "How wretched it is for one of them to say *I have forgotten such-and-such a verse*. **Keep reviewing the Qur'an, for it escapes from the breasts of men faster than livestock from their tethers**" [2]. The chapter this sits under in *al-Sunan al-Kubra* is titled *the command to review the Qur'an*, and the metaphor is exact: not that memory fades slowly, but that it bolts. Whatever schedule you set for new material, the review has to be bigger.

The same report adds a correction to how one speaks about it: "let none of you say *I have forgotten such-and-such a verse*. The Messenger of God said: rather, **he was made to forget**" [3].

**The second is about pace, and it is the more counter-intuitive one.** 'Abdullah b. 'Amr had memorised the whole Qur'an and was reciting all of it every night. The Prophet told him to slow down, and he kept pushing back:

> "I gathered the Qur'an and recited all of it every night. Word of that reached the Prophet, and he said to me: recite it every month. I said: Messenger of God, let me make use of my strength and my youth. He said: recite it every twenty. I said: Messenger of God, let me make use of my strength and my youth. He said: recite it every ten. I said: Messenger of God, let me make use of my strength and my youth. He said: recite it every seven. I said: Messenger of God, let me make use of my strength and my youth — **and he refused**." [4]

Another version of the exchange ends the same way — "recite it every fifteen ... every ten ... every five — **and he gave me no concession**" [5]. There is a floor as well as a ceiling: "one who recites the Qur'an in less than three has not understood it" [6]. And in the version Ibn Taymiyya cites, the instruction is explicit: "recite the Qur'an in a month. I said: I find I have strength. He said: then recite it in seven, **and do not exceed that**" [7].

The end of the story is the part worth keeping. Ibn Taymiyya quotes 'Abdullah b. 'Amr in old age: "**Would that I had accepted the Messenger of God's concession** — for I have grown old and weak" [8]. A man who negotiated himself into a harder routine at twenty spent the rest of his life wishing he had taken the easier one.

Ibn Taymiyya also records his working method, which is the most concrete study advice on the sheet: "he would recite his seventh of the Qur'an to one of his household during the day — and what he was to recite, he would go over during the day, so that it would be lighter on him at night" [9]. Recite it to another person, in daylight, before the session that matters. That is *'ard*, presenting to a listener, and it is how it was done.

**On struggling with it.** The well-known hadith puts the fluent reciter with the noble scribes, and gives the one who stumbles two rewards. Al-Nawawi transmits the necessary correction: "as for the one who stumbles in it, he is the one who falters in his recitation from weakness of memory, and he has two rewards — a reward for the recitation and a reward for his stumbling in his recitation and his difficulty. Qadi 'Iyad and other scholars said: its meaning is **not** that the one who stumbles has more reward than the proficient one — rather the proficient one is superior and greater in reward" [10]. The struggle is rewarded; it is not a shortcut past the work.

**On being a beginner, and on how much is realistic.** Ibn 'Abd al-Barr records something that is rarely said: "the Companions — and they are the ones addressed by this address — had none among them who memorised the entire Qur'an and completed it in the lifetime of the Messenger of God except a few: Ubayy b. Ka'b, Zayd b. Thabit, Mu'adh b. Jabal, Abu Zayd al-Ansari and 'Abdullah b. Mas'ud — **and every one of them would stop at its meanings and the meanings of what he had memorised of it**, and know its interpretation and retain its rulings; and one of them might well know many rulings of the Qur'an without having memorised its *suras*" [11]. He follows it with Hudhayfa: "we learned faith before we learned the Qur'an" [12].

**On forgetting, which is what stops most people from starting.** There is a stern strand and a lenient one, and Ibn Hajar lays out both with the grading attached. The stern: "the early scholars differed over forgetting the Qur'an. Some of them counted it among the major sins — Abu 'Ubayd narrated from al-Dahhak b. Muzahim, as his own statement: *no one learns the Qur'an and then forgets it except by a sin he has committed*, because God says *whatever of misfortune striketh you, it is what your right hands have earned* — and forgetting the Qur'an is among the greatest misfortunes" [13]. Ibn Hajar then grades the supporting evidence honestly: the marfu' hadith from Anas about no sin being greater "**has weakness in its chain**" [14], while the report from Abu al-'Aliya — "we used to count it among the greatest of sins that a man should learn the Qur'an and then sleep on it until he forgets it" — is one "**whose chain is good**" [15].

Against that, Ibn 'Abd al-Barr transmits Ibn 'Uyayna's reading, and it is decisive for anyone anxious about this: the threat "concerns abandoning the Qur'an and abandoning acting by what is in it, and *forgetting* is meant here as abandoning" [16] — and then: "**one who was eager to memorise it and had it slip away from him is not a forgetter of it**, so long as he holds lawful what it makes lawful and forbidden what it forbids, because such a one is not a forgetter of it. And if it were so, the Prophet would never have forgotten anything of it — and he did forget, and said: *he has reminded me of a verse I had forgotten*" [17].

**So, concretely.**

Review more than you learn; the sources treat loss as the default and review as the countermeasure. Recite what you are working on to another person, out loud, in daylight — that is the method that is actually described. Set a pace you can hold for decades rather than one you can hold for a month; the man who did the opposite is on record regretting it. Understand what you are memorising as you go, which is what the few Companion memorisers are specifically described as doing. Don't let fear of forgetting stop you: the harshest reports on it are the ones Ibn Hajar marks as weak, and the strongest classical reading applies them to abandonment, not to a memory that slips. And if you stumble, that is rewarded — just not more than fluency, so keep going.
