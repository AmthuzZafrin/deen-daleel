# Where's the line between a gift and a bribe?

## Answer

**The tradition supplies an unusually clean test for this, and it is a question rather than a rule: would this have been given to you if you did not hold the position you hold? If the answer is no, it is not a gift, whatever it is called.**

**The prohibition first.** `لعن رسول الله - صلى الله عليه وسلم - الراشي والمرتشي` [1] — a curse on **the one who gives a bribe and the one who takes it.** Both sides, which matters: this is not only a question for the person in the position of power.

**The case that generates the test.** A man was sent to collect the zakat, and on returning said: this is for you, and this was given to me as a gift. The response is the classic one, and Ibn Hajar reports the reasoning around it: `قال المهلب حيلة العامل ليهدى له تقع بان يسامح بعض من عليه الحق فلذلك قال هلا جلس في بيت أمه لينظر هل يهدى له فأشار إلى أنه لولا الطمع في وضعه من الحق ما أهدى له` [2] — the official's device for attracting gifts is to go easy on some of those who owe; and so he said, **why did he not sit in his mother's house and see whether he would be given anything?** — indicating that but for the hope that he would reduce what was owed, nothing would have been given to him.

The principle is stated directly: `أن الحقوق التي عمل لأجلها هي السبب في الاهداء له وأنه لو أقام في منزله لم يهد له شئ فلا ينبغي له أن يستحلها بمجرد كونها وصلت إليه على طريق الهدية` [3] — **the very duties for which he was appointed are the reason the gift came to him**; had he stayed at home nothing would have been given; so he may not treat it as lawful merely because it arrived under the description of a gift.

The label on the transaction is irrelevant. What matters is the counterfactual.

**Ibn Battal's three motives — and only one is clearly fatal.** `قال ابن بطال دل الحديث على أن الهدية للعامل تكون لشكر معروفه أو للتحبب إليه أو للطمع في وضعه من الحق فأشار النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم إلى أنه فيما يهدى له من ذلك كأحد المسلمين لا فضل له عليهم فيه وأنه لا يجوز الاستئثار به` [4] — a gift to an official may be **thanks for a kindness done**, or **cultivating his goodwill**, or **hoping he will reduce what is owed**; and the Prophet indicated that in what is given him on that account he is like any other Muslim with no special claim to it, and may not keep it for himself.

Ibn Hajar's verdict on the third: `والذي يظهر أن الصورة الثالثة إن وقعت لم تحل للعامل جزما` [5] — **the third case, if it occurs, is definitely not lawful for the official.**

That is a usefully graded answer. A gift given in hope of favourable treatment is decided. A gift given to build goodwill with someone in a position of power is not innocent either, on this reading — it is what the whole discussion is about. Genuine thanks for something already properly done is the one that survives, and even then the further question below applies.

**And the remedy is not "return it awkwardly."** `قال فأوجب النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم أخذ الهدية وضمها إلى أموال المسلمين` [6] — the gift was to be taken and **added to the public funds.** Where something has been given because of an office, it belongs to the institution rather than to the officeholder. That is a workable modern answer too: hand it in.

**Applying the test.**

Ask, honestly: *would I have received this if I did not hold this role?*

- **A supplier's gift to the person who awards their contract; anything offered while a decision affecting the giver is pending; a payment to make a process go faster or come out differently.** No — and the third case above covers it. That the giver calls it a gift, a facilitation fee, or a token of appreciation changes nothing.
- **A modest company-wide gesture — a shared hamper, a customary seasonal gift given to everyone regardless of role.** These do not fail the test in the same way, since they do not track your decision-making power.
- **Genuine thanks after the fact, for something you did properly and would have done anyway.** This is Ibn Battal's first category, and it is the mildest — but if the person may deal with you again, the safe course is the one above: declare it, or hand it to the organisation.

**Where the corpus leaves a genuine gap.** It does not address the situation of someone who must pay an official to obtain a right they are already entitled to — a permit lawfully due, a document being withheld. That is a live modern question with real disagreement among contemporary scholars, and the material here does not settle it, because the reports are about officials receiving, not about citizens being extorted. Nor does anything here address corporate hospitality, conflict-of-interest policies, or the disclosure regimes that now do much of this work.

**What to do.**

Use the test, and use your employer's policy as well — most organisations have one, and following it is usually both the lawful and the prudent course. If something is offered and you find yourself constructing a justification for keeping it, that effort is itself the answer. And if you are the one offering, remember that the curse in the first line falls on the giver first.
