# Can I post pictures of myself online?

> Reviewer note: Same caution as photographs.

## Answer

Two separate questions get run together here. *Is the photograph itself allowed?* is the image-making question, and it is contested. *Should you post it?* is a different question, and the sources have more to say about it than you might expect.

**On the image itself — the reasoning the jurists used points away from your phone.**

The prohibition on images is not stated as a rule about pictures in the abstract. Every commentator gives the same reason: idolatry, and the veneration that leads to it. Al-Sarakhsi writes it as an operative test and then applies it case by case.

He begins with size: an image "visible to onlookers from a distance" is the problem; "**but if it is small there is no harm, because one who worships images does not worship a very small one** — and on Abu Musa's signet-ring there were two flies; and when Daniel's ring was found, on its stone were two lions with a man between them, licking him" [1].

Two Companions' rings carried figures, and the Hanafi master's reason is that nobody venerates something that small.

He then makes the principle explicit. Where the image is behind the person praying, "the dislike is lighter, because **the meaning of veneration and of resembling those who worship images is absent here**" [2]. And on a rug: if the image lies where his face goes, disliked, "because the meaning of veneration is present through the nearness of the face to the image; and if it is where his feet go, there is no harm, **because the meaning of veneration is not achieved in it**" [3].

The whole discussion turns on one thing — whether veneration is in play — and a photograph on a phone, scrolled past among four hundred others, is the furthest thing in the world from a venerated object. Al-Sarakhsi's own reasoning, applied honestly, does not reach it.

Al-Qurtubi makes the historical point that supports this reading. Solomon's *timathil* included images of "the prophets and the scholars, and they were made in the places of worship **so that people would see them and increase in worship and striving**" [4]. So the practice was once permitted — "this indicates that image-making was permitted at that time, and that was abrogated by the law of Muhammad ﷺ" [5] — and the reason for the change is stated: "the wisdom in that was that he ﷺ was sent **while images were being worshipped**, so the soundest course was their removal" [6].

A prohibition given a stated purpose is one you can think with. If you want the classical rule to bind you at its strictest, follow your school. But nobody in these books is describing a camera roll.

**Now the part the sources really are about: what posting does to you and to the people who see it.**

Al-Qurtubi, commenting on the verses about mockery and fault-finding, states something that reads as though it were written about a feed: "**deeds are presumptive indications, not decisive proofs.** Consequent upon this is not going to excess in exalting one whom we have seen performing righteous acts, and not despising a Muslim whom we have seen performing bad ones — rather you despise and blame that bad state, not that erring person" [7].

That is the entire pathology of a social feed named in one sentence, seven centuries early. What you see is a presumptive sign — a curated one — and treating it as proof is a mistake in both directions: excessive admiration for the polished life, and contempt for the visible failure.

The other half applies to the person doing the looking. "Bakr b. 'Abdullah al-Muzani said: if you wish to see faults in abundance, consider a fault-finder — for he only finds fault in people by the surplus of what is in him" [8]. And the Prophet ﷺ: "one of you sees the speck in his brother's eye and leaves the log in his own" [9]. And: "**part of a man's good fortune is that his own faults occupy him away from the faults of others**" [10].

**So, practically.**

- **A photograph of yourself, for identification, work, or keeping in touch, is not what the classical prohibition describes**, on al-Sarakhsi's own stated reasoning. If your school takes the strict position on images generally, follow it — but do not accept a ruling that skips the reasoning.
- **The thing that would land inside the classical rule is a portrait displayed for admiration** — large, prominent, meant to be looked at with regard. That is worth asking yourself honestly about a profile built for exactly that effect, because the *reason* the jurists gave transfers even where the object does not.
- **Cover what should be covered.** Posting does not change what modesty requires; it enlarges the audience, permanently, to people you did not choose.
- **Do not post other people without asking**, and especially not children. Their faces are not yours to publish.
- **Watch what it makes you** — the comparison, the performance, the counting. Al-Qurtubi's warning cuts against both the posting and the scrolling: what you show is a presumptive sign, and so is what you see.

The question "is it haram" is not the most useful one you can ask about this. "What is it doing to me, and to how I see other Muslims" is the one the sources actually equip you to answer.
