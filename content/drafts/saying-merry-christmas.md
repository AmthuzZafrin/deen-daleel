# Can I say Merry Christmas?

## Answer

**No source in this corpus rules on a verbal greeting at someone else's festival.** What the jurists wrote about is *attending* the festival and *eating what was slaughtered* for it. So the honest answer has two parts: here is what they did rule on, and here is the reasoning that lets you decide the part they did not.

**What the sources actually prohibit.**

The evidence is a verse — *"and those who do not witness falsehood"* — read by three early authorities as meaning the festivals of the idolaters. Ibn Taymiyya assembles them: "God Most High said *and those who do not witness falsehood* — **Mujahid said: the festivals of the idolaters**; and likewise said al-Rabi' b. Anas. And Qadi Abu Ya'la said: *a question on the prohibition of attending the festivals of the idolaters*. And Abu al-Shaykh al-Asbahani narrated... from **al-Dahhak** on His words *and those who do not witness falsehood* — he said: **the festival of the idolaters**" [1].

A fourth reading, from 'Amr b. Murra, widens it: *those who do not witness falsehood* means "**they do not keep company with the people of idolatry upon their idolatry, and do not mix with them in it**" [2].

Note the qualifier in that last one — *upon their idolatry*. It is not a prohibition of company; it is a prohibition of company **in the act of worship**.

And on the food: "**eating the sacrifices of their festivals falls within this that they agreed to dislike — indeed in my view it is more severe.** Abu al-Qasim was asked about riding in the ships that the Christians ride to their festivals, and he disliked that, for fear of wrath descending on them because of the idolatry they have gathered upon" [3].

**Why the reasoning is about *rida* — being pleased with it.**

Ibn Taymiyya elsewhere: "**whoever is pleased with his own disbelief and another's disbelief, and his own corruption and another's, and his own sins and another's — he is not a follower of what pleases God**" [4].

That is the actual worry. Not the syllables, but assent — endorsing as true what is not true.

**And here is where the Hanafis' own method cuts the other way.**

Qadi Khan's chapter on expressions that entail disbelief is exactly the place to look for whether a *phrase* commits you to a creed. What is striking is how hard the jurists work to *avoid* that conclusion.

The general rule is severe: acting on something while believing it to be disbelief means "**he has consented to disbelief — and consent to disbelief is disbelief**" [5]. But then the applications:

A man says something in Persian that sounds like an endorsement of another's wrongdoing. "**They said: if he intended by it to censure that act, he does not disbelieve**" [6].

A man says *I will not pray*. "Al-Natifi related from Muhammad that he said: **a man's saying *I will not pray* bears four possibilities.** The first: I will not pray, because I have already prayed it. The second: I will not pray at your word, for one better than you has commanded me. The third: I will not pray, out of dissoluteness and frivolity. **In these three cases he does not disbelieve**" [7] — only the fourth, denying the obligation itself, does.

And al-Natifi's conclusion, which is the principle you need: "**so on this basis, if he speaks without qualification and says *I will not pray*, he does not disbelieve — because this expression is ambiguous**" [8].

An ambiguous phrase is not construed as a creed. That is a Hanafi rule of interpretation, applied to words far more alarming than *Merry Christmas*.

**So what does "Merry Christmas" mean?**

Ask what you are actually saying, because that is the question the method above puts to you.

- **If you mean *I affirm that God has a son and that this day is His birth*** — nobody permits that, and the *rida* reasoning bites in full.
- **If you mean *I hope you have a good day with your family*** — that is what the phrase conveys in ordinary English usage, to the person hearing it and to you saying it. On al-Natifi's rule it is an ambiguous expression carrying an innocent sense, and an ambiguous expression is not read as a confession of creed.

That is genuinely as far as the evidence in front of us reaches. It does not deliver a ruling, and this page will not manufacture one.

**What we can say with confidence, because the sources do say it.**

- **Do not attend the religious service.** The mass, the prayer, the rite — that is *shuhud al-zur* on the reading of Mujahid, al-Rabi' and al-Dahhak, and it is the one thing the evidence clearly reaches.
- **Do not eat what was slaughtered for the occasion.** Ibn Taymiyya calls this more severe than attending.
- **Company is not the problem; company *in the worship* is.** 'Amr b. Murra's wording is precise, and it is the whole difference between a family dinner and a service.
- **The safest wording is easy and costs you nothing.** *Have a lovely day. I hope you all have a wonderful time. Enjoy the holiday.* These carry the kindness with no ambiguity at all, and most families will not notice the difference.
- **The unsafe thing is coldness.** Nothing in these sources asks you to be unkind to your parents on the one day of the year they most want to see you.

**One more thing, which is where this whole area comes from.** Ibn Hajar, tracing the phrase *this is our festival*, finds its origin in 'A'isha's report about the two singing girls: "**every people has a festival, and this is ours**" [9]. The concern behind the whole discussion is that the Muslim community should have festivals of its own and know them as its own — not that a Muslim should have nothing warm to say to a neighbour.
