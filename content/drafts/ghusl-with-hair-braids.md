# Do I have to undo my braids or hair extensions for ghusl?

## Answer

**Generally no, you do not have to undo them** — the water has to reach the roots
of your hair, and if it does, the braid can stay. The question is whether it
does.

**The hadith this rests on** is a woman asking the Prophet exactly your question.
Umm Salama told him she bound the braids of her head tightly and asked whether
she had to undo them for the bath from janaba. He said no: **it is enough for you
to pour three handfuls over your head** [1]. Ibn Qudama presses the point that
makes it decisive — the Prophet gave that answer **along with her having informed
him that she bound the braids of her head tightly** [8].

Not loose plaits. *Tightly bound*, and he was told so. The answer was still no.

**Where the schools land.** Ibn Nujaym gives the Hanafi range, and the leading
position is the lenient one: **the first: it is sufficient that the water reach
the roots, whether the hair is undone or plaited — and this is the apparent
position of the school.** [2] The reasoning is straightforward: the hair itself
is not skin, so what must be wetted is the scalp, not every strand.

**The disagreement is about the length that hangs down.** Ibn Qudama sets out the
two views. The stricter one relies on a hadith — **under every hair is janaba, so
wet the hair and clean the skin** [3] — but he then weakens it himself: **as for
the hadith "wet the hair", only al-Harith ibn Wajih narrates it, alone, and he is
weak in hadith** [4]. The lenient view relies on Umm Salama's report, and notes
that three handfuls would not soak tightly bound hair in any case.

**So the practical test is the scalp, not the plait.** Work water in at the
partings until you can feel your scalp is wet all over. If your braids let water
through to the roots, you are done. If they are so dense that water cannot get to
the scalp — some protective styles, tight cornrows worn for weeks, glued or bonded
extensions — then the ruling that decides it is a different one.

**The thing that genuinely blocks a ghusl.** Ibn Nujaym: **if a woman has stuck
her head down with a fragranced preparation such that the water does not reach
the roots of the hair, she must remove it.** [5] That is the same principle as
nail polish — a sealing layer is a barrier, and there is a separate answer here
on barriers.

| | |
|---|---|
| Braids, plaits, twists, a bun | water reaches the scalp → **leave them in** |
| Clip-in or removable extensions | take out if they stop water at the scalp |
| Bonded, glued or sewn-in extensions, or a set style | reach the scalp at the partings; do not destroy the style if you can wet the roots |
| Wax, gel, oil-sealed or product-sealed scalp | **must come off** — this is a barrier |

**One real difference between the two baths.** Ibn Qudama: **the bath for
menstruation is like the bath for janaba, except in undoing the hair** [6]. Some
of the fuqaha require the hair to be loosened for the post-menstrual bath
specifically, where they do not for janaba. If you follow a school, follow it; if
you do not, the menstrual bath is a once-a-month event where undoing the hair is
usually possible anyway, and doing so puts you outside every disagreement.

**On hardship.** The women asking this today have the same problem Umm Salama
had, and the answer she was given was the lenient one. Braided styles exist partly
because undoing and redoing them is hours of work. That is precisely the kind of
difficulty the ruling was issued into — not a modern complication the law never
anticipated.

The requirement itself never changes: **and if ye are unclean, purify yourselves**
[7]. Water on all of you, hair roots included. How you get it there is yours to
arrange.
