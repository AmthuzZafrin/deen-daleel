# What are my parents' rights over me?

## Answer

Ibn al-'Arabi opens his treatment with a sentence that sets the level: "Kindness to parents is one of the pillars of the religion among the obligatory duties, and kindness to them is in words and in deeds" — in words, as in *say not 'fie' unto them nor repel them*; and the reason he gives is that they hold both the general claim of kinship and a particular one, "since you are a part of him, and he is the origin that brought you into being, and the one who sustained you in your weakness and your incapacity for yourself." [1] Al-Nawawi reports the agreement plainly: the scholars are unanimous that kindness to parents is commanded and that *'uquq* — cutting them off, treating them with contempt — "is forbidden and among the major sins" [2].

**The best-known statement of the ranking is a single hadith.** A man asked who had the most right to his good company: "Your mother, then your mother, then your mother, then your father, then the nearest to you, then the nearest to you." [3] Al-Nawawi gives the reason the scholars drew from it: "the cause of giving the mother precedence is the abundance of her toil over him, her tenderness, her service, and her endurance of hardships in carrying him, then delivering him, then nursing him, then raising him, serving him and nursing him through illness" [4].

There is an old and rather charming disagreement over the arithmetic of that, and it is worth showing because it is a case where a reader can watch scholars refuse to overclaim. Al-Qurtubi takes the hadith to give the mother three-quarters and the father a quarter, and reports al-Muhasibi's claim that there is no disagreement about that. Ibn Hajar, reporting the same material, gives Ibn Battal's reading — "its implication is that the mother has three times what the father has of kindness, and that was because of the difficulty of pregnancy, then delivery, then nursing; these she has alone and suffers in them, then she shares with the father in the raising" [5] — and then flatly refuses the claimed consensus: "but al-Harith al-Muhasibi transmitted consensus on giving the mother precedence in kindness, and in that there is something to look into" [6]. He then produces the counter-evidence: Malik, asked about a father summoning a son whom his mother forbids to go, answered "obey your father and do not disobey your mother", while al-Layth, asked the identical question, said "obey your mother, for she has two-thirds of the kindness" [7]. So the ranking is certain; the fractions are not.

**The right is heaviest exactly when it is least convenient**, which is old age. On the hadith "the nose is rubbed in dust of one who reaches his parents in old age, one or both of them, and does not enter Paradise" [8], al-Nawawi comments: "it means that kindness to them in their old age and their weakness, by service or by maintenance or otherwise, is a cause of entering Paradise; so whoever falls short in that has missed entering Paradise" [9]. The right also outlives them — "the finest of kindness is a child's keeping ties with the people his father loved" [10] — and Ibn al-'Arabi notes that among the father's claims are that he may take back a gift given to his child and may eat from his child's property, "for the Prophet said: the best of what a man eats is from his own earning, and his child is from his earning" [11].

**Now the limit, which is as firmly stated as the right.** Al-Jassas states it in one line: "obedience to parents is obligatory in what is right, not in disobedience to God, for there is no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator" [12]. That is not a loophole around the duty; it is a boundary written into the duty from the beginning, and everything below it stands.

Inside that boundary, the scope of what parents may forbid is not unlimited either, and the fiqh works are surprisingly precise about it. The Prophet turned back a man who had emigrated from Yemen to join him: "Go back to them and ask their permission; if they permit you, then fight, and if not, then be dutiful to them" [13] — and on that basis, al-Jassas says, obligatory military service required their leave. But he immediately draws the line: "as for going out for trade and the like, in which there is no fighting, there is no harm in it without their permission ... because the Prophet only prevented him from *jihad* without the parents' permission when someone else had discharged the obligation, on account of its exposure to being killed and the parents' grief at it; as for trades and dealing in permitted things, in which there is no exposure to being killed, the parents have no right to prevent him from them" [14]. The reasoning is worth reading twice, because it identifies *what* the parents' right is protecting — their grief at losing a child — rather than treating it as a general veto over an adult's life.

**Two harder cases sit at the edges, and the sources genuinely disagree.** The first is a father telling a son to divorce his wife. Al-Qurtubi cites the report of Ibn 'Umar: "I had a wife whom I loved, and my father disliked her, so he ordered me to divorce her and I refused. I mentioned that to the Prophet, and he said: Abdullah b. 'Umar, divorce your wife" [15]. That report is regularly produced as though it settles the matter for everyone. It is on the sheet, it is graded *hasan sahih* there, and readers should know it exists — but it is one incident involving one Companion and one father, and this sheet gives no jurist generalising it into a rule. Anyone told to act on it should ask a scholar what weight it actually carries.

The second is starker. Al-Jassas reports the Hanafi position that a Muslim should not kill his disbelieving father even in battle, reasoning from *keep them company in the world kindly*, and citing the Prophet forbidding Hanzala to kill his own father. Ibn al-'Arabi takes the other side, holding that it is the son's own honour to undertake it, and ends: "kinship has a claim, but when the claim of God arrived, the claim of kinship lapsed" [16]. This is battlefield law from a world that no longer exists, and neither position transfers to anything a reader today will face. It is here only because it shows the outer bound the jurists were arguing about — and even at that bound, they were arguing.

**None of this is conditional on the parents being Muslim.** Al-Qurtubi states it as a separate ruling: "kindness to parents is not restricted to their being Muslims; rather, if they are disbelievers he is kind to them and treats them well" [17], citing Asma' asking about her mother who had come to her while still an idolater — "my mother has come to me and she is desirous; shall I keep ties with her?" and the answer, "Yes, keep ties with your mother" [18]. Al-Jassas extends it past death: the Hanafis held "concerning a Muslim whose parents die as disbelievers, that he washes them, follows them and buries them, because that is part of the keeping company in kindness that God commanded" [19].

**One last practical note, because it is where this duty most often collides with something else.** Al-Nawawi, on the same hadith about permission for military service, records that the requirement of permission applies "if they are both Muslim, or by the permission of whichever of them is Muslim; if they are both idolaters, their permission is not a condition according to al-Shafi'i and those who agreed with him, though al-Thawri made it a condition" [20]. Note what that disagreement is not about: nobody there suggests the duty of kindness lapses. What varies is only whether their *veto* over one specific obligation holds. The kindness is not in dispute anywhere on this sheet.

So: a very large, unanimously affirmed, lifelong obligation of care, respect, service and maintenance, weighted toward the mother, heaviest in their old age, continuing after their death, owed regardless of their religion — bounded at exactly one place, which is a command to disobey God. Almost every real dispute a person has with their parents falls inside that boundary rather than on it, and the sources here treat what falls inside as owed.
