# Can I wear makeup outside?

## Answer

The sources give a criterion rather than a rule about cosmetics, and the
criterion is narrower than "no makeup". What they prohibit is *tabarruj* — and
the classical definitions of that word are worth reading, because most of them
are not about your face at all.

**The Hanafi statement of the rule is the most direct thing here.** On a woman
going out: "going out is only permitted on condition of the absence of adornment,
and of any change of appearance into what would draw men's gaze and attraction"
[1]. Two conditions, and the second explains the first: the concern is what
*invites the look*, not decoration as such.

**What *tabarruj* meant to the early commentators.** They give four readings,
and only one touches cosmetics. Mujahid: "a woman used to walk out among the
men — that is the *tabarruj* of the Jahiliyya" [2]. Qatada: "meaning when you go
out of your houses — they had a gait, a swaying and a coquetry, and Allah
forbade them that" [3]. Muqatil b. Hayyan gives a dress definition: "*tabarruj*
is that she throws the khimar over her head and does not fasten it, so that it
would conceal her necklaces, her earrings and her neck — and all of that shows
from her; that is *tabarruj*. Then it was extended to all the believing women"
[4]. And the fourth, which is the one that covers cosmetics: "and it is said it
is the display of one's good looks to men" [5].

Al-Jassas notes the verse was addressed to the Prophet's ﷺ wives but binds
beyond them: "all these matters are among what Allah disciplined the wives of
the Prophet ﷺ with, as a protection for them — and the rest of the believing
women are intended by them" [6].

Note what falls out of this. Three of the four definitions are about
*conduct and dress* — where you go, how you walk, whether the scarf is fastened.
The prohibition is on presenting yourself for attention, and cosmetics are one
possible instrument of that rather than the thing itself.

**The one place the sources reason about cosmetics directly.** Ibn Qudama, in
the chapter on the widow in mourning, explains why kohl is prohibited to her:
"because kohl is among the most effective of adornments, and adornment invites
towards it and stirs desire — so it is like perfume, and stronger than it" [7].
The context is mourning, not ordinary life, so the ruling does not transfer. But
the *distinction* he then draws does, and it is the most useful thing in this
file: "the prohibition was only of kohl with *ithmid*, because that is what
adornment is achieved by; as for kohl with *tutiya* and *'anzarut* and the like,
there is no harm in it, because there is no adornment in it — rather it makes
the eye look worse and increases its dullness" [8].

The same substance, applied to the same place, is prohibited or permitted
according to whether it beautifies. That is a functional test, not a
prohibition on a product. He allows even the prohibited kind under need: "if the
mourning woman is compelled to kohl with *ithmid* for treatment, she may apply
it at night and wipe it off by day" [9].

**Where this answer has to be careful.** No text on this file addresses modern
cosmetics as a category, and nothing here distinguishes concealer from
foundation from a full made-up face. What the sources supply is the test — does
this change your appearance into something that draws men's attention — and they
leave you to apply it, in a way that plainly depends on what is ordinary where
you live. In a place where a little colour is unremarkable, it does not draw the
look; in a place where it is not, the same thing might.

**So, practically.** Makeup worn to look presentable, at a level that passes
without notice in your setting, is not the *tabarruj* the verse names — none of
the four classical definitions reaches it. Makeup worn so as to be looked at, in
front of men who are not mahram, is exactly what the verse names, on the fourth
definition and on the Hanafi condition. Between those two is a judgement about
your own intention and your own society, and the sources hand it to you
deliberately.

At home, and among women and mahrams, none of this applies at all: the entire
discussion is about what is presented to men outside that circle.
