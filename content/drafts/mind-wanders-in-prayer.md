# My mind wanders constantly in prayer — does it still count?

## Answer

**Yes, it still counts. A wandering mind does not invalidate a prayer, and it is
not on any school's list of things that do.** What it costs you is some of the
reward, not the prayer.

That is the ruling, and it should settle the anxiety before anything else. Now the
more useful part: what *khushu'* actually is, and what you can do about it.

**Khushu' is stillness and attention, not emotion.** The early definitions are
strikingly concrete. Ibn Taymiyya gathers them: Qatada said **khushu' is in the
heart, and fear, and lowering the gaze in prayer** [1]; Ibn Sirin reported that
**the Prophet used to raise his gaze to the sky, and then he was commanded with
khushu', so he cast his gaze towards the place of his prostration** [2]; and the
plainest of all — **it is a person's stillness in his prayer** [3].

Not a feeling. A gaze, a posture, a quietness. Those you can actually control.

**Al-Nawawi's practical list** is the best thing on this page, because every item
is an action rather than a state to summon: **it is recommended that he enter the
prayer with energy and with turning towards it, and that he ponder the recitation
and the remembrances and recite them slowly and distinctly — and likewise the
du'a** [4].

Read that as instructions:

1. **Enter with energy.** Do not begin exhausted or mid-thought if you can help it.
2. **Slow down.** Rushing is the single largest cause of a wandering mind. Recite
   deliberately, and settle in each position.
3. **Understand what you are saying.** This is the highest-leverage change of all.
   Learn what al-Fatiha means, then the tashahhud, then the short suras. A mind
   wanders from sounds; it holds onto meaning.
4. **Fix your gaze** at the place of your prostration [2].
5. **Vary the suras** you recite, so the words do not become automatic.

**And the reward is attached to the attention, which is why this matters at all.**
Uqba ibn Amir reported the Prophet saying: **there is no Muslim who performs wudu
and does it well, then stands and prays two rak'ahs, turning towards them with his
heart and his face, but that Paradise becomes obligatory for him.** [5]

Note *turning towards them* — the effort, not a guaranteed emotional state.

**And the Prophet's own correction on this** was about fidgeting, not feeling:
**what is it with you, raising your hands as though they were the tails of restive
horses? Be still in the prayer.** [6]

**Three things worth knowing**

*Everyone's mind wanders.* This is one of the most universally reported
difficulties in the literature, and there is a well-known hadith about the Shaytan
coming between a person and their prayer to make them forget. It is a described,
expected condition, not a personal failure.

*Do not repeat the prayer.* Repeating a prayer because you were not present in it
is not a remedy the fiqh offers, and it very quickly becomes its own problem —
there is a separate answer here on that.

*The wandering itself is not sin.* You are answerable for what you choose, and you
did not choose the thought. Bringing yourself back — over and over, in the same
prayer — is the act being rewarded.

**And a note for reverts.** In a language you are still learning, of course your
attention drifts. It gets better as the words become meaningful rather than
phonetic, which happens faster than you expect. Meanwhile, use the du'a in sujud
and after the tashahhud — **in your own language** — where you can say what you
actually mean. That is where many people first find the prayer becomes theirs.
