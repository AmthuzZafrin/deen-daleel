# Can I talk during the Friday sermon?

## Answer

**No. Stay silent while the imam is giving the khutba — silent enough that even
telling someone else to be quiet is out.**

**The hadith, and how sharp it is.**

Abu Hurayra reports: **when you say to your companion "be quiet" on Friday while
the imam is delivering the khutba, you have spoken idly** [1]. That is in Muslim.

Al-Nawawi draws out why the wording is chosen so carefully: telling someone to be
quiet is itself enjoining good — and it is still called *laghw*, idle speech. **So
in the hadith there is a prohibition of every kind of speech during the khutba**
[2]. Ibn Abidin makes the same argument in one line: **if enjoining good is
forbidden — and it is an obligation — then what do you suppose about the
voluntary?** [3]

**So how do you tell someone to stop talking?**

By not talking. Al-Nawawi: **the way, if he wants to stop another from speaking,
is that he gesture to him to be silent** [4]. Ibn Qudama says the same and quotes
the hadith for it: **if a person hears someone speaking, he should not stop him
by speech** [5] — Ahmad's own recommendation being to put a finger to your lips.

**Forbidden, or just disliked? The schools differ.**

This is a real disagreement and it is worth knowing which side you are standing
on. Al-Nawawi: **the scholars differed as to whether speech is forbidden or
disliked in the sense of preferability — and these are two positions of
al-Shafi'i** [6]. In al-Majmu' he states his school's settled view: **the correct
position according to our companions is that speech is not forbidden** [7], and
names the other side: **Malik, al-Awza'i, Abu Hanifa, and Ahmad said it is
forbidden** [8].

He also reports the point everyone agrees on regardless: **Malik, Abu Hanifa,
al-Shafi'i, and the generality of scholars said listening silently to the khutba
is obligatory** [9]. The dispute is over the grade of the speech, not over
whether you should be quiet.

**The exception, and it is a real one.**

Ibn Qudama: **as for necessary speech — such as warning a blind man away from a
well, or someone in danger from a fire, or a snake, or a blaze, and the like — he
may do it** [10]. His reasoning is that this is permitted even inside the prayer
itself, where speech invalidates, so it is more clearly permitted during a
khutba.

**In practice.** Phone off before the khutba starts, not silenced. No greetings,
no whispered questions, no answering someone who greets you. If a child needs
managing, do it by hand and eye. If someone is about to walk into actual danger,
speak.
