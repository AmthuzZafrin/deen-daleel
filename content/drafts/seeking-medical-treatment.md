# Does Islam require me to seek medical treatment?

## Answer

**Yes — and the instruction is affirmative, not merely permissive.** Bedouins asked whether they should treat illness and were told: `تداووا، فإن الله عز وجل لم يضع داء إلا وضع له دواء غير داء واحد الهرم` [1] — treat yourselves, for God has not put down a disease without putting down a remedy for it, except one disease: old age. Another wording: `تداووا عباد الله فإن الله لم ينزل داء إلا وقد أنزل له دواء وقال مرة شفاء إلا الهرم قالوا فما خير ما أعطى الرجل يا رسول الله قال خلق حسن` [2] — servants of God, treat yourselves... They then asked what the best thing a man is given is, and were told: good character. And: `لكل داء دواء فإذا أصيب دواء الداء برئ بإذن الله` [3] — for every disease there is a remedy; when the remedy for the disease is hit upon, he recovers by God's permission.

**The "isn't that fatalism?" objection is not new, and the sources answer it head on.** Al-Nawawi: `وفى هذا الحديث إشارة إلى استحباب الدواء وهو مذهب أصحابنا وجمهور السلف وعامة الخلف` [4] — this indicates that medicine is recommended, which is the position of our school, of the great majority of the early generations, and of scholars generally. And then the argument itself: `وفيها رد على من أنكر التداوي من غلاة الصوفية وقال كل شئ بقضاء وقدر فلا حاجة إلى التداوي وحجة العلماء هذه الأحاديث ويعتقدون أن الله تعالى هو الفاعل وأن التداوي هو أيضا من قدر الله` [5] — a refutation of those who rejected treatment, the extremists among the Sufis, who said everything is by decree and destiny so there is no need to treat; the scholars' proof is these hadiths, and they hold that God is the Doer and **that treatment is itself part of God's decree**.

That is the whole answer to the objection. Taking the medicine is not competing with the decree; it is inside it, in the same way that eating is.

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr is sharper still: `فمن زعم أنه لا معنى للرقى والاستعاذة ومنع من التداوي والمعالجة ونحو ذلك مما يلتمس به العافية من الله فقد خرج من عرف المسلمين وخالف طريقهم قالوا ولو كان الأمر كما ذهب إليه من كره التداوي والرقي ما قطع الناس أيديهم وأرجلهم وغير ذلك من أعضائهم للعلاج وما افتصدوا ولا احتجموا وهذا عروة بن الزبير قد قطع ساقه` [6] — whoever claims there is no point in seeking healing from God by these means, and forbids treatment, has left what the Muslims recognise and departed from their way. Had it been as those who disliked treatment held, people would not have had hands and legs and other limbs amputated for treatment, nor been bled, nor cupped — and here is 'Urwa b. al-Zubayr, who had his leg cut off.

**Trust in God is located in the heart, not in the absence of means.** Ibn Hajar: `فلا يطمئن إلى الأسباب بقلبه بل يعتقد انها لا تجلب بذاتها نفعا ولا تدفع ضرا بل السبب والمسبب فعل الله تعالى` [7] — he does not rest his heart on the means; he holds that they neither bring benefit nor avert harm of themselves, and that the cause and the caused are both God's doing. And al-Qushayri: `وقال أبو القاسم القشيري التوكل محله القلب وأما الحركة الظاهرة فلا تنافيه` [8] — *tawakkul* is seated in the heart; outward activity does not contradict it.

Al-Nawawi puts medicine in the same category as eating: `وأن الطب غير قادح في التوكل إذ تطبب رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم والفضلاء من السلف وكل سبب مقطوع به كالأكل والشرب للغذاء والري لا يقدح في التوكل` [9] — medicine does not impair reliance on God, since the Prophet was treated and so were the best of the early generations; and any means whose effect is certain — like eating and drinking for nourishment and thirst — does not impair it. Al-Tabari's summary, in Ibn Hajar: `والحق أن من وثق بالله وأيقن أن قضاءه عليه ماض لم يقدح في توكله تعاطيه` [10] — whoever trusts God and is certain His decree upon him will pass, his use of means does not impair his reliance.

**Is it obligatory, though? Here the sources genuinely differ, and the range is worth knowing.** Al-Khattabi's position, reported by Ibn Hajar: `لكن مقام الرضا والتسليم أعلى من تعاطي الأسباب وإلى هذا نحا الخطابي ومن تبعه` [11] — the station of contentment and submission is higher than resorting to means.

Ibn Taymiyya argues that treatment is not obligatory at all, and gives two reasons. First: `أن كثيرا من المرضى أو أكثر المرضى يشفون بلا تداو لا سيما في أهل الوبر والقرى والساكنين في نواحي الأرض يشفيهم الله بما خلق فيهم من القوى المطبوعة في أبدانهم الرافعة للمرض وفيما ييسره لهم من نوع حركة وعمل أو دعوة مستجابة أو رقية نافعة أو قوة للقلب وحسن التوكل إلى غير ذلك من الأسباب الكثيرة غير الدواء وأما الأكل فهو ضروري ولم يجعل الله أبدان الحيوان تقوم إلا بالغذاء فلو لم يكن يأك` [12] — most of the sick recover without treatment, God healing them by the powers built into their bodies and by other means besides medicine; whereas food is a genuine necessity, since bodies do not subsist without it. Second: `وثانيها أن الأكل عند الضرورة واجب قال مسروق من اضطر إلى الميتة فلم يأكل فمات دخل النار والتداوي غير واجب ومن نازع فيه خصمته السنة في المرأة السوداء التي خيرها النبي بين الصبر على البلاء ودخول الجنة` [13] — eating in dire need is obligatory (Masruq: one compelled to eat carrion who refuses and dies has entered the Fire), but treatment is not; and whoever disputes it is answered by the case of the woman who was given the choice between enduring her affliction with the Garden as its price, and being prayed for.

So: **recommended in the majority view; permitted-and-not-required in Ibn Taymiyya's.** Nobody in this corpus holds it blameworthy, and nobody holds that seeking treatment reflects weak faith.

Note carefully what the minority position is and is not. It is a claim about what a competent adult may choose *for themselves* out of genuine contentment. It is not a warrant for refusing a child's medical care, not a reason to stop someone else's medication, and not something to say to a person who is ill. The same author who wrote it treats deliberately eating forbidden food to save your own life as obligatory.

**Visiting the sick is a separate duty, and it is owed widely.** `أطعموا الجائع، وعودوا المريض، وفكوا العاني` [14] — feed the hungry, visit the sick, free the captive. Al-Nawawi: `ان يعم بعيادته الصديق والعدو ومن يعرفه ومن لا يعرفه لعموم الأحاديث` [15] — he should extend his visiting to friend and enemy, to those he knows and those he does not, on the generality of the hadiths. On a non-Muslim: `والصواب عندي ان عيادة الكافر جائزة والقربة فيها موقوفة على نوع حرمة يقترن بها من جوار أو قرابة` [16] — the correct view is that visiting is permitted, and its being an act of merit depends on some claim attaching to it, such as neighbourhood or kinship. (The precedent is the Prophet visiting a sick Jewish boy who served him.)

And a piece of etiquette that people get wrong in the other direction: `ينبغي أن تكون العيادة غبا لا يواصلها كل يوم الا أن يكون مغلوبا قلت هذا لآحاد الناس أما أقارب المريض وأصدقاؤه` [17] — visiting should be intermittent, not every single day, unless the person is overcome by illness; and al-Nawawi adds that this is for ordinary acquaintances, while relatives and close friends are another matter. Ill people need rest more than they need a queue.

**One limit.** `إن الله عز وجل خلق الداء وخلق الدواء فتداووا ولا تداووا بحرام` [18] — God created the disease and created the remedy, so treat yourselves, and do not treat yourselves with what is forbidden. What counts as forbidden here, and how necessity affects it, are separate questions with their own material.

**What this does not settle.** Which treatments; end-of-life decisions and the withdrawal of treatment, which these works do not reach; mental-health treatment specifically; and the boundary of a legal *obligation* to accept treatment where refusal would kill you, on which the corpus gives you the argument but not a settled ruling.
