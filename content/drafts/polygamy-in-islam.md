# Why does Islam allow more than one wife?

## Answer

It permits it; it does not recommend it, and the verse that permits it spends most of its words
restricting it.

**"And if ye fear that ye will not deal fairly by the orphans, marry of the women, who seem good to you,
two or three or four; and if ye fear that ye cannot do justice (to so many) then one (only)"** [1].

### The verse is a restriction, not an invitation

This is the reading al-Tabari gives, and it is worth setting out because it inverts how the verse is
usually quoted. He asks whether "marry" here is a command at all, and answers no: **"it carries the sense
of indicating the prohibition of marrying such a number of women as the one marrying fears injustice in —
not the sense of a command to marry"** [2].

The structure, on his reading: **"marry of the women what is good to you — either two, if you are secure
from injustice in yourselves regarding what is owed to them; or three, if you do not fear that; or four,
if you are secure of that regarding them"** [3]. Each step is conditional on the step before.

Ibn al-'Arabi reads it as a descending scale: **"you may marry four; and if you cannot be just, then
three; and if you cannot be just, then two; and if you cannot be just, then one"** [4]. He is also blunt
about the arithmetic some people attempt: **"a group of the ignorant have imagined that this verse permits
a man nine wives"** [5]. The limit is four, and it is settled — **"the marriage of four is lawful, no
more"** [6], on which **"the four imams and the majority of Muslims are agreed"** [7]. When Ghaylan
al-Thaqafi became Muslim with ten wives, **"the Prophet ﷺ said to Ghaylan al-Thaqafi, when he became
Muslim and had ten wives under him: choose four of them and part from the rest"** [8].

That episode is the whole history in miniature. Islam did not introduce plural marriage. It found it
unlimited and capped it.

### The condition, and what it does and does not require

**"Ye will not be able to deal equally between (your) wives, however much ye wish (to do so). But turn
not altogether away (from one), leaving her as in suspense"** [9].

Al-Qurtubi explains what is being conceded and what is being demanded. The impossibility is about the
heart: **"God informed us of the negation of ability regarding justice between women, and that is in the
natural inclination of love, intimacy, and the heart's share"** [10]. What remains obligatory is
everything measurable — Mujahid: **"do not deliberately do wrong; rather, hold to equality in the
division of nights and in maintenance, because this is within one's power"** [11].

The Prophet ﷺ divided his time and then prayed: **"O God, this is my division in what I control, so do
not blame me for what I do not control"** [12]. That is the model: exact equality in what can be
measured, and an admission before God about what cannot.

Ibn Qudama confirms the boundary: **"we know of no disagreement among the people of knowledge that
equality between wives in intercourse is not obligatory"** [13] — because desire cannot be commanded —
**"but if equality between them in it is possible, it is better and more fitting, for it is more complete
in justice"** [14].

Failure carries a warning. **"Whoever has two wives and does not deal justly between them will come on
the Day of Resurrection with one of his sides sagging"** [15]. And the harm the verse names is being left
**"neither divorced nor with a husband"** [16] — a woman kept nominally married and actually abandoned.

### Practical constraints the books already impose

The mechanics assume equal treatment, not a hierarchy. A newly married wife has her own entitlement
before the rotation resumes: **"if she is a virgin, he owes her seven nights with their days without
making them up; and if she was previously married, she has the choice — if she wishes, seven"** [17].

And the Hanafi commentator names the obvious: plural marriage involves **"severe hardship, because of the
obligation of justice between them"** [18]. The classical jurists did not treat it as a privilege lightly
taken.

### So why is it permitted at all?

The classical sources give the permission without a lengthy justification, so the honest answer is that
they treat it as a licence bounded by capacity rather than a doctrine to be defended. What the texts
themselves emphasise is the framing: the verse arises in the context of orphans and their guardians [1],
it caps an unlimited practice [8], it makes each additional marriage conditional on justice [3], and it
closes by telling men they will not manage it [9].

Two things follow, and they are what the sources support rather than what the argument usually supplies.
A man who cannot meet [11] — equal nights, equal maintenance, no woman left in suspense — is not
permitted a second marriage by this verse; he is forbidden it by it. And a first wife's distress is not
evidence that she has misunderstood her religion; the verse itself concedes that the arrangement is hard
and that hearts do not divide.

Whether it is wise in a given case is not a fiqh question. Whether it is lawful is, and the answer is:
within four, on the conditions above, and not otherwise.
