# Are amulets and taweez allowed?

## Answer

This is a genuine disagreement, and you should know that before you know anything else. Amulets of the pre-Islamic kind are forbidden by consensus. Whether a paper bearing Qur'an, worn or hung, falls under that prohibition is disputed — al-Bayhaqi and al-Nawawi transmit a permission for it; Ibn Mas'ud's own conduct and the plain wording of the hadith pull the other way. Neither side is fringe.

**What is forbidden, and why.**

Ibn Mas'ud reported the Prophet ﷺ saying: "Ruqyas, amulets and love-charms are shirk" [1].

Ibn Hajar defines the terms. *Tama'im* are "the plural of *tamima*, and it is beads or a necklace hung on the head; in the Jahiliyya they believed that it repelled calamities" [2]. *Tiwala* is "a thing by which a woman would draw her husband's love, and it is a kind of sorcery" [3].

And he gives the reason, which is the part that decides every modern case: "that was only shirk because they intended the repelling of harms and the drawing of benefits *from other than Allah*" [4]. The wrong is not the string or the object. It is the direction the heart faces when the object is put on.

Ibn Taymiyya extends the same test to what is recited: "likewise the foreign-language ruqyas and incantations — they contain names of men from among the jinn who are called upon and sought help from, and are sworn by upon those they venerate, so the devils obey them on account of that in some affairs; and this is of the genus of sorcery and shirk" [5]. So a *ta'wiz* whose contents you have never seen, or which contains names, numbers, squares and symbols rather than Qur'an, is not a borderline case at all.

**The hadith's own scene is instructive.**

When Ibn Mas'ud said this, his wife Zaynab objected from experience: "She said: I said, why do you say this? By Allah, my eye used to discharge, and I would go to so-and-so the Jew who performed ruqya on me, and when he performed it, it settled. 'Abdullah said: That was only the work of Shaytan — he was prodding it with his hand, and when he performed the ruqya he withheld from it" [6]. And he gave her the replacement: "it would have been enough for you to say as the Messenger of Allah ﷺ used to say: *Remove the harm, Lord of mankind; heal, You are the Healer, there is no healing but Your healing, a healing that leaves no illness behind*" [7].

Two things worth taking from that. First, "it worked" was not accepted as an argument — the relief was real and the explanation was still wrong. Second, he did not leave her with nothing. He replaced the amulet with words. That is the shape of the whole answer.

**Where the disagreement is.**

Both sides agree the Jahili charm is out. The question is the paper with Qur'an on it.

*The permitting position.* Al-Nawawi transmits from al-Bayhaqi: the prohibition and dislike "may be regarding one who hangs it while seeing complete wellbeing and the removal of the illness as coming *by it*, as the Jahiliyya were upon. But as for one who hangs it seeking blessing through the remembrance of Allah in it, knowing that none removes it but Allah and none repels it but He — there is no harm in it, if Allah wills" [8]. Abu 'Ubayd read the prohibition in the same restricted way: "what is meant by the prohibition is what is in other than the Arabic tongue, of which it is not known what it is" [9]. And 'A'isha is reported to have drawn the line by timing: "the *tama'im* are what is hung *before* the descent of the affliction; what is hung after the descent of the affliction is not a *tamima*. Al-Bayhaqi said: this narration is the more authentic" [10].

*The restricting position.* Ibn Hajar does carve out an exception — "what is by Allah's names and His speech does not enter into that, for the use of it before its occurrence is established in the hadiths" [11] — but read what he cites in support: the Prophet ﷺ blowing the *mu'awwidhat* over himself at bedtime, and "the hadith of Ibn 'Abbas that he ﷺ used to seek refuge for al-Hasan and al-Husayn with the perfect words of Allah" [12]. Both are *recitation*, not suspension. His exception establishes that Qur'an may be used preventively; it does not by itself establish that Qur'an may be hung. And the hadith of 'Uqba b. 'Amir is unqualified: "whoever hangs a *tamima*, may Allah not complete for him; and whoever hangs a cowrie shell, may Allah not leave him in ease" [13].

**How to act while the scholars differ.**

Nothing in either position makes the amulet *necessary*. Everything either position permits is available in a better form: recite it. That asymmetry is the practical resolution — the disputed act is optional, and the undisputed alternative is superior. Abu Bakr's instruction when he found a Jewish woman performing ruqya over the ailing 'A'isha was simply: "Perform ruqya over her with the Book of Allah" [14].

So: if you are wearing a *ta'wiz* whose contents you cannot read, remove it — that is not in dispute on either side. If it is verifiably Qur'an and you hold that Allah alone heals, you are following a transmitted permission and no one should call you a mushrik for it; but you are also doing the harder version of an easier thing, and you would be on ground no one contests if you recited instead.

**The related worry: does using remedies at all diminish tawakkul?**

The hadith of the seventy thousand who enter Paradise without reckoning describes them as those who "do not cauterise, do not seek ruqya, and rely upon their Lord." Al-Khattabi and others held "that what is meant is those who leave these out of reliance upon Allah and contentment with His decree and His trial — al-Khattabi said: and this is among the highest ranks of those firmly grounded in faith" [15]. Al-Dawudi narrowed it further: "what is meant by the hadith is what they do while in health — for it is disliked for one who has no illness to take up amulets and use ruqyas" [16].

Ibn Hajar keeps the two apart cleanly: "otherwise, ruqya in itself is not forbidden; what was forbidden of it is only what was shirk or bore the possibility of it" [17] — and those who abstained did so "to cut off the material cause, because one who does it is not safe from entrusting himself to it" [18].

That last phrase is the whole of it. *Entrusting himself to it* — that is the danger the amulet poses, and it is a danger of the heart, which is why no amount of Qur'anic content on the paper settles the question by itself. Whatever you hang, you can still be leaning on it. Whatever you recite, you are addressing Allah directly.
