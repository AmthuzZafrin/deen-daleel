# Is black magic real, and how do I know if I'm affected?

## Answer

Two questions, and the sources answer them very unevenly. Whether *sihr* is real they answer at length and with near-unanimity: yes. How you would know you are affected they do not answer at all — and that gap is the most important thing on this page, because almost all the harm done in this area today happens in it.

**Is it real?**

Al-Qurtubi states the division: "Ahl al-Sunna held that sorcery is established and has a reality; the general body of the Mu'tazila, and Abu Ishaq al-Istarabadhi of the companions of al-Shafi'i, held that sorcery has no reality — it is only deception, illusion and the making of a thing seem other than it is, and that it is a species of sleight and conjuring" [1].

The majority argument is short. First, the Qur'an describes sorcery being *taught*: "if it had no reality, it could not be taught, nor would Allah have informed us that they teach it to people — so this indicates that it has a reality" [2]. Second, the Prophet ﷺ was himself affected, and when it was undone he said "Allah has cured me" — and "cure is only by the lifting of an ailment and the removal of an illness, which indicates that it has a truth and a reality" [3].

Ibn Qudama records the same split inside the Shafi'i school and the Hanafi qualification, then sides with reality on the strength of Surat al-Falaq: the command to seek refuge "from the evil of the women who blow on knots" would be empty otherwise [4].

**What the word covers.**

Part of the confusion is that *sihr* in Arabic is much wider than "black magic." Ibn Hajar, following al-Raghib, lists the senses: first, "what is subtle and fine — *saharta al-sabi*, you charmed the child, you drew him to you; and everyone who draws something to himself has enchanted it" [5]; second, "what occurs by deception and illusions that have no reality, like what the conjuror does in diverting eyes from what he handles by the quickness of his hand" [6]; third, "what is obtained by the assistance of devils, through a kind of drawing near to them" [7].

Only the third is the thing people mean. The first is a metaphor — Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, on "some eloquence is sorcery," says the hadith "indicates praise of clear speech and the merit of eloquence… and in it is metaphor and fine simile, because clear speech is not sorcery in reality" [8]. The second is stage magic.

Ibn Qudama defines the third: "sorcery: it is knots, incantations, and speech uttered or written, or something worked upon the body of the bewitched, or his heart, or his mind, without direct contact with him" [9] — and its effects: "it has a reality: some of it kills, and some sickens, and it takes a man from his wife so that it prevents him from her; and some of it separates between a man and his wife, and makes one of them hateful to the other, or makes two people love each other" [10].

**The Prophet ﷺ was affected — and that matters pastorally.**

The hadith of 'A'isha, which Ibn Qudama cites from al-Bukhari: he ﷺ said, "Do you know that Allah has given me a ruling on what I asked Him about? Two angels came to me; one sat at my head and the other at my feet. One said: What ails the man? He said: He is bewitched. He said: Who bewitched him? He said: Labid b. al-A'sam — in a comb and combings, in the spathe of a male palm, in the well of Dharwan" [11]. Ibn Qudama's comment: "so he affirmed sorcery for them" [12].

Take the consolation in that. Being affected by sihr is not evidence of weak faith, of sin, or of Allah's displeasure. It happened to the best of creation.

**What the Prophet ﷺ did next is the part usually skipped.**

He did not expose the man, did not publicise it, did not pursue him. Al-Nawawi explains why, and the reasoning is a general principle of the religion: bringing it out would have spread harm and evil among the Muslims through the recollection of sorcery, its being learnt, and its circulation — "this is of the category of abandoning a benefit for fear of a corruption greater than it, and it is among the most important foundational rules of Islam" [13].

A community that talks constantly about sihr, diagnoses it everywhere, and sustains an industry of practitioners around it has done the opposite of what he did.

**The ruling on practising it.**

Al-Nawawi, quoted by Ibn Hajar: "performing sorcery is forbidden, and it is among the major sins by consensus — the Prophet ﷺ counted it among the seven destroyers. Some of it is disbelief and some of it is not disbelief but a major sin; if it contains a statement or act that entails disbelief then it is disbelief, and otherwise not" [14]. Ibn Hajar applies that to his own typology: the *kufr* verdict "is clear in some of its types which I set out — namely devotional service to devils or to the planets; but as for the other type, which is of the category of conjuring, one who learns it does not disbelieve by it at all" [15]. Al-Jassas grounds the *kufr* reading in the verse itself: the two angels' warning "do not disbelieve" establishes "that it is disbelief when one acts by it and believes in it" [16].

On what a court should do with a convicted sorcerer, the schools differ. Ibn 'Abidin: "sorcery is forbidden with no disagreement among the people of knowledge, and believing it permitted is disbelief. From our companions, and Malik and Ahmad: the sorcerer disbelieves by learning and doing it, whether he believes it forbidden or not, and he is killed… And with al-Shafi'i: he is not killed and does not disbelieve unless he believes it permitted" [17]. Ibn 'Abidin's own preference is the narrower one: "the upshot is that he chose that he does not disbelieve unless he believes something that entails disbelief… and that he is killed absolutely if his practising it is known" [18]. He also records the mildest case: "a sorcerer who performs sorcery experimentally and does not believe in it does not disbelieve" [19].

That is a matter for courts and states, not for you. Nothing in it authorises a private person to act against anyone.

**Learning it, including to undo it.**

Ibn Hajar reports that "some scholars permitted learning sorcery for one of two purposes: either to distinguish what in it is disbelief from what is not, or to remove it from one it has befallen" [20]. But he shuts the second door where it counts: "as for the second, if it cannot be completed — as some claimed — except by a type of disbelief or grave sin, then it is not permissible at all" [21].

So sihr may not be undone by sihr. A practitioner who says he will use "the good kind," or names jinn, or asks for your mother's name, or requires an animal slaughtered without Allah's name, is offering you the forbidden thing under a new label.

Ibn 'Abidin's summary judgement is the cleanest line in this material: "sorcery in itself is real, a thing that exists — except that it is fit for nothing but evil and harm to creation; and the means to evil is evil, so it becomes blameworthy" [22].

**Now the hard part: how do you know if you are affected?**

Honestly: these sources do not tell you. They establish that sihr exists, define it, rule on it, and record that the Prophet ﷺ was afflicted and cured. They give no diagnostic criteria — no list of symptoms, no test, no procedure for determining that a particular illness or hardship in a particular person has this cause. Anyone who hands you such a checklist has produced it from somewhere other than this evidence.

That silence is worth respecting rather than filling. The symptoms usually attributed — fatigue, low mood, marital strain, bad luck, disturbed sleep, physical illness — are the ordinary furniture of human life, and each has ordinary causes that are treatable. Reading them as sorcery has a predictable cost: real medical and psychological conditions go untreated, real marital problems get externalised onto an invisible enemy, money goes to practitioners, and suspicion settles on relatives and neighbours.

What the evidence does license is straightforward and available to you now, with no intermediary. Recite — Surat al-Falaq and al-Nas were revealed in connection with exactly this, along with al-Fatiha, Ayat al-Kursi and the end of al-Baqara. Make du'a; the Prophet ﷺ asked Allah, and it was Allah who informed and cured him, and his own words on being relieved were "Allah has cured me" [3]. Keep your obligatory prayers and morning and evening adhkar. And see a doctor, because the fact that sihr is real does not make it the explanation for any particular thing that is wrong with you.

If a specific, credible reason points to sorcery, ruqya with Qur'an is legitimate and needs no specialist — you may perform it on yourself. What you should not do is enter the market that has grown up around this: it is largely populated by people selling either nothing or the forbidden thing, and both are paid the same.
