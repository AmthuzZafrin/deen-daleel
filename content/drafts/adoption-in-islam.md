# How does adoption work in Islam?

## Answer

The short version: **taking a child in and raising them is one of the most highly praised acts in the tradition. Changing whose child they are is what is forbidden.** English uses one word for both, which is why this question causes so much confusion.

**What was abolished, and how.** Ibn al-'Arabi tells the story that occasioned the ruling. Zayd b. Haritha was taken captive as a boy, ended up in Khadija's household, and was given to the Prophet, who "adopted him and raised him, and he was called after him according to the custom of the Arabs" [1]. Ibn 'Umar's testimony is that this was simply what everyone said: "we used not to call Zayd b. Haritha anything but Zayd b. Muhammad, until there was revealed: *call them by their fathers' names, that is more equitable in the sight of God*" [2].

The verses Ibn al-'Arabi then quotes in full are the whole of the ruling, and their last clauses matter as much as the first: "*And He has not made your adopted sons your sons; that is your saying with your mouths, and God speaks the truth and He guides to the way. Call them by their fathers' names — that is more equitable in the sight of God. **And if you do not know their fathers, then they are your brothers in religion and your clients.** And there is no fault in you for what you did in error, but only in what your hearts intended; and God is Forgiving, Merciful*" [3]. So the rule contemplates the case where the father is unknown and does not treat it as a problem: such a child is your brother in religion, and the child of nobody's fiction.

**The prohibition, stated as sharply as anything in the corpus.** "Whoever claims descent from other than his father, knowing that he is not his father — Paradise is forbidden him" [4], and "do not turn away from your fathers; whoever turns away from his father, that is disbelief" [5]. Note the condition inside the first: *knowing*. It is about a deliberate falsification of parentage, not about a child who does not know, or an error.

And it cuts both ways. The Hanafi tradition pairs it with the opposite offence, in the hadith cited in *Radd al-Muhtar*: "and any man who denies his child while looking at him — God will veil Himself from him on the Day of Resurrection and disgrace him before the first and the last" [6]. Falsely attaching a child and falsely detaching one are treated as the same kind of wrong.

The reason the tradition is so firm here is visible in how lineage actually gets assigned. In the dispute between Sa'd b. Abi Waqqas and 'Abd b. Zam'a over a boy, the Prophet looked and saw a clear resemblance to 'Utba — and ruled against the resemblance: "He is yours, 'Abd. The child belongs to the bed, and for the fornicator is the stone. And veil yourself from him, Sawda bint Zam'a" [7]. Parentage follows an objective rule, not a claim, not an appearance, and not a preference. That is a protection for the child, and it is why the fiction was closed.

**What replaced it is not a lesser thing.** "I and the guardian of the orphan will be in the Garden like these two" — and the narrator points to his index and middle fingers; al-Tirmidhi grades it *hasan sahih* [8]. The wording in other narrations is "*the guardian of an orphan, his own or another's*", which Ibn 'Abd al-Barr glosses without hesitation: "the meaning of his words in this hadith, *his own or another's*, is: from among his relatives and from other than his relatives" [9]. Al-Nawawi spells the same out at length — the relative case covers a grandfather, mother, grandmother, brother, sister, uncle or aunt, "and the case of another's is that he be unrelated" [10]. A stranger raising a child that is not theirs is squarely inside the promise.

Al-Nawawi also defines what the role actually consists of, and it is the substance of parenting: "the guardian of the orphan is the one who undertakes his affairs — maintenance, clothing, discipline, upbringing and other things; and this merit is attained by one who maintains him from his own wealth or from the orphan's wealth by a lawful guardianship" [11]. And the same standing attaches to the household more broadly: "the one who strives on behalf of the widow and the orphan is like the one who fights in the way of God" [12].

**So the shape of it is:** take the child, house them, feed them, clothe them, educate them, raise them, be to them in every practical respect what a parent is — and keep their name and their lineage theirs. Where the father is unknown, the verse itself says what to do: they are your brother in religion. What is forbidden is the specific act of asserting that a child descends from someone they do not.

**What this sheet does not settle.** It gives no ruling on the modern legal apparatus: a court adoption order, a birth certificate reissued in the adoptive parents' name, a changed surname, the legal severance of the birth family. Those are twentieth-century instruments, and the works retrieved here do not address them — including the practical question of whether a surname can be carried for legal or administrative reasons without amounting to the claim of descent that is prohibited. Nor is there anything here on how an adopted child stands for inheritance, or on the *mahram* question and whether milk-kinship is used to resolve it: those questions have well-developed classical answers, but not on this sheet, and I am not going to reconstruct them from passages about something else.

If you are actually going through an adoption, those are exactly the points to take to a scholar, with your paperwork in hand. What you can take from here is the principle that will govern their answers: the care is praised as highly as almost anything in the tradition; the child's parentage is theirs and is not yours to reassign.
