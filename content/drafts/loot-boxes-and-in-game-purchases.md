# Are loot boxes and in-game purchases gambling?

> Reviewer note: Maysir turns on paying for an unknown outcome. Say what the definition catches and what it does not — a straightforward purchase of a known item is not the same transaction.


## Answer

**Separate the two things you can buy in a game, because the books treat them completely differently.** Paying a known price for a known item is an ordinary sale and raises nothing. Paying a known price for an unknown item — a box, a crate, a pull — is the case, and it meets more of the classical description of *maysir* than most people expect. It is not a settled question, but it is a much closer one than "it's only a game" suggests.

**First: how wide the category was drawn.**

`الميسر: القمار` [1] — maysir is qimar, gambling — said al-Hasan, and the early authorities then spent their effort insisting the category does not depend on the stake being worth anything. Mujahid: `كل شئ فيه قمار فهو ميسر حتى لعب الصبيان بالجوز` [2] — everything with gambling in it is maysir, even children playing for walnuts. Tawus and 'Ata': `كل قمار فهو من الميسر، حتى لعب الصبيان بالكعاب، والجوز` [3] — even children's knucklebones.

Ibn Sirin goes furthest, and his example is the one to hold on to: `كل قمار ميسر حتى اللعب بالنرد على القيام والصياح والريشة يجعلها الرجل في رأسه` [4] — every gamble is maysir, even playing backgammon for the stake of standing up, or shouting, or a feather a man puts on his head.

A feather on the head. Whatever else that settles, it disposes of the argument that a purely cosmetic reward is outside the category because it has no resale value. These authorities were at pains to say the opposite.

**Second: an axis that has nothing to do with money at all.**

Al-Nawawi explains why backgammon is forbidden while chess was argued about, and the reason is not the stake: `ولان المعول فيه على ما يخرجه الكعبان فشابه الأزلام` [5] — because reliance in it is on what the dice throw up, so it resembles the `أزلام`, the divining arrows the verse names. And the contrast: `ويخالف الشطرنج فإن المعول فيه على رأيه` [6] — chess differs, because reliance in it is on the player's judgement.

The `أزلام` were `السهام التي كان أهل الجاهلية يستقسمون بها` [7] — arrows the pre-Islamic Arabs drew lots with. So one strand of this prohibition is about *submitting an outcome to a randomiser* rather than to skill, quite apart from what is won.

A loot box is a randomiser you pay to pull. On this axis, that is the whole design.

**Third: the technical definition, and where a real argument lives.**

Al-Nawawi gives the Shafi'i test precisely: `لان القمار أن لا يخلو أحد من أن يغنم أو يغرم` [8] — qimar is that neither party escapes either gaining or losing. Where one side stakes and the other cannot lose, he says: `وههنا أحدهما يغنم ولا يغرم` [9] — here one gains and does not lose — and that is not qimar, though it may be invalid for other reasons.

This is where a serious counter-argument sits, and it deserves stating fairly rather than being brushed aside. A loot box buyer always receives *something*; the publisher is not risking a stake against them. On the strict two-sided test, someone can argue that is a sale of an unspecified good rather than a wager. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr records that the scholars did not differ that `القمار من الميسر المحرم` [10] — but what counts as qimar is exactly the question.

The reply is that the buyer plainly can lose: the price is fixed and the value received is not, so on any given pull he may pay far more than what he gets. Whether that makes him a wagerer or a buyer of something undescribed is the live issue, and it is a question for a scholar rather than for this page.

**Fourth, and this is the strongest thing here.**

Ibn Taymiyya argues directly that reading maysir as a wealth offence misses the point: `ومما يبين أن الميسر لم يحرم لمجرد أكل المال بالباطل` [11] — what shows that maysir was not forbidden merely for consuming wealth wrongfully — `بل في الميسر علة آخرى غير أكل المال بالباطل كما في الخمر` [12], rather there is another cause in maysir besides that, as there is in wine. His evidence is that God paired them and gave them a shared cause: `أن الله قرن بيت الخمر والميسر وجعل العلة في تحريم هذا هي العلة في تحريم هذا` [13].

What is that cause? He names both benefits people took from maysir: `وكذلك الميسر كانت النفوس تنتفع بما تحصله به من المال وما يحصل به من لذة اللعب` [14] — the money gained, *and the pleasure of the play itself*. And his verdict on the trade: a measure of delight, then `فما يعقبه من المضار ويفوته من المسار أضعاف ذلك` [15] — the harms following it and the goods it costs you are multiples of that.

That is the argument that reaches a randomised purchase most directly, and it does not depend on the item having resale value. It is about what a repeated, uncertain, pleasurable pull does to the person pulling. It is also, almost word for word, the modern objection to the mechanic.

The verse's own stated harm is in the same register: `ومن كونها تصد عن ذكر الله وعن الصلاة` [16] — that it turns you away from the remembrance of God and from prayer.

**What these books do not reach.**

No author here considered a purchase where the buyer is guaranteed an item of roughly equal value whatever drops, nor "pity timers" that guarantee the desired item after a fixed number of pulls, nor the difference between rewards that can be sold for real money and rewards locked inside the account. Nothing addresses a battle pass or a season pass, which is a known set of rewards for a known price and looks like an ordinary sale. Nothing addresses free boxes earned by playing, where no money is staked at all.

**What to do.**

Take these facts to a scholar rather than the phrase "in-game purchase":

- Do you pay money, and is what you receive determined at random? If both, this is the question; if either is no, it is probably an ordinary sale.
- Can what you win be sold or traded for real money? That makes the wealth argument direct rather than analogical.
- Is there a guaranteed floor to what you receive, or can a pull be worth almost nothing?
- Is it something you do once, or something you find yourself returning to? On Ibn Taymiyya's reasoning at [11] and [14], that question is not a side issue — it is closer to the cause of the prohibition than the money is.

Buying a specific item you can see, for a price you know, is not what any of this material is about. If you are unsure whether you have crossed from one to the other, the honest test is the last question above, and you already know the answer to it.
