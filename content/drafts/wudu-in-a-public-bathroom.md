# How do I make wudu in a public toilet at work?

## Answer

**You can make wudu in a normal workplace bathroom, and the books permit it
explicitly.** The awkwardness is real, but it is logistical, not legal.

**The ruling that covers a modern cubicle.** Al-Nawawi states the classical
preference and then its exception in the same breath: **our colleagues agreed
that it is recommended not to cleanse with water in the place of relieving
oneself, so that it does not splash onto him — and this is in other than the
privies built for that purpose. As for what is built for that, like the marhad,
there is no harm in it, because it does not splash onto him, and because going
out of it to somewhere else involves hardship.** [1]

That is precisely your situation. The old preference was about squatting in the
open or over a pit, where splash-back was the concern. A tiled room with a
drain — a *marhad* — is the case he carves out. The concern is hygiene, not
sanctity, and a modern bathroom answers it.

**The adab that still applies.** These are the things worth doing properly, and
they cost nothing.

- **Say the du'a before you go in, not after.** Anas: **when the Prophet entered
  the privy he would say: O Allah, I seek refuge in You from the male and female
  devils.** [2] Al-Nawawi is precise about the timing — **his words "when he
  entered" mean when he intended to enter, and it comes expressly so in Bukhari's
  narration** [3]. Say it at the door, silently.
- **Left foot in.** Ibn Abidin: **when he reaches the door he begins with the
  basmala — it is said the du'a, and this is the sound view — so he says: In the
  name of Allah; O Allah, I seek refuge in You from the male and female devils.
  Then he enters with the left.** [4]
- **Right foot out, and say *ghufranak*** — **then he goes out with his right
  foot and says: Your forgiveness.** [5]
- **Don't talk in there.** **He does not return a greeting, nor answer a
  mu'adhdhin.** [6] If a colleague speaks to you, this is why you can simply not
  answer until you are out.

**And this applies wherever you are.** Al-Nawawi: **this adab is agreed to be
recommended, and there is no difference in it between built structures and open
country.** [7]

**The practical problem: the feet.**

There is no ruling requiring you to hoist a foot into a shared sink, and it is
worth saying plainly that you are not obliged to make a scene. Options, roughly
in order of how well they usually work:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Wipe over socks** | The real answer for most people. Make full wudu at home, put socks on, and from then on wipe over them — a day and a night for a resident. There is a separate answer here on this and on which socks qualify. |
| Disabled or accessible washroom | Usually has space and a lower basin, and no queue. |
| A bottle | Fill it at the sink, wash your feet over the drain or outside. |
| The sink, if the room is empty | Permitted. Dry up after — leaving a mess is not part of the wudu. |

The socks route is the one to plan around, because it removes the problem
entirely rather than managing it. It requires only that you make full wudu before
you leave the house.

**Two small things people ask about.**

*Wudu in the same room as a toilet is valid.* The reservations in the books are
about splashing and about dignity, not about the water or the wudu being
affected. Nothing in the passages above invalidates anything.

*Do the du'a and dhikr quietly.* You are not required to recite aloud, and the
adab above in fact tells you to keep the mouth still in there.

The verse asks for a specific, finite thing: **when ye rise up for prayer, wash
you faces, and your hands up to the elbows, and lightly rub your heads and (wash)
your feet up to the ankles** [8]. Do that, in whatever room you have. It is meant
to be possible in the middle of a working day, and it is.
