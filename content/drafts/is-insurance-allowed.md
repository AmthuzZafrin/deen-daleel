## Answer

**These books never discuss insurance — it did not exist in a form any of them would recognise. But they contain both halves of the modern argument about it, fully worked out, and the disagreement among contemporary scholars is a continuation of materials you can read here directly. This page sets out both sides and does not rule.**

**The objection: `غرر`.**

Al-Nawawi treats this as foundational: `وأما النهى عن بيع الغرر فهو أصل عظيم من أصول كتاب البيوع ولهذا قدمه ويحدث ويدخل فيه مسائل كثيرة غير منحصرة كبيع الآبق والمعدوم والمجهول وما لا يقدر على تسليمه ولم يتم ملك البائع عليه وبيع السمك في الماء الكثير واللبن في الضرع وبيع الحمل في البطن` [1] — the prohibition of sales involving `غرر` is a great principle of the book of sales, taking in countless cases: the runaway slave, the non-existent, the unknown, what cannot be delivered, fish in open water, milk in the udder, the unborn young.

An insurance policy has that shape. You pay a known premium for a payout that may never come, whose size you do not know, on an event that may not happen.

Ibn Taymiyya sharpens what the vice actually is, and this matters because the objection is often stated badly. There is **no** general prohibition on selling what does not yet exist: `وليست العلة في المنع لا الوجود ولا العدم بل الذي ثبت في الصحيح عن النبى انه نهي عن بيع الغرر والغرر ما لا يقدر على تسليمه سواء كان موجودا أو معدوما` [2] — the operative cause is neither existence nor non-existence; it is inability to deliver, whether the thing exists or not.

And the reason that is bad: `فان موجب البيع تسليم المبيع والبائع عاجز عنه والمشترى انما يشتريه مخاطرة ومقامرة فان امكنه اخذه كان المشترى قد قمر البائع وان لم يمكنه أخذه كان البائع قد قمر المشترى` [3] — the buyer is purchasing **as a speculation and a gamble**: if delivery turns out possible the buyer has won off the seller; if not, the seller has won off the buyer. Of selling a tree's future crop he says plainly: `فهذا من القمار وهو من الميسر الذي نهى الله عنه` [4].

That is the serious form of the objection to commercial insurance. Not that the future is unknown — all trade involves that — but that the contract is structured so that one party's gain is the other's loss on the turn of an event, which makes it a wager rather than an exchange.

**But the same author who calls `غرر` a great principle also states the test for when it does not invalidate.**

Al-Nawawi: `وقد يحتمل بعض الغرر بيعا إذ دعت إليه حاجة كالجهل بأساس الدار وكما إذا باع الشاة الحامل والتي في ضرعها لبن فإنه يصح للبيع لأن الأساس تابع للظاهر من الدار` [5] — some `غرر` is tolerated in a sale where need calls for it. And the agreed examples are startlingly ordinary: `وأجمعوا على جواز إجارة الدار والدابة والثوب ونحو ذلك شهرا مع أن الشهر قد يكون ثلاثين يوما وقد يكون تسعة وعشرين وأجمعوا على جواز دخول الحمام بالأجرة مع اختلاف الناس في استعمالهم الماء وفي قدر مكثهم` [6] — renting by the month, when a month may be thirty days or twenty-nine; paying to enter the bathhouse, though people use different amounts of water and stay different lengths of time.

Then the rule itself: `وهو أنه إن دعت حاجة إلى ارتكاب الغرر ولا يمكن الاحتراز عنه الا بمشقة وكان الغرر حقيرا جاز البيع والا فلا` [7] — **if need calls for accepting the uncertainty, and it cannot be avoided without hardship, and the uncertainty is slight, the sale is valid; otherwise not.**

Three conditions. Every modern argument that insurance is permissible runs through that sentence, and every argument that it is not disputes whether the uncertainty in an insurance contract is `حقير` — slight — when it is in fact the entire substance of the contract.

**The case on the other side: the `عاقلة`.**

Islamic law already contains a compulsory scheme in which a group jointly bears one member's accidental liability. `العاقلة: من يحمل العقل. والعقل: الدية` [8] — those who bear the blood-money. `أن الدية في قتل الخطأ وعمد الخطأ تجب على العاقلة وهم العصبات` [9] — in accidental killing the compensation falls on the kin-group.

Al-Jassas confronts the obvious objection — that no soul bears another's burden — and his answer is the heart of the matter: `لأن الآية إنما نفت أن يؤخذ الإنسان بذنب غيره وليس في إيجاب الدية على العاقلة أخذهم بذنب الجاني إنما الدية عندنا على القاتل وأمر هؤلاء القوم بالدخول معه في تحملها على وجه المواساة له من غير أن يلزمهم ذنب جنايته` [10] — the verse denies that a person be seized for another's sin; imposing the blood-money on the kin is not that. The liability is the killer's; the group is ordered **to join him in bearing it by way of mutual support**, without any guilt attaching to them.

And the mechanics are recognisably those of an insurance pool: `فكذلك أمرت العاقلة بتحمل الدية عن قاتل الخطأ على جهة المواساة من غير إجحاف بهم به وإنما يلزم كل رجل منهم ثلاثة دراهم أو أربعة دراهم ويجعل ذلك في أعطياتهم إذا كانوا من أهل الديوان ومؤجلة ثلاث سنين` [11] — each man is liable for only three or four dirhams, deducted from their stipends, spread over three years. A small periodic contribution from many, covering a large loss falling on one.

Al-Jassas also notes it was not an innovation: `وقد كان تحمل الديات مشهورا في العرب قبل الإسلام وكان ذلك مما يعد من جميل أفعالهم ومكارم أخلاقهم` [12] — the bearing of blood-money was well known among the Arabs before Islam and counted among their finest practices. It was retained rather than abolished.

**Its limits are as instructive as its existence.** It covers accident only: `ليس على العاقلة عقل في قتل العمد إنما عليهم عقل قتل الخطأ` [13] — nothing for deliberate killing. And it has a threshold: `والأمر عندنا ان الدية لا تجب على العاقلة حتى تبلغ الثلث فصاعدا فما بلغ الثلث فهو على العاقلة وما كان دون الثلث فهو في مال الجارح خاصة` [14] — below a third, the injurer pays alone.

Deliberate harm is excluded, and small losses stay with the person who caused them. Any modern scheme that lets someone recover for damage they caused on purpose, or that insures trivia, is further from this precedent than one that does not.

**Why the two sides do not simply cancel out.**

The distinction most contemporary scholars draw runs along the line the material above already marks. In the `عاقلة`, nobody buys anything: contributions are `مواساة`, mutual support, and no contributor is trying to profit from another's misfortune. In a commercial policy, a premium is exchanged for a contingent payout, and the insurer profits when claims are low. The first is a donation arrangement; the second is a sale — and it is sales that the `غرر` rule governs.

That is why mutual or cooperative models are argued to sit closer to the classical precedent, and why conventional policies are argued to fall under Ibn Taymiyya's `مخاطرة ومقامرة`. It is also why the counter-argument appeals to al-Nawawi's three conditions: that need is real, that the risk cannot be avoided without hardship, and — the contested part — that the uncertainty in a regulated, actuarially priced modern policy is not the raw wager the classical cases describe.

**What is genuinely absent here.**

None of these authors saw a contract where the counterparty is a regulated company holding reserves, where the price is set from mortality or accident tables rather than guessed, where the payout is defined in the policy rather than left open, or where the state requires you to hold cover before you may drive or rent a home. Those features are exactly what the modern argument turns on, and there is no text in this collection that weighs them.

Nor is there anything on the further question of what an insurer does with the pooled money, which for many scholars is a separate objection to conventional policies independent of `غرر`.

**What to do.**

This needs a scholar, and it needs the actual policy rather than the word "insurance," because the questions above sort real products differently:

- Is it a mutual or cooperative scheme where contributions are donations and surpluses return to members, or a commercial contract for profit?
- Is it required by law where you live, or voluntary? Compulsion bears directly on al-Nawawi's `حاجة` condition.
- Does it cover accident and genuine loss, or does it reach into things the `عاقلة` precedent excludes?
- If it is life or savings-linked rather than indemnity, that is a different contract again and raises the questions on the pages about interest.

The general principle is not obscure — uncertainty is tolerated where need requires it and it is slight, and a contract that is a wager dressed as a sale is not. Which of those a given policy is, is a judgement about that policy, and this page is not in a position to make it.
