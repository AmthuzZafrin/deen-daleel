# Can I accept a Christmas or birthday gift?

## Answer

Yes. And the objection you may have heard rests on two reports that the jurists themselves spent a great deal of effort reconciling — with every one of their reconciliations pointing away from your case.

**Start with the practice.**

'A'isha: the Prophet ﷺ "**used to accept the gift and repay it**" [1].

And gifts from non-Muslims specifically. Ibn Qudama: "**it is permissible to accept the gift of the disbelievers among the people of war — because the Prophet ﷺ accepted the gift of al-Muqawqis, the ruler of Egypt**" [2].

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr lists more of them by name: he accepted from "**Ukaydir of Duma, Farwa b. Nufatha, al-Muqawqis and others**" [3]. And: "**the Prophet ﷺ did accept the gift of a people among the idolaters, and a group of the jurists permitted accepting it**" [4].

**The two reports on the other side.**

'Amir b. Malik — the one called *Mula'ib al-Asinna* — "said: I came to the Prophet, upon him be peace, with a gift, and he said: **we will not accept the gift of an idolater**" [5]. There is a second of the same kind, in which he says he has been forbidden *zabd al-mushrikin*.

**And here is how the jurists handled the conflict — three answers, and none of them is a general rule against gifts.**

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr sets them out:

*Abrogation.* Some said the refusal abrogated the earlier acceptances [3]. He reports this and does not adopt it.

*Situational, about war and conversion.* "Others said: **there is neither abrogating nor abrogated in the two**. The meaning in them is that he would not accept a gift from one over whom he hoped for victory and the taking of his country, or [hoped for] his entering Islam. From such a one he was forbidden to accept a gift and make truce with him and leave him on his religion, while having power over him, or hoping for his guidance" [6].

*A matter of his own courtesy.* "Others said he had a choice in accepting their gift and in declining it — **because it was of his character ﷺ to repay a gift with something better than it, and so he did not accept an idolater's gift, lest he repay him with something better**" [7].

The Hanafis reached the same place by a different route. Al-Fatawa al-Hindiyya: "Muhammad transmitted in *al-Siyar al-Kabir* **conflicting reports** — in some of them that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ accepted the gifts of the idolater, and in some that he did not accept. **So a reconciliation is necessary**" [8]. And Abu Ja'far al-Hinduwani's: "what is narrated that he did not accept is to be understood as his not accepting from a person of whom it was the Prophet's ﷺ strong impression that this person had formed the idea **that he fought them out of desire for wealth, not to raise the word of God**" [9].

Read all four together. The refusal is about *what the gift would be taken to mean* between a commander and an enemy in wartime. Not one of them is about a colleague handing you a wrapped box in December.

Al-Awza'i, asked whether the Byzantine emperor's gift to the Caliph could be accepted, answered simply: "**I see no harm in that**" [10].

**What about the occasion?**

Nothing in the sources makes the date of a gift the thing that matters. What the jurists guard against is different: eating what was slaughtered for a festival, and taking part in the festival as a festival. A gift is a gift.

Two practical qualifications from the same body of material:

- **If the gift is food, ask what is in it.** Wine in a hamper, pork in a selection, meat slaughtered for the occasion — these are judged as themselves and you decline that item, not the gesture. You can say so plainly and most people take it well.
- **Repay it.** The Prophet's practice was to accept *and* to give back [1], and Ibn 'Abd al-Barr's third reconciliation shows how seriously he took the repayment. Accepting without ever giving is not the sunna here.

**And there is a reason to accept beyond permission.**

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, explaining why some thought the refusal was a scruple, notes what gift-giving does: "**because of what is in mutual gift-giving and in *zabd* of mutual love and the softening of hearts**" [11].

He mentions it as the reason for caution in a state of war. In your case it is the reason to say yes. Your family is not at war with you; a present at Christmas from a mother who is worried she is losing you is a hand held out, and refusing it teaches her that Islam took her child away.

**Practically.**

- **Take the gift.** Say thank you, and mean it.
- **Give one back**, at a time of your choosing — theirs, or 'Id, or no occasion at all.
- **Decline only the specific thing**, if the contents are a problem, and without a lecture.
- **Accepting a present is not celebrating a festival.** These are two separate questions, and the sources treat them separately.
- **A birthday present is the same case** and easier: nothing about it touches another religion's rites at all.
