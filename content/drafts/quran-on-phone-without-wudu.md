# Can I read Quran on my phone without wudu?

## Answer

A phone did not exist when any of these books were written, so nothing here rules on it directly. What the sources do give — and give in unusual detail — is the *reasoning* the rule about the *mushaf* rests on, plus a set of intermediate cases the jurists argued over that are much closer to a phone than a bound codex is. Those are enough to see the shape of the answer, and to see where it is genuinely open.

**Start with what is agreed and is not in question at all.** Al-Nawawi, commenting on a hadith, states it as consensus: "the scholars are agreed that one in a state of minor impurity may eat, drink, remember God Most High, recite the Qur'an, and have relations — and there is no dislike in any of that; the evidences of the sound and well-known *sunna* have been abundant on all of this, together with the consensus of the community" [1]. He states the same specifically in the *Majmu'*: "the Muslims are agreed on the permissibility of a person in a state of minor impurity reciting the Qur'an, though it is preferable that he perform *wudu* for it. The Imam of the Two Sanctuaries and others said: it is not to be said that the recitation of one in a state of impurity is disliked, for it is authentically established from the Prophet that he used to recite while in a state of impurity" [2].

So the rule under discussion is not about the Qur'an as text. It is about handling one particular object.

**Al-Nawawi's second point goes further, and it is the one that most directly touches a screen.** "It is permissible for the person in a state of major impurity and for the menstruating woman to look at the *mushaf* and to read it with the heart without moving the tongue — **and there is no disagreement about this**" [3]. Looking at written Qur'an is not touching it. That distinction is already in the books; the screen simply makes it easier.

**Now the reasoning behind the rule about the *mushaf*.** Ibn Qudama states the ruling on things that contain Qur'an without being one: "it is permissible to touch books of *tafsir*, *fiqh* and others, and letters, even if there are verses of the Qur'an in them — by the evidence that the Prophet wrote to Caesar a letter containing a verse; **and because the name *mushaf* does not fall upon them, and its sanctity is not established for them**" [4].

That is a two-part test, and both parts are worth holding onto: does the object *count* as a *mushaf*, and is the specific sanctity of a *mushaf* established for it? A device that holds an app among ten thousand other things is not obviously on the same side of that line as a bound copy.

Ibn Qudama then works two intermediate cases, and his reasoning in both is the reasoning a modern question needs.

*Children's writing-boards*: "concerning schoolchildren touching their boards that have Qur'an on them there are two views: the first is permissibility, because it is a place of need — for if we made purity a condition it would lead to their being put off memorising it; the second is prevention, because they fall under the generality of the verse" [5].

*Coins with Qur'an stamped on them*: "concerning dirhams with the Qur'an written on them there are two views: the first is prevention, and it is the position of Abu Hanifa — and 'Ata', al-Qasim and al-Sha'bi disliked it — because the Qur'an is written on them, so they resemble the leaf. The second is permissibility, because the name *mushaf* does not fall upon them, so they resemble books of *fiqh*; **and because there is hardship in guarding against them, so they resemble the children's boards**" [6].

Read those two together and you have three named grounds for treating an object as not being a *mushaf*: the name does not apply to it, guarding against it is a hardship, and restricting it would obstruct learning. A phone attracts all three at once more strongly than a coin does. The sources do not say that — but they say what the grounds are, and the grounds are checkable.

**The Hanafis worked the same territory from the direction of barriers, and one of their jurists left a model of how to handle a case with no precedent.** *Al-Bahr al-Ra'iq* records the sleeve dispute: "in *al-Muhit*: it is not disliked to touch it with the sleeve, in the view of the majority — and the author chose this in *al-Kafi*, and he reasoned that touching is prohibited, and it is a name for direct contact with the hand without a barrier" [7]. On books that merely contain verses the school is split: "in *al-Khulasa*: touching books of hadith and *fiqh* is disliked for the one in a state of impurity, according to the two companions; and according to Abu Hanifa the sounder view is that it is not disliked" [8]. And they noticed their own reasoning ran too far: "they said it is disliked to touch books of *tafsir*, *fiqh* and the *sunan*, because they are not free of Qur'anic verses — **and this reasoning would prevent touching commentaries on grammar as well**" [9].

Then the passage most worth the reader's attention. A jurist is asked whether one may touch the *mushaf* with a handkerchief he is wearing round his neck, and answers: "**I know of no transmitted text on it.** What appears to me is that if it is with its end and it moves with his movement, it ought not to be permitted; and if it does not move with his movement, it ought to be permitted" [10]. He says plainly that there is no precedent, reasons from a nearby case, and marks the answer as his own inference. That is exactly the register a question about a phone deserves, and it is the register the classical books themselves use when they hit something new.

**One more thing worth knowing, because it dissolves the emergency.** Ibn Qudama: "if the one in a state of impurity needs to touch the *mushaf* when there is no water, he performs *tayammum* and touching it is permitted" [11]. Even on the strictest reading, the rule was never meant to leave a person unable to reach the Qur'an.

**And a note on the piety, which the same page records but does not present as law.** *Al-Bahr al-Ra'iq* passes on al-Halwani's "I attained this knowledge only through veneration — for I never took up paper except in a state of purity", and that al-Sarakhsi, unwell one night while revising his book, performed *wudu* seventeen times. Those are reported as personal scrupulousness, not as requirements, and the same passage reports the schools disagreeing over whether even touching books of hadith is disliked.

**So, where does this leave a phone?**

What is settled: you may recite from memory, look at the screen, and read without moving your tongue, in any state — with no disagreement. You may use a *tafsir* app, or any app that is not a plain *mushaf*, on Ibn Qudama's reasoning about books containing verses, though the two Hanafi companions were stricter about that class.

What is not settled here: whether a device displaying Qur'an takes the sanctity of a *mushaf* while it is displayed. The books give the grounds — the name, the hardship of guarding against it, the obstruction of learning — and the analogues, the coin and the child's board, on which the schools split. They do not give the answer, because they never faced the question. Contemporary scholars have addressed it and have not all landed in the same place; if you want a position to follow rather than the reasoning behind one, that is who to ask.

The practical upshot is small: if you have *wudu*, nothing here is a question at all. If you do not, reciting and reading are open by consensus, and the only live issue is deliberate contact with the displayed text — an issue on which the classical grounds point one way and the classical caution points the other.

ابن الحارث حدثنا شعبة عن قتادة قال سمعت أنسا يقول قال رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم لأبي بمثله هذه الأسانيد الثلاثة رواتها كلهم بصريون وهذا من المستطرفات أن يجتمع ثلاثة أسانيد متصلة مسلسلون بغير قصد وقد سبق بيان مثله وشعبة واسطي بصرى سبق بيانه مرات وفي الطريق الثالث فائدة حسنة وهي أن قتادة صرح بالسماع من أنس بخلاف الأوليين وقتادة مدلس فينتفي أن يخاف من تدليسه بتصريحه بالسماع وقد سبق التنبيه على مثل هذا مرات وفي الحديث فوائد كثيرة منها استحباب قراءة القرآن على الحذاق فيه وأهل العلم به والفضل وإن كان القارئ أفضل من المقروء عليه ومنها المنقبة الشريفة لأبي بقراءة النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم ولا يعلم أحد من الناس شاركه في هذا ومنها منقبة أخرى له بذكر الله تعالى له ونصه عليه في هذه المنزلة الرفيعة ومنها البكاء للسرور والفرح مما يبشر الإنسان به ويعطاه من معالي الأمور وأما قوله آلله سماني لك فيه أنه يجوز أن يكون الله تعالى أمر النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم يقرأ على رجل من أمته ولم ينص على أبي فأراد أبي أن يتحقق هل نص عليه أو قال على رجل فيؤخذ منه الاستثبات في المحتملات   واختلفوا في الحكمة في قراءته صلى الله عليه وسلم على أبي والمختار أن سببها أن تستن الأمة بذلك في القراءة على أهل الاتقان والفضل ويتعلموا آداب القراءة ولا يأنف أحد من ذلك وقيل للتنبيه على جلالة أبي وأهليته لأخذ القرآن عنه وكان بعده صلى الله عليه وسلم رأسا وإمام في اقراء القرآن وهو أجل ناشرته أو من أجلهم ويتضمن معجزة لرسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم وأما تخصيص هذه السورة فلأنها وجيزة جامعة لقواعد كثيرة من أصول الدين وفروعه ومهماته والاخلاص وتطهير القلوب وكان الوقت يقتضي الاختصار والله أعلم باب فضل استماع القرآن وطلب القراءة من حافظه للاستماع والبكاء عن القراءة والتدبر قال مسلم حدثنا أبو بكر بن أبي شيبة وأبو كريب جميعا عن حفص قال أبو بكر حدثنا حفص بن
