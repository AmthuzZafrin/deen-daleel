# Can I celebrate New Year?

## Answer

This is the clearest case in this whole section, and the answer depends entirely on which New Year you mean.

**What the sources prohibit, stated plainly.**

Ibn Taymiyya: "**it is not lawful for the Muslims to imitate them in anything that belongs specifically to their festivals** — not in food, nor dress, nor bathing, nor lighting fires, nor suspending a habit of livelihood or worship or anything else. And it is not lawful to hold a banquet, nor to give gifts, nor to sell what assists in that, for the sake of that; nor to let children and the like play the games that belong to the festivals; nor to display adornment. **In sum: it is not for them to single out their festivals with anything of their emblems**" [1].

And the standard he sets in place of it: "**rather, the day of their festival should be for the Muslims like any other day — the Muslims singling it out with nothing of their distinguishing marks**" [2].

He cites 'Abdullah b. 'Amr b. al-'As, and it is worth quoting because it names the Persian New Year specifically: "**whoever settles in the lands of the non-Arabs and makes their *nayruz* and their *mihrajan*, and imitates them until he dies in that state — he is gathered with them on the Day of Resurrection**" [3].

The hadith behind the reasoning: "**whoever imitates a people is one of them**", and in another wording "**he is not of us who imitates other than us** — and it is a good hadith" [4].

He extends it past attendance to assistance: "likewise they forbade **assisting them in their festivals by gift or by trade**, and they said it is not lawful for the Muslims to sell to the Christians anything for the benefit of their festival — no meat, no blood, no cloth — nor is a beast to be lent to them, nor are they to be helped in anything of their religion, **because that is a magnifying of their idolatry and a help to them in their disbelief**" [5]. And on the food: "**the majority of the imams disliked — either as prohibition or as avoidance — eating what they slaughtered for their festivals and their offerings**, entering it under *what has been dedicated to other than God* and *what is slaughtered on the stone altars*" [6].

**And what Islam puts in place of festivals.**

Anas: the Prophet ﷺ came to Medina and found two days on which they used to play, and said: "**God has replaced them for you with two better than them — the day of Sacrifice and the day of Fitr**" [7].

**So: which New Year?**

*A religious new year — Nayruz, or a festival of another religion's calendar with rites attached.* This is precisely what Ibn Taymiyya is describing, and 'Abdullah b. 'Amr names *nayruz* by name. No.

*The first of January, as it exists in most of the world now.* This is the harder case, and the honest position is that the sources are not describing it. There is no rite, no deity, no priest, no offering; the date is the turnover of an administrative calendar. On the other hand it is unmistakably marked as an occasion, and the reasoning above is about **singling a day out with its own emblems** — which is exactly what fireworks at midnight are.

Weigh the two things the sources actually name:

- **Is anything of another religion's worship in it for you?** If not, the *shi'ar* — the emblem — reasoning is the only one still in play, and it is a reasoning about resemblance rather than about creed.
- **Are you singling the day out with observances that belong to it?** Ibn Taymiyya's list is specific: special food, new clothes, decorations, suspending your ordinary routine *because of the day*. If your first of January is indistinguishable from any other day, nothing here reaches you.

**What that means in practice.**

- **Do not attend a religious new-year rite** of any tradition. This is the settled case.
- **Do not buy or supply what a religious festival needs.** Ibn Taymiyya's prohibition on assisting is explicit and it covers the trade as well as the attendance.
- **A midnight party, countdown, fireworks and toasts** — the drinking is separately forbidden, the mixed-gathering questions apply as they always do, and the whole occasion is the clearest instance of *singling out a day with its emblems* that a modern calendar offers. The reasoning above bears on it directly and the safe course is plain.
- **Being awake at midnight is not a sin.** Neither is wishing a colleague a good year on the 2nd of January, or noting that a year has ended.
- **A work party you cannot avoid** is governed by the ordinary rules about places where wrong is done: if you cannot change what happens there, do not go; if attending is genuinely required of you and nothing forbidden is at your table, the sources are less severe than people assume.
- **The positive half matters too.** Islam gives you two festivals and asks you to keep them well. A community that keeps its own 'Ids properly has much less trouble with this question than one that does not.

**One note on the reasoning, so you can use it yourself.** Every prohibition quoted above hangs on one idea — that a festival is an *emblem*, a public mark of who you belong to. That is why the objection covers food, dress and decoration rather than belief alone, and it is why the same authors are unbothered by a Muslim eating with Christians on an ordinary Tuesday. If you keep the distinction between *joining people* and *joining their rites*, you will get most of this section right without having to look anything up.
