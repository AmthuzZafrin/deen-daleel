# Who do I have to cover in front of?

## Answer

The verse gives a list, and the list is the answer to most of the question. The
two things it does not settle — uncles, and how much you may relax in front of
those it does name — are settled by the fiqh, and one of them has a live
disagreement worth knowing about.

**The list.** Qur'an 24:31 names those before whom a woman need not cover: "not
to reveal their adornment save to their own husbands or fathers or husbands'
fathers, or their sons or their husbands' sons, or their brothers or their
brothers' sons or sisters' sons, or their women... or male attendants who lack
vigour, or children who know naught of women's nakedness" [1].

**The principle behind the list, which answers the cousin question.**
Al-Qurtubi: "all of this is in the sense of those forbidden in marriage — for
that runs on the meanings in the lines of descent — and these are mahrams" [2].
The category tracks the marriage prohibitions. A cousin is someone you may
marry, so he is not on the list and not a mahram, and covering in front of him
is the same as covering in front of any other man. This is the single most
common source of confusion in extended families, and the sources are not
ambiguous about it: the test is *could you marry him*, not *did you grow up in
the same house*.

By the same principle, foster relations count even though the verse does not
name them: "the verse does not mention suckling, and it is like lineage" [3].

**Uncles: a real disagreement, with an intelligible reason.** They are not in
the list either. The majority read them in anyway — "the majority hold that the
paternal uncle and the maternal uncle are like the rest of the mahrams in the
permissibility of their looking at what is permitted to the others" [4]. But not
everyone: "in the view of al-Sha'bi and 'Ikrima the paternal and maternal uncle
are not among the mahrams; and 'Ikrima said: they were not mentioned in the
verse because they are followed by their sons" [5]. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr preserves
the same exchange in blunter form — asked why the uncles were left out, they
answered: "because they describe her to their sons" [6]. The concern is not the
uncle. It is that the uncle has sons you could marry. Take it as a caution
rather than a ruling: the majority position is that uncles are mahrams.

**How much may be uncovered in front of a mahram.** Al-Nawawi: "as for a man
looking at his mahrams and their looking at him, the sound view is that it is
permitted above the navel and below the knee; and it is said that only what
appears in the course of work and daily activity is permitted" [7]. Qadi Khan
gives the Hanafi list concretely: "there is no harm in a man looking at his
mother, his adult daughter, his sister and every mahram of his — such as
grandmothers, grandchildren, paternal and maternal aunts — at her hair, her
chest, her head, her breast, her upper arm and her shin" [8]; but "he does not
look at her back, her belly, or what is between her navel and past the knee"
[9]. So: hair uncovered, arms and lower legs uncovered, and the trunk covered.

**The stricter reading, honestly reported.** Ibn 'Abbas took the verse to permit
much less: "the adornment she displays to these is her earrings, her necklace
and her bracelet; as for her anklet, her waist, her neck and her hair, she does
not display those except to her husband" [10] — and Ibn 'Abd al-Barr notes this
was also the position of Ibn Mas'ud, Mujahid, 'Ata' and al-Sha'bi. Against it he
sets the practice of the early Muslims and the working position of the jurists:
"on the view of these, the imams of fatwa in the cities hold that there is no
harm in a man looking at his mother's hair, and likewise the hair of mahrams —
the elderly rather than the young, and those from whom fitna is feared" [11].
Both positions are in the record. The second is the one the schools built on.

**The condition that governs all of it.** Qadi Khan attaches it directly:
"looking and touching these places is only permitted without desire — so if he
is in a state where, were he to look at that, he would desire, or his
predominant judgement is that he would desire, then he lowers his gaze and does
not touch her" [12]. Al-Nawawi states it at full strength: "looking with desire
is forbidden to everyone other than the husband..., so that it is forbidden for
a person to look at his mother or his daughter with desire" [13].

That is the shape of it. Being a mahram relaxes the covering; it does not
relax the gaze. And where a household has someone in it who is not a mahram —
a cousin, a brother-in-law, a stepfather's son — the covering applies there,
however familiar he is.
