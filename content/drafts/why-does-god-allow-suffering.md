# Why does God allow suffering?

## Answer

**An honest opening: the passages below do not contain a proof that resolves
this.** What the classical sources offer is a framework for what suffering *is*
and what it does — not an argument that explains it away. If you came looking for
the second thing, it is better to say so than to dress the first thing up as it.

**The starting claim.** Qur'an 64:11: **"No calamity befalleth save by Allah's
leave. And whosoever believeth in Allah, He guideth his heart."** [1]. Notice
the two halves. The first says nothing happens outside Allah's permission — which
is the part that makes the question urgent. The second is about the heart being
guided *through* it, which is where the sources put their weight.

**Suffering as a test with two possible responses.** Ibn Taymiyya:
**"وإذا كان الله تعالى قد يبتلى العبد من الحسنات والسيئات والسراء والضراء بما
يحصل معه شكره وصبره أم كفره وجزعه وطاعته أم معصيته"** — *Allah tests the servant
with good and ill, with ease and hardship, by which his gratitude and patience
emerge, or his ingratitude and panic; his obedience, or his disobedience* [2].
And he is explicit that this includes harm done by people, not only illness or
accident: **"وسواء في ذلك المصائب السمائية والمصائب التى تحصل بأفعال
الآدميين"** — *and it is the same in this regard whether the afflictions come
from the heavens or come about through the acts of human beings* [3].

That second point matters. The framework is not restricted to earthquakes and
disease; cruelty is inside it too. What it does not do is make the cruelty
acceptable — the same body of law that says this also punishes the person who
did it.

**What the sources say suffering accomplishes.** Muslim has a chapter for it —
"the reward of the believer for what befalls him of illness, or grief, or the
like, even a thorn that pricks him" [4] — and from 'A'isha:
**"ما يصيب المؤمن من شوكة فما فوقها إلا رفعه الله بها درجة، أو حط عنه بها
خطيئة"** — *no believer is struck by a thorn or anything greater but that Allah
raises him a degree by it, or removes a sin from him by it* [5]; and in another
wording, **"ما من شيء يصيب المؤمن حتى الشوكة تصيبه، إلا كتب الله له بها حسنة أو
حطت عنه بها خطيئة"** — *a good deed is written for him by it, or a sin is
dropped from him* [6].

Al-Tirmidhi extends it to the interior kinds: **"ما من شيء يصيب المؤمن من نصب
ولا حزن"** — *nothing befalls the believer of fatigue or grief* [7] — **"ولا
وصب، حتى الهم يهمه، إلا يكفر الله به عنه سيئاته"** — *nor sickness, not even a
worry that troubles him, but that Allah expiates his sins by it* [8]. Al-Tirmidhi
notes that anxiety being counted this way is reported only in that hadith, which
is the kind of remark that tells you the transmitters were being careful.

**And the balance the sources draw.** Ibn Hajar cites Suhayb: **"إن أصابته سراء
فشكر الله فله أجر وإن أصابته ضراء فصبر فله أجر فكل قضاء الله للمسلم خير"** — *if
ease comes to him and he thanks Allah, he has reward; and if hardship comes and
he is patient, he has reward — so every decree of Allah is good for the Muslim*
[9]. Both states are opportunities; neither is the good one.

**An honest complication the sources themselves record.** Ibn Hajar reports Abu
'Ubayda, visited when ill and told he had "spent the night with reward",
answering that he had not, and citing: **"من ابتلاه الله ببلاء في جسده فهو له
حطة"** — *whoever Allah afflicts with an illness in his body, it is a reduction
[of sins] for him* [10] — that is, expiation rather than reward. Ibn Hajar
discusses the tension and resolves it by tying the *reward* to the patience
rather than to the affliction itself. So even within the tradition, "suffering
earns you something" is stated with more care than it is usually repeated.

**What this does and does not answer.** It gives suffering a function — testing,
expiation, elevation [2][5][8] — and a promise that nothing in it is outside
Allah's knowledge and permission [1]. It does not explain why *this* person, or
why a child, or why on this scale. The Qur'an's own instruction to the Prophet ﷺ
in the face of it is not an explanation either: **"Endure thou patiently (O
Muhammad). Thine endurance is only by (the help of) Allah."** [11] — patience,
and even the patience is given rather than summoned.

**If you are asking from inside something.** Then the material at [7] and [8] is
the part addressed to you: fatigue, grief, sickness, and *even a worry that
troubles you* are named specifically, which is a strange thing for a religious
text to bother with unless the smallness of it was the point. Nothing above asks
you to feel fine. Grief, and saying so to Allah, is not a failure of faith —
what the sources warn against is *jaza'*, the collapse into despair [2], which is
a different thing from pain.

And if the suffering is being done to you by someone, none of this is a reason to
stay in it. The framework describes how to bear what cannot be changed; it is not
an instruction to accept harm that can be.

**On the language above:** "musiba" is a calamity or affliction; "sabr" is
patience or endurance; "jaza'" is panic or despair, the opposite of sabr; "ibtila'"
is testing; "hitta" is a reduction or dropping of sins [10]; "nasab", "wasab" and
"hamm" are fatigue, sickness and worry [7][8].

**A limit worth naming.** The philosophical problem — how an all-powerful and
merciful God is compatible with suffering — was discussed at length by Muslim
theologians, and these passages are not that discussion; Ibn Taymiyya's text at
[2] belongs to an argument about the decree rather than to a defence. This answer
reports what the sources say suffering *does*. If the question is a crisis rather
than an inquiry, please talk to someone — an imam, a counsellor, a doctor — and
not only to a page.
