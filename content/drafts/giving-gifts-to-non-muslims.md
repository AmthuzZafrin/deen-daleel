# Can I give gifts to my non-Muslim family on their holidays?

## Answer

Yes, and for family there is a hadith that settles it directly.

**The case of Asma'.**

Asma' bint Abi Bakr: "**my mother came to me, hoping [for something], and she was an idolater, during the treaty of Quraysh and their term with the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. So I said: Messenger of Allah, my mother has come to me hoping, and she is an idolater — shall I maintain ties with her?**" [1] — and he answered: *maintain ties with her.*

That is your situation almost exactly: a mother who is not Muslim, arriving in need or in hope, and a daughter who is not sure whether her new religion permits her to give. The answer was one word.

**And it is not confined to family.**

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr: "**as for voluntary charity, it is permissible to the people of disbelief — those who are related and others — and I know of no disagreement about that**" [2]. Not merely permitted but, in his phrase, without any recorded dissent.

**What giving is for.**

Al-Qurtubi: "**the gift is something recommended, and it is among the things that bequeath affection and remove enmity**" [3] — and he cites: "**shake hands and rancour will go; exchange gifts and you will love one another, and ill-will will go**" [4], and "**exchange gifts among yourselves, for the gift removes ill-feeling from the breast**" [5].

He adds a description that fits a family who have been awkward with each other since you became Muslim: gift-giving "**removes the roughnesses of the souls, and gains for the giver and the one given a warmth in meeting and in sitting together**" [6].

**The report people cite against this, and why it does not apply.**

There is a hadith in which the Prophet ﷺ says he has been forbidden *zabd al-mushrikin* — the gifts and grants of the idolaters. Al-Qurtubi handles it: "a group of the scholars held that there is abrogation in the two. **Others said: there is neither abrogating nor abrogated in them.** The meaning is that he would not accept a gift from one over whom he hoped for victory and the taking of his land and his entering Islam... **And this is the best interpretation the scholars have given here, because it brings the hadiths together**" [7].

Note two things. It is about *accepting*, not giving; and the interpretation al-Qurtubi calls best confines it to a wartime relationship between a commander and an enemy.

There is a genuine caution in the same literature, but it is about a different risk. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, discussing gifts to officials: "**if a gift is given to a man who is not a person of authority, in thanks for some good that came from him — I prefer that he not accept it, though it is not, in my view, forbidden to him if he accepts it and takes it**" [8]. That is a scruple about payment disguised as a present. It has nothing to do with a scarf for your aunt.

**On the timing.**

Nothing in these sources makes the date on which a gift is given the thing that decides it. What the jurists do guard is the *festival as a rite* — taking part in its worship, or in what was slaughtered for it. Handing your mother a present on the day she will actually be at home, surrounded by her family, is not that.

If it matters to you to keep the two apart, you have easy options and nobody will think worse of you for them: give it a day early, or give it at 'Id, or give with no occasion at all — which is arguably the better sunna, since it cannot be mistaken for anything.

**Practically.**

- **Give.** To parents and relatives above all, and the Asma' hadith is the authority.
- **Nothing here forbids the occasion.** But if you would rather not mark the festival itself, shift the day and say so kindly; the gesture is what your family will remember.
- **Avoid the specific contents that would be wrong to buy** — alcohol above all. Buying a bottle is participating in the thing, not the day.
- **Do not make them ask.** Asma's mother came *raghiba* — hoping. The version of this you want is the one where she never has to.
- **Remember what it is for.** *Exchange gifts and you will love one another, and ill-will will go.* A family that has watched you change religion has ill-will to lose, and the sunna's own prescription for that is in your hands.
