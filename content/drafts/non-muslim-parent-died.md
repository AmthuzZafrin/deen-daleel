# My non-Muslim parent died — can I pray for them?

## Answer

This is one of the few questions where the sources give a plain answer and the plain answer is hard, so it is better said early than buried. Asking God to forgive someone who has died outside of Islam is the one thing the Qur'an closes off by name — and it closes it off in the very verse that mentions close family, so the closeness is not an argument against it: "It is not for the Prophet, and those who believe, to pray for the forgiveness of idolaters even though they may be near of kin (to them) after it hath become clear that they are people of hell-fire." [1]

The thing worth understanding is **where the line falls**, because it does not fall where people assume. It falls at death. Al-Tabari glosses "after it hath become clear" as meaning "after they have died upon their association of others with God and their worship of idols" [2]. Qatada says the same of the Prophet and his uncle: "it became clear to him when he died, and he knew that repentance had been cut off from him" [3]. Ibn Hajar, weighing the reports about Abraham, cites Ibn 'Abbas through a chain he calls sound: "he sought forgiveness for him as long as he was alive; when he died, he stopped" [4]. And al-Qurtubi gives the reason outright — seeking forgiveness for the living is permitted, "because their faith is still hoped for" [5]. So the difference between what you could do a year ago and what you can do now is not about how much you loved them. It is that a case still open became closed.

Abraham is the example the Qur'an itself raises, and then limits. "The prayer of Abraham for the forgiveness of his father was only because of a promise he had promised him, but when it had become clear unto him that he (his father) was an enemy to Allah he (Abraham) disowned him" [6]. The same exception appears in the passage that calls him a pattern to follow — "save that which Abraham promised his father (when he said): I will ask forgiveness for thee, though I own nothing for thee from Allah" [7]. Ibn Hajar records that the commentators disagreed over exactly when Abraham withdrew, some placing it at Azar's death and some on the Day of Resurrection [8]; the report he grades soundest places it at death.

None of this was abstract for the people who first heard it. Al-Nasa'i places the Abu Talib narration under a chapter titled *the prohibition of seeking forgiveness for idolaters*: the Prophet at his uncle's deathbed, "Uncle, say *there is no god but God* — a word by which I may plead for you before God", the two men at the bedside pressing him the other way [9], and afterwards "I will surely ask forgiveness for you as long as I am not forbidden to" [10] — and then the verse came down. In the same chapter, 'Ali sees a man doing it and challenges him: "I heard a man asking forgiveness for his parents, and they were both idolaters. I said: do you ask forgiveness for them when they are idolaters? He said: did Abraham not ask forgiveness for his father?" [11] The most personal version is Abu Hurayra's, who buried a Muslim mother and an idolater father and drew the line across his own family out loud: "God have mercy on a man who asks forgiveness for Abu Hurayra and for his mother. I said: and for his father? He said: no. My father died an idolater." [12] Al-Dahhak reduces the whole thing to four words — "if they died as idolaters" [13].

**What this does not settle is nearly everything else**, and the works on this sheet are uneven about the rest. Al-Qurtubi reads the verse widely, as cutting off alliance with disbelievers "living and dead", so that "seeking forgiveness for an idolater is among what is not permitted" [14]. But the act named is a petition for forgiveness, not every act of a child toward a parent.

On the funeral itself the sheet gives one direct statement. 'Ata' b. Abi Rabah held that the verse covers the funeral prayer as well: "the verse concerns the prohibition of praying over idolaters, and *istighfar* here means the prayer" [15] — a reading that lines up with "And never (O Muhammad) pray for one of them who dieth, nor stand by his grave" [16]. Consistent with that, the funeral prayer as these works describe it is something Muslims owe Muslims: Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, on the Prophet praying in absence over the Negus, says it is not permitted "to leave a Muslim's funeral without prayer, nor is it lawful for one present to bury him without praying over him" [17]. And the greeting the Prophet taught at the graves is addressed by name to the believing dead — "Peace be upon you, people of these dwellings, of the believers and the Muslims" [18].

What these passages do **not** answer: whether you attend the funeral, whether you may help wash or carry or bury, what you do at a service held in a church or a crematorium, and what you say to relatives who expect you to pray aloud with them. The works retrieved here rule on the petition and on the funeral prayer, and stop. Those are exactly the questions a person in this situation actually has, and they turn on facts — what the ceremony involves, what you would be taken to be affirming — that no book can supply. Put them to a scholar who will hear the details.

One thing is worth saying against the impression the above may leave. Among the lessons Ibn Hajar draws from the Prophet's conduct at the burial of 'Abdullah b. Ubayy is "the obedient living person's regard, through kindness, for the disobedient dead" [19]. That case is not the same as yours — Ibn Ubayy professed Islam outwardly, and Ibn Hajar notes in the same passage that the outward rulings of Islam were applied to him for that reason — so it does not transfer as a ruling. But it does show that in the sources, being barred from one prayer is not read as being released from every duty of decency. Nothing here forbids you to grieve, to speak well of them, to keep what you promised them, or to look after the people they left behind.

Finally, the verse most often produced against all of this. The Prophet is remembered saying, "O God, forgive my people, for they do not know." Al-Qurtubi raises the objection himself and answers it: that saying "was by way of narrating one who came before him among the prophets" [20] — the Prophet was quoting an earlier prophet's words, not making the supplication in his own name, and al-Qurtubi points to the sound narrations that carry it in exactly that form. It is not a licence.

A last word on register. The sources here are setting a limit on what you may *ask for*, in a matter they treat as already decided elsewhere and not by you. That is a genuinely painful thing to be told, and it is not made less painful by being clearly evidenced. If you are carrying it, the next step is a person, not a page.
