# How do Muslims respond to questions about Aisha's age?

## Answer

**What the reports actually say.** In her own words: `تزوجني رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم وأنا بنت ست سنين ودخل علي وأنا بنت تسع سنين وكنت ألعب بالبنات` [1] — the Messenger of God married me when I was six, and consummated the marriage with me when I was nine, and I used to play with dolls. Al-Nawawi records a variant putting the contract at seven [2]. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr sets out the range the reports give and names the strongest: `وقد تزوجها رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم وهي بنت ست سنين أو سبع سنين ودخل بها وهي ابنة تسع أو عشر سنين` [3]. Nothing in this corpus proposes a materially older age. The revisionist chronologies argued today — reconstructions from her sister's dates or from the dating of her conversion — are modern and are not found in these books, so nothing here confirms or refutes them.

**What the jurists built on it, and it is narrower than either its defenders or its critics usually say.** The consensus reported is about the *contract*, not about consummation. Ibn al-Mundhir, as Ibn Qudama transmits him: `قال ابن المنذر أجمع كل من نحفظ عنه من أهل العلم، أن نكاح الأب ابنته البكر الصغيرة جائز، إذا زوجها من كفء` [4] — that a father may contract his minor virgin daughter in marriage to a suitable match. Al-Nawawi says the same and immediately reports a live split over whether she can undo it: `وأجمع المسلمون على جواز تزويجه بنته البكر الصغيرة لهذا الحديث وإذا بلغت فلا خيار لها في فسخه عند مالك والشافعي وسائر فقهاء الحجاز وقال أهل العراق لها الخيار إذا بلغت` [5] — the Hijazis say she has no option to dissolve it on reaching majority; the Iraqis say she does. Al-Quduri states the Hanafi rule: `فإن زوجهما الأب أو الجد فلا خيار لهما بعد بلوغهما وإن زوجهما غير الأب والجد فلكل واحد منهما الخيار إذا بلغ إن شاء أقام على النكاح وإن شاء فسخ` [6] — married off by anyone other than father or grandfather, the minor may repudiate the marriage on reaching puberty.

The contract is one thing; the consummation is separately gated, and not on age. Ibn Battal, as Ibn Hajar reports him: `يجوز تزويج الصغيرة بالكبير اجماعا ولو كانت في المهد لكن لا يمكن منها حتى تصلح للوطء` [7] — the contract is permitted by consensus even for an infant in the cradle, **but she is not given over until she is fit for intercourse**. Whatever one makes of that as a standard, it is a standard of physical capacity, and it is not the number nine.

**Ibn Hajar himself declines to use this episode to establish the rule.** Ibn Battal had inferred from the hadith that a father marries off his minor virgin daughter without asking her. Ibn Hajar's response: `وليس بواضح الدلالة بل يحتمل أن يكون ذلك قبل ورود الامر باستئذان البكر وهو الظاهر فان القصة وقعت بمكة قبل الهجرة` [8] — the inference is not clear; it may well be, and this is the apparent reading, that the episode preceded the command to seek a virgin's permission, since it happened in Mecca before the Hijra. The leading commentator on al-Bukhari is saying, in effect, that this marriage may sit *before* the rule rather than being its foundation. That is a point from inside the tradition and it is rarely quoted on either side of the modern argument.

**The command he is referring to.** `تستأمر النساء في أبضاعهن قالت قلت يا رسول الله إنهن يستحيين قال الأيم أحق بنفسها والبكر تستأمر وسكوتها إقرارها` [9] — women are to be consulted about their marriages; 'A'isha objected that they are too shy to speak, and was told the previously-married woman has the greater right over herself, and the virgin is consulted, her silence being her assent. Whether this reaches the father was itself disputed. Malik was asked directly and said it did not: `سألت مالكا عن قول النبي عليه السلام والبكر تستأذن في نفسها أيصيب هذا القول الأب قال لا لم يعن الأب بهذا إنما عني به` [10] — no, the father is not meant by it. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr states the majority position and grounds it in this very marriage: `أجمع العلماء على أن للأب أن يزوج ابنته الصغيرة ولا يشاورها لتزويج رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم عائشة وهي بنت ست سنين إلا أن العراقيين قالوا لها الخيار إذا بلغت وأبى ذلك أهل الحجاز` [11].

**One jurist did try to read a minimum age out of this story, and the tradition did not follow him.** The view was that a girl married below nine should not be consummated with until she reaches nine. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr's comment: `فإن زوجت صغيرة دون تسع سنين فلا أرى أن يدخل بها حتى تبلغ تسع سنين قال أبو عمر لا أعلم أحدا قاله غيره وأظنه أخذه من قصة عائشة في الدخول` [12] — I know of no one else who said it, and I think he took it from 'A'isha's case. So: a single jurist treated the episode as fixing a floor, and the reporter notes he stood alone.

The majority went the other way, and explicitly denied that nine is a threshold. Ibn Qudama on the mainstream position of Malik, al-Shafi'i, Abu Hanifa and the rest: `حكم بنت تسع سنين، حكم بنت ثمان ; لأنها غير بالغة، ولأن إذنها لا يعتبر في سائر التصرفات، فكذلك في النكاح` [13] — a nine-year-old is judged exactly as an eight-year-old, because she has not reached majority and her consent is not operative in any other transaction either. He records a second Hanbali narration going the other way, `والرواية الثانية، حكمها حكم البالغة` [14], resting partly on a statement attributed to 'A'isha herself: `إذا بلغت الجارية تسع سنين فهي امرأة` [15]. The sources do not speak with one voice on this.

**And "majority" itself was contested across a wide range.** Al-Qurtubi: menstruation and pregnancy are agreed signs — `فأما الحيض والحبل فلم يختلف العلماء في أنه بلوغ وأن الفرائض والأحكام تجب بهما` [16] — while for one who has shown no sign, al-Awza'i, al-Shafi'i and Ibn Hanbal set `خمس عشرة سنة بلوغ لمن لم يحتلم` [17], fifteen years. Ibn Hajar records the full spread: `وقال أبو حنيفة سن البلوغ تسع عشرة أو ثمان عشرة للغلام وسبع عشرة للجارية وقال أكثر المالكية حده فيهما سبع عشرة أو ثمان عشرة وقال الشافعي وأحمد وابن وهب والجمهور حده فيهما استكمال خمس عشرة سنة` [18] — nineteen or eighteen for a boy and seventeen for a girl according to Abu Hanifa, seventeen or eighteen for most Malikis, fifteen for al-Shafi'i, Ahmad and the majority. So the category of adulthood in these books runs anywhere from the onset of menses to eighteen or nineteen, and the jurists never resolved it. Ibn Hajar also reports, in the course of that discussion, a case offered as evidence of how early the body could move: `حاضت باستكمال تسع ووضعت بنتا لاستكمال عشر` [19] — she menstruated at the completion of nine and bore a daughter at the completion of ten.

**What this corpus cannot answer.** No work here addresses the modern question at all — child development, the capacity of a child to consent, statutory ages of marriage, or child protection as a field. The question was never put to these authors, so they are not answering it well or badly; they are not answering it. Whether a marriage of this kind would be permissible now is a question for living scholars working with the legal systems people actually live under, and every modern Muslim jurisdiction that has legislated a minimum marriage age has done so on grounds that are not in these books. Nor does this corpus contain any of the apologetic reconstructions that circulate today, and it would be dishonest to present them from here as though it did.

**One thing that does belong in an accurate account and is usually missing from both sides.** Ibn Hajar, closing his notice on her: she taught for close to fifty years, `فأكثر الناس الاخذ عنها ونقلوا عنها من الاحكام والآداب شيئا كثيرا حتى قيل إن ربع الأحكام الشرعية منقول عنها` [20] — people took from her in great numbers and transmitted from her a vast amount of law and conduct, so much that it was said a quarter of the rulings of the *shari'a* come through her. That does not settle the moral question and should not be offered as if it did. But someone forming a picture of who she was in this tradition should know that she is one of its principal sources, not a passive figure in someone else's biography.
