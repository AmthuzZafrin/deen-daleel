# Is birth control allowed?

## Answer

**Short answer: the classical position on spacing or avoiding pregnancy is
permission, and it rests on a strong hadith basis — with one condition the
sources are firm about, that a wife's consent is required.** Whether every modern
method inherits that permission is a separate question the classical texts cannot
answer for you.

**The evidence.** Muslim heads a chapter **"باب حكم العزل"**, "the ruling on
'azl" — withdrawal [1] — and under it reports Jabir: **"كنا نعزل، والقرآن
ينزل"** — *we used to practise withdrawal while the Qur'an was being revealed*
[2]. Sufyan's inference, transmitted with the report:
**"لو كان شيئا ينهى عنه لنهانا عنه القرآن"** — *had it been something to be
forbidden, the Qur'an would have forbidden it to us* [3]. Another wording is more
explicit still: **"كنا نعزل على عهد رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم، فبلغ ذلك نبي
الله صلى الله عليه وسلم، فلم ينهنا"** — *we practised it in the time of the
Messenger of Allah ﷺ, word of it reached him, and he did not forbid us* [4].

And a man who asked directly, wanting to avoid a pregnancy, was answered:
**"اعزل عنها إن شئت، فإنه سيأتيها ما قدر لها"** — *withdraw from her if you wish,
for what has been decreed for her will come to her* [5]. Note both halves: the
permission, and the reminder that the outcome is not ultimately in your hands.
The same report ends with the pregnancy happening anyway.

**The report that pulls the other way, since you will meet it.** Al-Nawawi
records the hadith of Judama: **"ثم سألوه عن العزل فقال رسول الله صلى الله عليه
وسلم ذلك الوأد الخفي"** — *then they asked him about withdrawal and he said: that
is the hidden burying-alive* [6]. He does not suppress the tension; he introduces
the legal discussion with **"أما الأحكام فقد اختلف السلف في حكم العزل"** — *as
for the rulings, the early generations differed over the ruling on withdrawal*
[7] — and the discussion that follows in the fiqh works proceeds on the basis
that it is permitted. Al-Ghazali's position is reported as the settled one:
**"وقال الغزالي يجوز العزل، وهو المصحح عند المتأخرين"** — *al-Ghazali said
withdrawal is permitted, and this is what the later scholars declared correct*
[8].

**The condition the sources insist on: her consent.** Ibn Qudama states it
plainly: **"ولا يعزل عن زوجته الحرة إلا بإذنها"** — *he does not withdraw from
his wife except with her permission* [9], citing the report from 'Umar:
**"نهى رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم أن يعزل عن الحرة إلا بإذنها"** [10]. And he
gives the reason, which is worth reading as a statement about her standing:
**"ولأن لها في الولد حقا، وعليها في العزل ضرر، فلم يجز إلا بإذنها"** — *because
she has a right in the child, and withdrawal causes her harm, so it is not
permitted except with her permission* [11]. Al-Nawawi reports Ibn 'Abd al-Barr
claiming consensus on the point: **"فقال ابن عبد البر لا خلاف بين العلماء أنه لا
يعزل عن الزوجة الحرة إلا بإذنها، لان الجماع من حقها"** — *there is no
disagreement among the scholars that he may not withdraw from his free wife
except with her permission, because intercourse is her right* [7]. Ibn Hajar
notes the Shafi'i school reasoned differently on the premise —
**"وتعقب بان المعروف عند الشافعية أن المرأة لا حق لها في الجماع أصلا"** [12] —
which is a dispute about the grounds rather than about whether the decision
should be joint in practice.

The upshot: on this material, contraception is a decision for the couple
together [7][9], and a husband deciding it unilaterally is the case the sources
object to [11].

**A related report on spacing.** Al-Nawawi records the Prophet ﷺ considering a
prohibition on intercourse with a nursing wife and deciding against it:
**"لقد هممت أن أنهى عن الغيلة حتى ذكرت أن الروم وفارس يصنعون ذلك فلا يضر
أولادهم"** — *I had considered forbidding intercourse with a nursing woman, until
I recalled that the Byzantines and Persians do it and it does not harm their
children* [13]; al-Nawawi's comment: **"وفي الحديث جواز الغيلة فإنه صلى الله
عليه وسلم لم ينه عنها وبين سبب ترك النهى"** — *the hadith establishes its
permissibility, since he did not forbid it and explained why he refrained from
forbidding it* [14]. Two things are worth noticing there. The reasoning is
empirical — he observed that the feared harm did not in fact occur — and a
prohibition was set aside on that basis.

**What transfers to modern methods, and what does not.** Every text above is
about a reversible act that prevents conception. Contemporary scholars reason
from that to reversible methods — barrier methods, hormonal contraception, the
coil — and the mainstream position among them is permission, particularly for
spacing births or where pregnancy would carry risk. That reasoning is theirs,
not these texts', and it is stated here as a report rather than as a ruling.

Two things are genuinely different questions and are **not** covered by anything
above. **Permanent sterilisation** is not reversible, which is the feature the
'azl evidence turns on, and scholars treat it separately and more restrictively.
**Abortion** concerns an existing pregnancy rather than preventing one, is
governed by an entirely different body of fiqh, and nothing in this answer bears
on it. Do not read the permission here across to either.

**So, practically.** Deciding together to space your children or to wait is
within what the sources permit [2][4][8], and the decision needs to be a joint
one [9][11]. If a specific method matters to you — because of health, because of
how it works, or because someone has told you it is forbidden — ask a scholar
about that method specifically, and a doctor about the medicine. And the note
at [5] is worth keeping: taking the means is permitted, and the outcome still
is not yours to guarantee.

**On the language above:** "'azl" is coitus interruptus, withdrawal; "ghila" is
intercourse with a nursing wife; "al-wa'd al-khafi" is the phrase in the Judama
report, "the hidden burying-alive", referring to the pre-Islamic practice of
burying infant daughters.

**A caution.** The early generations differed on this [7], the reconciliation of
the two sets of reports is a matter of interpretation, and the application to
modern methods is a contemporary judgement rather than something these works
decide. For your own circumstances — especially if there is a medical factor or a
disagreement between you and your spouse — ask a scholar who can hear the
details.
