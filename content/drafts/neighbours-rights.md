# What are my neighbours' rights over me?

## Answer

'A'isha reported the Prophet ﷺ saying: "**Jibril kept on urging me about the neighbour until I thought he would make him an heir**" [1]. Al-Tirmidhi grades the same report *hasan sahih* [2].

That is the register the whole subject is in. And the negative side is put as sharply: "**He will not enter Paradise whose neighbour is not safe from his harms**" [3]. Al-Qurtubi cites the version from Abu Shurayh where the Prophet ﷺ swore first and explained after: "*By Allah, he does not believe. By Allah, he does not believe. By Allah, he does not believe.* It was said: Messenger of Allah, who? He said: *The one whose neighbour is not safe from his harms*" [4].

Ibn Hajar reads the force of it correctly: "he ﷺ negated faith from one whose neighbour is not safe from his harms… and it is an emphatic expression conveying the magnification of the neighbour's right, and that harming him is among the major sins" [5].

**Who counts as your neighbour — including the non-Muslim one.**

Ibn al-'Arabi: "the sanctity of the neighbour is immense, in the Jahiliyya and in Islam — rationally grounded, legislated, a matter of decency and of religion. The Prophet ﷺ said: *Jibril kept on urging me about the neighbour until I thought he would make him an heir*; and he said: *Whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him honour his neighbour*" [6].

And the graded scheme, which answers the question people most often ask: "**Neighbours are three.** One who has three rights: the Muslim neighbour who is a relative — he has the right of neighbourliness, the right of kinship and the right of Islam. One who has two rights: the Muslim neighbour, who has the right of Islam and the right of neighbourliness. And one who has one right: the disbeliever — he has the right of neighbourliness" [7].

So a non-Muslim neighbour has a *right* over you, not merely tolerance. Al-Qurtubi states it plainly: "urging concerning the neighbour is commanded and recommended, **whether he is a Muslim or a disbeliever** — and this is the correct view. And kindness may be by way of material support, and may be by way of good company, withholding harm, and defending him" [8].

'Abdullah b. 'Amr, one of the narrators of the Jibril hadith, applied it exactly that way: "he understood the hadith generally — so when a sheep was slaughtered for him, he ordered that some of it be given as a gift to his Jewish neighbour" [9].

**How near is a neighbour?** Ibn al-'Arabi: "the furthest, in al-Zuhri's view, is one between whom and you are forty houses. And it is said the distant one is he who adjoins you by a wall, and the near one he who adjoins you by his door — because of the Prophet's ﷺ words to a man who said *I have two neighbours; to which of them shall I give a gift?* He said: *To the one nearer to you by door*" [10].

**What the right actually consists of.**

Ibn Hajar gives the practical list: "fulfilment of the injunction concerning him is attained by conveying the various kinds of kindness to him according to capacity — **such as a gift, and the greeting, and a cheerful face when meeting him, and enquiring after his condition, and assisting him in what he needs**, and other than that; and by withholding the causes of harm from him, in all their kinds, whether physical or non-physical" [11].

That is a workable list for a modern street: greet them, be pleasant, notice when something is wrong, help when you can, send food, and above all do not be the cause of their difficulty — noise, parking, rubbish, a blocked path, an unrepaired leak.

Abu Dharr transmits the simplest instruction in the corpus on this: "**O Abu Dharr, when you cook a broth, increase its water and take care of your neighbours**" [12]. In another wording: "when you cook a broth, increase its water, then look to a household among your neighbours and give them some of it in a good way" [13].

That is a low bar, deliberately. It costs nothing, and it makes you the sort of person your street is glad to have.

**Neighbours who are difficult.**

Ibn Hajar addresses the case directly, distinguishing the righteous neighbour from the one who is not: "what covers all of them is wanting good for him, admonishing him in the best way, supplicating for his guidance, and abandoning harm to him — except in the place where harming him is obligatory, in word or deed" [14].

Note what is common to *every* neighbour on that list: want good for them, and do not harm them. Their conduct changes what else you owe; it does not release you from those two.

**And a limit, honestly stated.**

Ibn al-'Arabi is unusually candid about how far the obligation is enforceable. On the hadith "let none of you prevent his neighbour from fixing a beam in his wall": "**all the scholars have held that this is recommended rather than obligatory, and that refusing it is disliked rather than forbidden** — because everyone has the better right to his own property, and the owner needs the wall; if he gives it, his property is diminished, and if he lends it, he is burdened with safeguarding it by taking witnesses, and harms himself. So if he wishes to bear that for him, he has the reward; and if he refuses, there is no sin upon him" [15].

Set that against the Jibril hadith and you get the true shape: the neighbour's right is enormous in the register of piety and character, while much of it is not something a court will compel. Which means it is the sort of obligation you keep because of who you want to be, not because someone can make you.

One right that *is* legal in some schools: "**the neighbour has the better claim by proximity**" [16] — the basis for a neighbour's pre-emption right over an adjoining property sale. Al-Qurtubi notes this was contested and that Abu Hanifa and others differed over its scope.

**In practice.** Learn their names. Say salam. Send food when you cook. Notice the elderly one who hasn't been seen for a few days. Keep your noise, your bins and your building work from becoming their problem. And do all of it regardless of whether they are Muslim — a right of neighbourliness is theirs by being your neighbour.
