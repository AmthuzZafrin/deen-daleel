# Can a Muslim woman marry a non-Muslim man?

## Answer

No. This is one of the few marriage questions with no disagreement behind it, and it is worth being
straightforward about that rather than leaving you to discover it later.

Al-Qurtubi, commenting on the verse: **"that is, do not marry a Muslim woman to an idolater — and the
community has agreed that an idolater may not have marital relations with a believing woman in any way,
because of the diminishment of Islam in that"** [1].

### The verse

**"And give not your daughters in marriage to idolaters till they believe, for lo! a believing slave is
better than an idolater though he please you"** [2].

Al-Tabari reads the scope as total: **"God has forbidden believing women to marry an idolater — whoever
the idolater may be, of whatever kind of idolatry"** [3]. And the early authorities applied it
explicitly to Jews and Christians. Qatada and al-Zuhri on this verse: **"it is not lawful for you to
marry a Jew, or a Christian, or an idolater not of your religion"** [4]. 'Ikrima and al-Hasan al-Basri:
**"He forbade Muslim women to their men — meaning the men of the idolaters"** [5].

### Why the man's case is different

The obvious objection is that a Muslim man *may* marry a Jewish or Christian woman. The answer the
jurists give is grammatical and structural, not a claim about worth.

Ibn Taymiyya points to the wording: **"and He distinguished between His saying 'do not give in marriage
to idolaters' and His saying 'do not marry idolatresses' — and this distinction is among what some of
the early authorities of the Prophet's household argued from"** [6]. The permission in Surat al-Ma'ida is
phrased for men marrying, and was never read as reciprocal. The prohibition on giving daughters in
marriage was never limited to idolaters in the narrow sense — as [4] and [5] show, the earliest readers
applied it to the People of the Book too.

The reason given by al-Qurtubi is *al-ghadada 'ala al-Islam* [1] — a diminishment of the religion's
standing. In a household, the classical scholars assumed, the man's religion governs the children and
the practice of the home. A Muslim wife of a Christian husband keeps her faith by his permission; a
Christian wife of a Muslim husband is guaranteed hers by his law. That is the asymmetry the rule
encodes, and whether or not it maps onto every modern household, it is the reasoning actually given.

### What the verse offers instead

Al-Tabari's paraphrase of the second half, which is the part usually skipped: **"and that you marry them
to a believing slave who affirms God and His Messenger and what he brought from God is better for you
than that you marry them to a free idolater — even if his lineage is noble and his origin honourable,
and even if his standing and descent please you"** [7].

The verse anticipates exactly the situation people are usually in when they ask this: a good man, by
every worldly measure, whom the family would be pleased with. The verse does not deny that he is
impressive. It says that is not the measure.

### If you are already in this situation

The ruling is what it is, but the pastoral reality is not simple, and it is not settled by a webpage.

If you are married to a non-Muslim man and have since become Muslim, that is a different question with
its own body of law — the classical books treat conversion during a marriage separately from contracting
one, and there is real scholarly discussion of waiting periods and outcomes. Do not assume you know the
answer from this page. Take it to a scholar who will hear your actual circumstances.

If you are considering such a marriage, the honest position is that the tradition is unanimous against
it, and that no school offers a route. Anyone telling you otherwise is not reporting the classical
material.
