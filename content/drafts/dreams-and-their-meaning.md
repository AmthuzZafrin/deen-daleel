# Do dreams mean anything in Islam?

## Answer

Some do. Most don't. The Prophet ﷺ sorted dreams into three kinds and only one of them means anything — and the practical advice attached to the other two is to stop taking them seriously, not to look them up.

**The three kinds.**

Abu Hurayra reported, in a report al-Tirmidhi graded *sahih*: "the truest of them in dreams are the truest of them in speech; and the dream of a Muslim is one part of forty-six parts of prophethood. Dreams are three: the good dream is glad tidings from Allah; a dream is from the grieving of Shaytan; and a dream is from what a man tells himself. So when one of you sees what he dislikes, let him get up and spit lightly, and let him not tell people of it" [1].

'Awf b. Malik's version, which al-Qurtubi cites, puts the same division in a different order: "dreams are three: among them are the terrors of Shaytan, to grieve the son of Adam; among them is what a man is preoccupied with in waking, so he sees it in his sleep; and among them is one part of forty-six parts of prophethood" [2].

Note what the middle category is. Your mind processing your own preoccupations — the exam, the argument, the person you have been thinking about — is a named category in the hadith. It is not a message. It does not need decoding.

Al-Qurtubi defines the first category by what it excludes: "the dream attributed to Allah is the one that is free of confused jumbles and vain imaginings, and whose interpretation accords with what is in the Preserved Tablet; and the one that is of the report of jumbles is the *hulm*, which is attributed to Shaytan" [3].

**If you had a nightmare.**

This is the most practically useful material in the whole subject, and it is short. Abu Qatada reported: "Dreams are from Allah, and the *hulm* is from Shaytan. So when one of you has a dream he dislikes, let him blow lightly to his left three times, and seek refuge in Allah from its evil — it will not harm him" [4]. One narration adds: "and let him turn over from the side he was on" [5]. And al-Tirmidhi's version adds the part people most need: do not tell it to anyone [1].

Then take the testimony of Abu Salama, who transmitted the hadith and reported his own experience with it: "I used to see a dream heavier upon me than a mountain — and it was no more than that I heard this hadith, and I no longer cared about it" [6].

That is the intended effect. The instruction is not a ritual that neutralises an omen; it is a way of refusing to carry the thing. A frightening dream is not a prophecy, not a punishment, and not a sign that something bad is coming. Spit lightly to your left, seek refuge, roll over, tell nobody, and let it go.

**"One part of forty-six parts of prophethood" — what that does and does not mean.**

The number varies across reports, and the scholars noticed. Al-Nawawi lists forty-six, forty-five, seventy, forty, forty-nine, fifty, twenty-six and forty-four. Al-Qurtubi grades them: "the sound one among them is the hadith of forty-six, and following it in soundness is the hadith of seventy — and Muslim did not include in his *Sahih* other than these two" [7].

Al-Tabari reconciled them by the state of the dreamer: "this variation goes back to the difference in the condition of the one seeing — so the righteous believer's dream is one part of forty-six parts, and the corrupt person's one part of seventy" [8]. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr agrees and makes the mechanism explicit: "it may be that the good dream, from some of those who see it, is according to what there is of truthfulness of speech, discharge of trust, firm religion and goodness of certainty — so in the measure of people's difference in what we have described, dreams come to them in the differing numbers of parts; so whoever's intention in worshipping his Lord is pure, and his certainty, and his truthfulness of speech, his dream is truer and closer to prophethood" [9].

That is the same thing the hadith itself says — "the truest of them in dreams are the truest of them in speech" [1]. Truthful dreams are downstream of a truthful life. They are not a shortcut around one.

Now the limits. Al-Khattabi: "it was only a part of the parts of prophethood in respect of the prophets rather than others — the prophets, peace be upon them, were given revelation in their sleep as they were given it in waking" [10]. Al-Mazari offers a narrower reading still: "it may be that what is meant is that in the dream there is report of the unseen, and that is one of the fruits of prophethood — and it is not within the definition of prophethood" [11].

So the fraction is not a licence. A dream is not revelation, carries no authority, and cannot establish a ruling, permit what is forbidden, or tell you what Allah wants of you. Al-Qurtubi notes the reach of the point from the other end: a true dream can come to a non-Muslim — "it is possible that the dream of the people of shirk be a true dream, as the dream of the two youths was true; except that it is not permissible to attribute it to prophethood as the believer's dream is attributed to it" [12].

**On interpretation.**

The Qur'an takes dream interpretation seriously — Yusuf's story turns on it. But look at how the classical scholars actually interpret, because it is not what dream dictionaries do.

Al-Nawawi on the Prophet ﷺ's stated preference for seeing fetters and dislike of seeing a shackle: "he only liked the fetter because it is on the two legs, and it is a restraint from sins and evils and kinds of falsehood; whereas the shackle, its place is the neck, and it is a description of the people of the Fire" [13]. That is not a lookup. It is a reason, drawn from what the object does and from a Qur'anic association. Ibn Hajar works the same way: silk in a dream "may here be interpreted as the nobility of religion and knowledge, because silk is the noblest of the garments of this world, and likewise knowledge of the religion is the noblest of the sciences" [14].

Al-Qurtubi records Ibn al-'Arabi's constraint on the raw material: "nothing is seen in a dream except what is sound to perceive in waking — and for that reason one does not see in a dream a person standing and sitting at once; one sees only the possible and the customary" [15].

The practical upshot: the tradition has a genuine art of interpretation, exercised by people with knowledge who reason from the dreamer's state, the Qur'an, and the language. What it does not have is a table where "snake = enemy" regardless of who dreamt it. If a dream genuinely troubles you, take it to someone of learning and character who knows you — and if they tell you they can read anyone's dreams from a book, that is a reason to stop.

**Do not tell a troubling dream around.**

The Qur'an makes this a plot point. Ya'qub told Yusuf not to relate his dream to his brothers, and al-Qurtubi glosses the reason: they would "contrive your destruction, because its interpretation is apparent — so Shaytan might carry them to intending harm against you at that moment" [16]. A dream told is a dream let loose among people who will have their own reactions to it. The hadith's instruction on the bad dream is simply "let him not tell people of it" [1].

**Seeing the Prophet ﷺ.**

People ask about this most of all, and it is worth knowing that the scholars did not agree on what the relevant hadith even means. Ibn Hajar sets out the options: al-Qadi 'Iyad reports that "it was said its meaning is that he will see the interpretation of that dream in wakefulness, and its truth; and it was said the meaning of the vision in wakefulness is that he will see him in the Hereafter" [17] — and Ibn Hajar records objections to that second reading as well. So even here, no confident single meaning is available.

**In short.** Dreams are one part of forty-six parts of something, in the sense that they can carry glad tidings — the Prophet ﷺ said "nothing remains of the glad tidings after me except the good, true dream that a righteous man sees, or that is seen for him" [18]. Take a good dream as encouragement, thank Allah, and tell only someone who wishes you well. Take a bad one as nothing: spit, seek refuge, turn over, say nothing. And take the ordinary residue of the day for what it is. Nothing you dreamt changes what you owe Allah tomorrow morning, and no dream outranks a prayer you can pray while awake.

Qur'an 19:45 — Surah Maryam, verse 45 of chapter 19

Translation (Marmaduke Pickthall):
O my father! Lo! I fear lest a punishment from the Beneficent overtake thee so that thou become a comrade of the devil.
