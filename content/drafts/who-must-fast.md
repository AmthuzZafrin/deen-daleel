# Who has to fast and who's excused?

## Answer

**Every adult Muslim of sound mind who is able. The excuses are: illness, travel,
menstruation and post-natal bleeding, pregnancy and nursing where there is harm,
and permanent inability through old age or chronic illness.**

The first four make up days later. The last one does not — it pays *fidya*
instead.

**Illness.**

Ibn Qudama: **the people of knowledge are agreed on the permissibility of breaking
the fast for the sick person in general** [1].

But not every ailment counts. He sets the bar: **the illness that permits breaking
the fast is the severe one that is increased by fasting, or where a slowing of
recovery is feared** [2]. A toothache or a cut finger, he says explicitly, is not
enough. Ahmad was asked when the sick person breaks his fast: **when he is not
able** [3] — and asked whether a fever qualified, he answered that no illness is
more severe than a fever.

And where the illness *is* serious, fasting anyway is not admirable. Ibn Qudama:
**if the sick person bears it and fasts despite this, he has done something
disliked — because of the harm to himself it involves, and his abandoning God's
lightening and the acceptance of His dispensation** [4]. The fast still counts if
he does it. But he was wrong to.

**Permanent inability — old age and chronic illness.**

This is the category that does not make up days. Ibn Qudama: **the elderly man and
the elderly woman, when fasting exhausts them and is severely difficult for them,
may break the fast and feed a poor person for each day** [5]. And the same for
illness with no prospect of recovery: **the sick person whose recovery is not
hoped for breaks the fast and feeds a poor person for each day, because he is in
the meaning of the elderly** [6].

The dividing line is whether you will ever be able to make the days up. Ibn
Qudama: **recourse is had to the fidya only upon despairing of the make-up** [7].
So a diabetic whose condition is managed and who can fast in winter owes days, not
fidya; someone with a permanent condition owes fidya. See the separate answers on
fidya and on making up fasts.

**The others, in brief** — each has its own answer here:

| Excused | What is owed |
|---|---|
| Illness (temporary) | Make up the days |
| Travel | Make up the days |
| Menstruation, post-natal bleeding | Make up the days — and this is obligatory, not optional |
| Pregnancy, nursing, where harm is feared | Make up the days; some schools add fidya |
| Old age, permanent illness | Fidya. No make-up |

**Who is not obliged at all:** children before puberty (they are encouraged to try
part-days, not required), and anyone who has permanently lost mental capacity.

**A word on judging your own case.** The books put the threshold at real harm, not
discomfort — hunger, thirst, tiredness and headache are the fast working, not
grounds to break it. But they also treat pushing through genuine illness as a
fault, not a virtue. If you have a medical condition, the decision belongs with
your doctor and a scholar who knows your situation together; neither one alone has
the whole picture.
