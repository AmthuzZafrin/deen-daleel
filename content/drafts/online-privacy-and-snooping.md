# Is it haram to read someone's messages or track them?

> Reviewer note: Tajassus is explicit and the reports on looking into a house without permission are unusually blunt. Note that a parent's duty of care is a real consideration the reader should raise with a scholar.


## Answer

**This is one of the places where the sources are blunter than most people expect, and where the strongest reports are about a caliph being told to stop.** The prohibition is explicit, the reasoning behind it is stated, and the best-known applications of it are cases where someone had *good grounds* to suspect and was told that made no difference.

**The verse, and what the word means.**

`ولا تجسسوا` — do not spy. Al-Qurtubi's definition: `لأن التجسس البحث عما يكتم عنك` [1] — tajassus is searching out what is concealed from you. His reading of the command is direct: `خذوا ما   ظهر ولا تتبعوا عورات المسلمين أي لا يبحث أحدكم عن عيب أخيه حتى يطلع عليه بعد أن ستره الله` [2] — take what is apparent and do not pursue people's hidden faults; let none of you search out his brother's flaw until he uncovers what God had covered.

Note the last clause. The concealment is treated as *God's* doing, and going looking is treated as undoing it.

**The reason given, which is not what you would guess.**

The stated harm is not embarrassment. It is that surveillance ruins the people subjected to it: `إنك إن اتبعت عورات الناس أفسدتهم أو كدت تفسدهم` [3] — if you pursue people's faults you corrupt them, or come near to it. Abu al-Darda' remarked that Mu'awiya was helped by hearing that one sentence.

The same applied to authority: `إن الأمير إذا ابتغى الريبة في الناس أفسدهم` [4] — when the ruler goes looking for suspicion among people, he corrupts them.

And a warning turned back on the watcher: `فإن من اتبع عوراتهم يتبع الله عورته ومن يتبع الله عورته يفضحه في بيته` [5] — whoever pursues their faults, God pursues his, and whom God pursues He exposes in his own house.

**The cases, which are the point.**

Ibn Mas'ud, told that a man's beard was dripping with wine: `إنا قد نهينا عن التجسس ولكن إن يظهر لنا شيء نأخذ به` [6] — we have been forbidden to spy; but if something becomes apparent to us, we act on it. The suspicion was strong. He still would not go looking.

Then 'Umar, twice. Patrolling Madina with 'Abd al-Rahman b. 'Awf, they see a lamp and hear raised voices from a house. 'Umar names the household and says they are drinking; what do you think? `قلت : أرى أنا قد أتينا ما نهى الله عنه قال الله تعالى : ولا تجسسوا وقد تجسسنا فانصرف عمر وتركهم` [7] — I said: I think we have done what God forbade; God said *do not spy*, and we have spied. So 'Umar turned away and left them.

And Abu Mihjan, whom 'Umar actually walked in on: `إن هذا لا يحل لك قد نهاك الله عن التجسس فخرج عمر وتركه` [8] — this is not lawful for you; God forbade you to spy. And 'Umar went out and left him.

The caliph, with a real offence in front of him, accepting that the manner of discovery made the knowledge unusable. That is a much higher standard than "don't be nosy".

**Looking into a house.**

`من اطلع في دار قوم بغير إذنهم ففقؤوا عينه، فقد هدرت عينه` [9] — whoever looks into a household's home without their permission, and they put out his eye, his eye goes uncompensated.

Read it as a measure of gravity rather than a licence: the jurists dispute the practical application, and nobody should take it as permission to injure anyone. What it establishes is that the interior of a person's home was protected to an extraordinary degree.

A phone is where the interior of a life now sits.

**So, plainly.**

Reading a spouse's messages, going through their phone, installing something to watch them, reading a colleague's screen, scrolling a friend's private account through a fake one — every one of these is `البحث عما يكتم عنك`, searching out what is concealed from you. The suspicion being justified does not change it; that is exactly what the 'Umar and Ibn Mas'ud reports settle.

**Where these books genuinely leave a gap.**

A parent's duty of care over a child is real and no report above was addressed to it. Nothing here weighs the protection of a minor against the prohibition on spying, and someone must weigh them. The same is true of an employer's duty over work equipment and a guardian's over someone who cannot protect themselves. And nothing addresses a state's obligations, disclosed monitoring that the monitored person has agreed to, or a safeguarding investigation.

Those are real questions, and a scholar can weigh them against this material. What the material does settle is that "I suspected something" is not on its own an answer, since that is precisely the fact pattern in every case above.

**What to ask, and one thing to do first.**

- Is what you want to look at concealed from you, or apparent?
- Would you be doing this if you did not suspect? If not, notice that this is the exact position 'Umar was in.
- Is there a duty of care here — a child, someone vulnerable — or is it your own peace of mind?
- Could you ask them instead? An open question is not tajassus.
- If you find nothing, what have you done to the relationship; and if you find something, how will you explain how you found it?

And the thing to do first, if you already looked: `من ستر مسلما` is a chapter of its own, and covering what you have seen is a better move than acting on it. If what you saw is serious enough that it cannot be left, take it to a scholar or a counsellor and tell them how you came by it. That part matters as much as what you found.
