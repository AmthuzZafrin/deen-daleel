# I feel nothing when I pray — what's wrong with me?

## Answer

Nothing is wrong with you. The pattern you are describing has a name in the
hadith, it was expected, and the Prophet ﷺ gave a rule for judging someone who
is in it — a rule that comes out in your favour.

**The hadith.** Abu Hurayra: the Prophet ﷺ said, "**every thing has a period of
zeal, and every period of zeal has a slackening — so if its owner aims straight
and keeps close, then hope for him; but if he is pointed at with fingers, do not
count on him**" [1]. Al-Tirmidhi: "this is a *hasan sahih* hadith, rare from this
route" [2].

*Shirra* is the burst of eagerness; *fatra* is the flatness that follows it. The
hadith does not treat the flatness as a fall. It treats it as the second half of
a cycle, and asks only one thing of the person in it: keep aiming, stay close.
That is the state you are in, and it is the state the hadith says to have hope
for.

**And it is not about how you look.** When al-Hasan al-Basri mentioned this
hadith, his students misread the second clause as being about fame, and said:
"but when you pass through the market, people point at you! He said: he did not
mean this — he only meant the innovator in his religion and the corrupt in his
worldly life" [3]. The warning is about someone who has gone off, not someone who
has gone quiet.

There is a second wording that puts it even more plainly: "every worker has a
slackening, and every slackening has a zeal — so whoever's slackening is towards
my *sunna*, he has succeeded" [4]. Not *whoever has no slackening*. Whoever, when
the energy drops, is still inside the ordinary practice.

**Do less, not more.** This is the counter-intuitive instruction the sources
actually give. Tawus: "**the best worship is the lightest of it**" — and Ibn 'Abd
al-Barr's gloss: "he means the lightest upon the hearts and the most beloved to
the souls, for that is likelier that its doer will persist in it until it becomes
a habit and a character for him" [5]. And the warning against the opposite:
"Allah does not tire until you tire — meaning that whoever tires of a deed he is
doing has his reward cut off from him" [6]. Ibn 'Abbas: "beware of excess in
religion, for those before you were destroyed only by excess in religion" [7].

If prayer feels hollow, the answer in these texts is not to add night vigils. It
is to make what you are already doing sustainable.

**Now, on feeling nothing — because *khushu'* is not a feeling.** This is the
most useful correction on the file. Sufyan al-Thawri asked al-A'mash about
*khushu'*, who said he had asked Ibrahim al-Nakha'i, and Ibrahim said: "you want
to be an imam to people and you do not know *khushu'*? **Khushu' is not eating
coarse food and wearing coarse clothes and bowing the head — rather *khushu'* is
that you see the noble and the lowly as equal where the truth is concerned**" [8].

'Umar saw a young man with his head hanging down and said: "you there, raise your
head — **for *khushu'* does not exceed what is in the heart**" [9]. And
al-Qurtubi's conclusion: "whoever displays to people a *khushu'* above what is in
his heart has only displayed hypocrisy upon hypocrisy" [10].

Qatada's definition is the workable one: "*khushu'* is in the heart, and fear, and
lowering the gaze in the prayer" [11] — and the practice behind it is physical
and simple: "the Prophet ﷺ used to raise his gaze to the sky, then he was
commanded with *khushu'*, so he cast his gaze towards his place of prostration"
[12].

None of that requires you to feel anything. It requires you to stand still, look
down, and mean the words.

**What to actually do.** Al-Nawawi's prescription is short: "it is recommended
that he enter the prayer with vigour and with attention towards it, and that he
ponder the recitation and the words of remembrance, and recite them measuredly"
[13]. And the promise attached: "**no Muslim performs ablution and does it well,
then stands and prays two rak'ahs turning towards them with his heart and his
face, but Paradise becomes obligatory for him**" [14].

*Turning towards them with his heart and his face.* That is an act of attention,
not a state of emotion. You can do it in a prayer that feels like nothing.

**So.** Keep the prayers. Cut back anything voluntary that you have been forcing.
Lower your gaze in the prayer and slow the recitation down enough to hear
yourself. And stop treating the dryness as a verdict on you — the hadith names it
as an ordinary phase and tells others to have hope for the person who is in it
and still turning up.
