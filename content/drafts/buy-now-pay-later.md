# Are buy-now-pay-later schemes allowed?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

## Answer

**The payment structure is usually fine; the late fee is the problem. In most of these schemes you pay the same total you would have paid in cash, spread over instalments — and that is an ordinary deferred-price sale. What sits badly is the clause that adds to what you owe if you miss a date, because an increase on a debt for the passage of time is the original and defining case of what is forbidden.**

**Why the instalments themselves are not the issue.** If the total is fixed and known and you pay no more for paying later, there is a single sale at a determinate price. That is the analysis on the page about buying in instalments, and nothing here disturbs it. Where the provider is paid by the merchant rather than by you, you are not paying an increase at all.

**Why the late fee is.** Ibn al-'Arabi describes the practice the prohibition was revealed against, in terms that map onto a missed payment exactly: `يبايع الرجل الرجل إلى أجل فإذا حل الأجل قال أتقضي أم تربي يعني أم تزيدني على مالي عليك وأصبر أجلا آخر فحرم الله تعالى الربا وهو الزيادة` [1] — a man would sell to another for a term; when the term fell due he would say **do you pay, or do you increase?** — meaning, will you add to what you owe me and I will wait a further term — **so God forbade `الربا`, which is the increase.**

And the word itself: `قال علماؤنا الربا في اللغة هو الزيادة` [2] — in the language, `الربا` **is** increase.

A missed instalment followed by a charge that raises the outstanding amount is that transaction, automated. The debtor did not pay; the sum owed went up; time was extended. That the increase is a fixed sum rather than a percentage changes the arithmetic, not the structure.

**A distinction worth being precise about.** A genuine charge for a service rendered — the actual administrative cost of chasing a missed payment — is argued by some contemporary scholars to be a fee rather than an increase on the debt. That argument is available, but it has to be true: a charge that is set as a deterrent, that scales with the amount outstanding, or that repeats for each period of delay is not a service cost. Most of these schemes' fees do not look like cost recovery, and you can usually tell by asking whether the charge would exist if the provider incurred no cost at all.

Note also what the classical remedy for late payment actually was. The tradition treats stalling by someone able to pay as a real wrong — but its answer is compulsion applied by a court, not an automatic increase in the sum. That is set out on the page about credit cards, and it is the reason the two remedies must not be confused.

**A genuine classical question, if the clause is objectionable: does it spoil the whole contract?**

The jurists disagreed about a corrupt condition attached to an otherwise sound sale. Ibn Qudama gives both narrations. On the first: `هل يفسد به البيع ؟ على روايتين: إحداهما، يفسد، وهو مذهب الشافعي ; لأنه عقد قارنه شرط فاسد، فأفسده` [3] — it spoils the sale, this being al-Shafi'i's position, because a corrupt condition accompanied the contract and so corrupted it. The supporting reasoning is worth noting: `ولأن الشرط يأخذ قسطا من الثمن، فإذا حذفناه وجب رد ما سقط من الثمن من أجله، وذلك مجهول، فيكون الثمن مجهولا، فيفسد العقد` [4] — the condition takes a share of the price, so striking it out would require returning the part of the price attributable to it, which is unknown — making the price unknown and the contract corrupt.

On the second: `والثانية، لا يفسد العقد به، وهو قول ابن أبي ليلى ; لحديث بريرة. ولأن العقد قد تم بأركانه، والشرط زائد، فإذا فسد وزال، سقط الفاسد، وبقي العقد بركنيه، كما لو لم يشترط` [5] — it does not spoil the contract; the contract is complete in its essentials and the condition is an addition, so when it fails it drops away and the contract remains as if it had never been stipulated.

That disagreement is why scholars answer this question differently. On the stricter view, agreeing to a contract containing a late-fee clause is itself entering a corrupt contract. On the other, the clause falls away and what remains is a lawful deferred sale — which is the reasoning behind the common practical answer: **use it only if you are certain you will not trigger the fee.**

**What is absent from these books.** They know nothing of a third party paying the merchant and collecting from you, of credit checks and reporting, or of how these products are designed around the expectation that a proportion of users will pay late. That last is a real feature of the business model and it is worth knowing, because a scheme whose revenue depends on your missing a payment is not neutral about whether you do.

**What to do.**

1. **Read the late-fee terms before agreeing**, not after. That clause is the whole question.
2. **Only use it if the money is already there.** If you could pay in full today and are spreading it for convenience, the fee clause is theoretical. If you are using it because you cannot afford the thing, you are relying on the part that is objectionable.
3. **Set the payment dates against your income dates** so the fee cannot be triggered by timing.
4. **Prefer the merchant's own instalment offer** where one exists at the same total price, since it is a plain deferred sale with no third party.
5. **If a fee has been charged**, pay it to clear the debt, and take the specific product to a scholar if you intend to keep using it — this is a case where the contract wording genuinely decides the answer.
