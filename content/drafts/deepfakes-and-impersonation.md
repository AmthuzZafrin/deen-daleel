# Is making a deepfake of someone haram?

> Reviewer note: Falls under false witness, forgery and slander long before it touches the image rules. The harm is that it puts words and acts on a person that were not theirs.


## Answer

**A deepfake is not really a question about images, and treating it as one lets it off far too lightly.** Putting a face into a video is the least of it. What a deepfake does is attribute *speech and action* to a person who did not speak or act — and that lands in a chapter the sources treat far more severely than the chapter on pictures.

**Start where the tradition puts it: false witness.**

Abu Bakra reported that the Prophet ﷺ asked whether he should tell them the greatest of the major sins. They said yes. He named `الإشراك بالله، وعقوق الوالدين، وشهادة الزور، أو قول الزور` [1] — associating partners with God, disobedience to parents, and false witness, or false speech. Al-Tirmidhi grades it *hasan sahih*. What the narrator remembers next is the detail worth keeping: `فما زال رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم يقولها   حتى قلنا: ليته سكت` [2] — he kept repeating it until we wished he would stop.

Note that the third item is stated twice over, `شهادة الزور` and `قول الزور`. Not only sworn testimony in a court, but false *speech*. A fabricated recording is false speech given a form that is harder to deny than testimony ever was.

**Then the verse about what it does to the person.**

`And those who malign believing men and believing women undeservedly, they bear the guilt of slander and manifest sin.` [3]

Al-Tabari on `بهتان`: `فقد احتملوا زورا وكذبا وفرية شنيعة` [4] — they have taken on falsehood, lying, and a hideous fabrication — and on the sin: `وإثما يبين لسامعه أنه إثم وزور` [5], a sin evident as sin to whoever hears it. Mujahid reads the maligning as `والذين يقفون المؤمنين والمؤمنات، ويعيبونهم طلبا لشينهم` [6] — those who trail believing men and women and fault them, seeking their disgrace. That is a fair description of what a malicious deepfake is *for*.

Qatada's comment on the verse is the one to carry: `فإياكم وأذى المؤمن، فإن الله يحوطه، ويغضب له` [7] — beware of harming a believer, for God guards him and is angered for his sake.

**One case the sources single out above every other.**

Lying about the Prophet ﷺ is not treated as an instance of lying. It is treated as its own offence. Muslim opens his *Sahih* with a chapter of it. 'Ali: `لا تكذبوا علي، فإنه من يكذب علي يلج النار` [8]. Abu Hurayra: `من كذب علي متعمدا، فليتبوأ مقعده من النار` [9]. And al-Mughira with the clause that makes the distinction explicit: `إن كذبا علي ليس ككذب على أحد` [10] — lying about me is not like lying about anyone else.

Anas's own reaction to that hadith is instructive, because it is about restraint rather than punishment: `إنه ليمنعني أن أحدثكم حديثا كثيرا أن رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم، قال: «من تعمد علي كذبا، فليتبوأ مقعده من النار»` [11] — what stops me narrating a great deal to you is that the Messenger of God ﷺ said this. A Companion narrating *less* because fabrication was so grave. Synthesised sermons, cloned voices of scholars, and generated audio of the Prophet ﷺ or the Companions are the modern shape of exactly what he was being careful about.

**The second axis: fraud, and why "you can't tell how much" matters.**

Ibn Taymiyya was asked about adulterated goods and gave a rule with an unusual test in it. Adulterated milk may not be sold `ولو علم المشترى أنه مخلوط بالماء` — even if the buyer knows it is mixed — and the reason is: `فان مقدار الغش غير معلوم` [12], the *measure* of the adulteration is unknown. Knowing that something has been tampered with does not cure it when you cannot tell how much. He generalises: `وهكذا كلما كان من المغشوش الذي لا يعلم قدر غشه فانه ينهي عن بيعه وعن عمله لمن يبيعه` [13] — so with everything adulterated whose degree of adulteration is unknown: forbidden to sell, and forbidden to *make* for someone who will sell it.

That last clause reaches the person who builds the thing, not only the person who publishes it.

And the test travels unusually well. A world with convincing synthetic media is one where a real recording can no longer be trusted either, because the measure of the adulteration is unknown. The harm is not confined to the individual fake.

**What these books do not reach.**

Nothing here addresses a deepfake that is clearly labelled — satire, parody, a film's visual effects, a dubbed voice with the audience told. On the reasoning above, disclosure goes directly to whether it is `زور` at all, but no author here drew that line for this medium and it would be dishonest to draw it for them. Nothing addresses consent — a person licensing their own likeness. Nothing addresses using a synthetic voice for accessibility, or restoring a damaged recording. Nothing addresses the depiction of a public figure in a way everyone understands to be invention.

**What to do.**

The questions that decide it, on the material above, are these:

- Will anyone believe it happened? If yes, this is `قول الزور` and the rest is detail.
- Is the subject a real, identifiable person, and did they consent?
- Does it put words, acts or a state into someone that were not theirs — the thing the verse calls `بغير ما اكتسبوا`, *undeservedly*, for what they did not do?
- Is it religious content attributed to the Prophet ﷺ, a Companion or a scholar? Then the material at [10] applies before anything else, and the safe answer is not to.

If it is labelled, consented to, and about nobody in particular, take it to a scholar as a question about images rather than about testimony — there is a separate answer on generated images of people. And if you are the *subject* of one, the verse and Qatada's comment on it are addressed to your situation, not only to the person who made it.
