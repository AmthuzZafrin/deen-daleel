# Is cannabis haram if it's legal where I live?

> Reviewer note: The muskir definition does not turn on the law of the land. The classical discussion of hashish is directly on point and worth quoting rather than reasoning around.


## Answer

**The law where you live is not one of the things this ruling turns on, and that is the shortest honest answer.** The category is `مسكر` — intoxicant — and it is defined by what a substance does to a mind, not by what a parliament has done with it. What is genuinely worth knowing is that cannabis is not a modern question here at all: the jurists argued about it directly, by name, and the arguments they had are close to the ones people still have.

**The rule, and how wide it was drawn.**

Ibn 'Umar reported the Prophet ﷺ: `كل مسكر خمر، وكل خمر حرام` [1] — every intoxicant is khamr, and every khamr is forbidden. In the same chapter of Muslim: `كل مسكر حرام، إن على الله عز وجل عهدا لمن يشرب المسكر أن يسقيه من طينة الخبال` [2], and when the Prophet ﷺ was asked about two local Yemeni drinks he answered with a rule rather than a list — `أنهى عن كل مسكر أسكر عن الصلاة` [3], I forbid every intoxicant that intoxicates away from the prayer.

The point of answering by rule was that the material does not matter. Ibn Taymiyya: `وكل ما كانت فيه هذه الشدة المطربة فهو خمر من أى مادة كان من الحبوب والثمار وغير ذلك` [4] — whatever carries that exhilarating potency is khamr, from whatever substance, grain or fruit or anything else. And the name is not the test either: `واسم ( الخمر ) في لغة العرب الذين خوطبوا بالقرآن كان يتناول المسكر من التمر وغيره ولا يختص بالمسكر من العنب` [5] — in the Arabic of those the Qur'an addressed, *khamr* covered the intoxicant of dates and the rest, not grapes alone. He notes the historical proof: when khamr was forbidden at Madina there were no grapevines there, and what people poured away was made from dates.

Quantity is not the test either. `ما اسكر كثيره فقليله حرام` [6] — what intoxicates in quantity is forbidden in a little — which he notes more than one hadith master graded sound. The governing verse is `إنما الخمر والميسر والأنصاب والأزلام رجس من عمل الشيطان فاجتنبوه لعلكم تفلحون` [7].

**Cannabis by name.**

This is the part worth reading, because it is not analogy — it is the same substance. Ibn Taymiyya was asked and answered: `هذه الحشيشة الصلبة حرام سواء سكر منها أو لم يسكر والسكر منها حرام باتفاق المسلمين` [8] — forbidden whether or not one becomes intoxicated by it, and intoxication by it forbidden by the agreement of the Muslims. He fixes its arrival in time: `وانما حدثت في الناس بحدوث التتار` [9] — it appeared among people with the coming of the Tatars, which is to say it was, for him, a new drug, and he treated it as one.

He also answers the objection people still make — that it is not really an intoxicant, that it only fogs. `ومن ظن أن الحشيشة لا تسكر وانما تغيب العقل بلا لذة فلم يعرف حقيقة أمرها فانه لولا ما فيها من اللذة لم يتناولوها ولا أكلوها` [10] — whoever supposes hashish does not intoxicate but merely blanks the mind without pleasure has not understood it; were there no pleasure in it, they would not take it. His verdict on the jurists who looked properly: `وأما المحققون من الفقهاء فعلموا أنها مسكرة وإنما يتناولها الفجار لما فيها من النشوة والطرب` [11].

He is precise about how its effect differs from wine rather than pretending it is identical: `والخمر توجب الحركة والخصومة وهذه توجب الفتور والذلة` [12] — wine produces motion and quarrelling; this produces lassitude and abjection. The difference did not change the ruling, because the category was never "makes you fight".

And he records that the contrary opinion existed and was argued in verse — someone had said they `حرموها من غير عقل ونقل` [13], forbade it without reason or transmitted proof. He rejects it, but the disagreement is in the books rather than hidden.

He extends the principle past intoxication proper: `وكل ما يغيب العقل فانه حرام وان لم تحصل به نشوة ولاطرب فان تغيب العقل حرام باجماع المسلمين` [14] — everything that blanks the intellect is forbidden even where no exhilaration follows. Where a substance does that without intoxicating, he places it under discretionary punishment rather than the fixed penalty: `وأما تعاطى ( البنج ) الذى لم يسكر ولم يغيب العقل ففيه التعزير` [15].

**Where there was real disagreement, and why it does not rescue the case.**

The majority position — `أن كل مسكر خمر وكل خمر حرام وما أسكر كثيره فقليله حرام وهذا مذهب مالك وأصحابه والشافعى وأصحابه وأحمد بن حنبل وأصحابه` [16] — was not unanimous. A group of the Kufans held otherwise: `وذهب طائفة من العلماء من أهل الكوفة كالنخعى والشعبى وأبى حنيفة وشريك وغيرهم` [17], that for intoxicants from other than the grape and the date-palm, only the intoxicating quantity is forbidden.

Note what that dispute is actually about: whether a *small, non-intoxicating* amount of a non-grape drink is forbidden. Even on that minority view, the intoxicating amount is forbidden. It is not a licence; it is an argument about the threshold.

**What these books do not reach.**

Nothing here addresses cannabis prescribed for a medical condition, which is a different question — the material on treatment and necessity is a separate chapter and is not in front of us here. Nothing addresses CBD or preparations that do not act on the mind at all, which on the reasoning above would not enter the category in the first place, since the category is defined by the effect. Nothing addresses modern potency, dosage, or the difference between smoking and edibles. And no author here was writing where the substance was lawful and socially ordinary, which changes nothing about the ruling but changes a great deal about living with it.

**What to do.**

If the question is recreational use where it is legal, the material above is not ambiguous and a scholar will tell you the same; legality was never among the conditions.

If it is medical, that is a real question and not this one — take the diagnosis, the prescription and the alternatives to a scholar and to your doctor together, and ask about it as a question of treatment.

If the honest situation is that you are already using and want to stop, the useful thing is not a further ruling. Ibn Taymiyya's observation that it produces `الفتور والذلة` [12] is a description more than a condemnation, and it names what makes stopping hard. That is worth taking to someone who can help with the habit rather than only with the verdict.
