# Why can't Muslims be cremated?

## Answer

**A Muslim is not cremated. But notice how the answer is reached, because that matters for the cases where it gets complicated.** There is no chapter on cremation in these books, and no hadith that says "do not burn your dead." Nobody put the question, because nobody proposed it. The ruling is derived, and it rests on three things that *are* stated directly: burial is an obligation, the dead body keeps its inviolability, and fire is singled out.

**One: burial is obligatory, not a custom.** Al-Qurtubi on the verse "Have We not made the earth a receptacle": `ألم نجعل الأرض كفاتا ) أي ضامه تضم الأحياء على ظهورها والأموات في بطنها وهذا يدل على وجوب مواراة الميت ودفنه ودفن شعره وسائر ما يزيله عنه` [1] — it gathers, holding the living on its back and the dead in its belly; and this indicates the obligation of concealing the dead and burying him, and burying his hair and whatever else is removed from him.

Ibn al-'Arabi gives the two purposes: `دفن الميت لوجهين أحدهما لستره الثاني لئلا يؤذي الأحياء بجيفته` [2] — burial is for two reasons, to cover him, and so that the living are not harmed by the corpse. And then the ruling, which is worth reading in full because of its last clause: `فصار ذلك سنة باقية في الخلق وفرضا على جميع الناس على الكفاية من فعله منهم سقط عن الباقين فرضه وأخص الخلق به الأقربون ثم الذين يلونهم من الجيرة ثم سائر الناس المسلمين وهو حق في الكافر أيضا` [3] — it became an abiding practice among mankind and a communal obligation on all people; whoever performs it lifts it from the rest; those most bound to it are the nearest kin, then the neighbours, then the Muslims at large — **and it is a right owed to the non-Muslim as well.**

Two things follow from that. Burial is an obligation *on the living*, not a preference of the deceased — so a request for cremation in a will does not transfer the duty to someone else. And the tradition regards burial as something owed to any human body, not a privilege of membership.

**Two: the dead body keeps its inviolability.** `كسر عظم الميت ككسره حيا` [4] — breaking the bone of a dead person is like breaking it while he is alive. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr transmits it from 'A'isha with her own gloss: `أنه بلغه أن عائشة كانت تقول كسر عظم المؤمن ميتا ككسره وهو حي تعني في الإثم` [5] — she meant *in respect of the sin*. And he notes what the ruling is used for: `لأنه يدل على كراهة حفر قبور المسلمين` [6] — it shows the disapproval of digging up Muslims' graves.

Note the gloss carefully, because it is doing real work. The equivalence is in the sin borne by the person who does it, not in pain suffered by the dead. That is the tradition's own framing of why bodies are handled the way they are.

Al-Nawawi adds the point that removes the only rationale a purity-based law might have offered for burning: `هذا الحديث أصل عظيم في طهارة المسلم حيا وميتا` [7] — a great foundation for the purity of the Muslim, living and dead — with `ولقوله صلى الله عليه وسلم ان المسلم لا ينجس وذكر البخاري في صحيحه عن ابن عباس تعليقا المسلم لا ينجس حيا ولا ميتا` [8]. A corpse is not treated as contamination in this law, so there is nothing to burn away. And again the reach is wider than Muslims: `وأما الكافر فحكمه في الطهارة والنجاسة حكم المسلم هذا مذهبنا ومذهب الجماهير من السلف والخلف` [9] — the non-Muslim's ruling in purity is the same as the Muslim's, and this is the position of the great majority, early and late.

**Three: fire is singled out.** `وفيه وإن النار لا يعذب بها إلا الله وبينت هناك اسمهما وما يتعلق بشرح الحديث وعند أبي داود عن ابن مسعود في قصة أخرى أنه لا ينبغي أن يعذب بالنار إلا رب النار` [10] — none punishes with fire but God; and from Ibn Mas'ud, none should punish with fire but the Lord of the fire. Al-Qurtubi cites the same alongside the prohibition of mutilation: `وبالنهي عن المثلة وقوله : ( لا يعذب بالنار إلا رب النار` [11].

Be precise about the strength of this one. Those statements are about punishment and about conduct in war, not about funerals, and it would be sloppy to quote them as though they were a direct prohibition of cremation. Their weight here is joined to the first two points: the body must be buried, it may not be broken, and of all the ways of destroying it, fire is the one the sources treat as reserved. Together those are why the ruling has never been in doubt, even though no author states it as such.

Ibn al-'Arabi puts the underlying principle in a related context: `حرمة النبي ميتا كحرمته حيا` [12] — inviolability does not lapse at death.

**So, practically.**

*If you are arranging a Muslim's funeral:* washing, shrouding, the funeral prayer and burial in the ground. The duty is yours as the living, and it is not discharged by cremation, whatever was requested.

*If a will asks for cremation:* the obligation of burial rests on the living kin, per Ibn al-'Arabi above, and a person cannot instruct someone else out of a duty the law places on them. That is a derivation from the passages here rather than a ruling any of these authors states, so if this is a live dispute in a family it is worth putting to a scholar directly.

*If a non-Muslim relative is being cremated:* you are not the one making that decision, and nothing here makes their family's rite your responsibility or your sin. What the corpus does say is that burial is a right owed to them too, which is a reason to say so if you are asked, and not a licence to fight about it at a funeral. Whether a Muslim may attend a cremation is not addressed in these sources at all, and I am not going to manufacture an answer to it.

**What this does not cover.** What happens to ashes; whether cremation harms the person cremated — nothing here addresses it, though 'A'isha's gloss `تعني في الإثم` points the whole question toward the sin of the one who does it rather than injury to the dead; post-mortem examination and organ donation, which are separate questions with their own material; and situations where cremation is legally compulsory, which no premodern work anticipates. On that last one, a scholar working with your jurisdiction is the right address, not this shelf.
