# Are video games haram?

## Answer

No source in front of us discusses video games. What the sources do discuss is *play* — when it is permitted, when it is not, and why — and they give three tests that between them settle nearly every case a game can present.

**Test one: is money riding on the outcome?**

This is the hard line, and it is where a great deal of modern gaming lands.

The Hanafi *Bada'i' al-Sana'i'*, on the conditions for a lawful contest: "that the stake be **from one of the two sides only** — unless a *muhallil* is present. So if the stake is from both sides together and they have not brought in a *muhallil*, it is not permitted, **because it is in the meaning of gambling**" [1].

Read that structurally, because it names exactly what a modern game does. Each side puts money in; one side takes the pot. That is the forbidden form, whatever it is called — an entry-fee tournament, a wagered match, a skin bet. What is permitted is a prize put up by someone who is not competing. Ibn Hajar reports the same rule as agreed: "they agreed on its permissibility with a prize, **on condition that it be from someone other than the competitors** — like the ruler, where he has no horse among them; and the majority permitted it to be from one of the two sides among the competitors, and likewise where a third *muhallil* is with them, on condition that he puts up nothing" [2].

Loot boxes and paid random draws are the same structure again: you pay a fixed price for an unknown return. That is the *gharar* that the classical writers place next to *maysir*, and it is the reason a free game and the same game with a paid crate are not one question.

And al-Qasim b. Muhammad's answer, when asked to define *maysir*, widens it past money altogether: "**everything that distracts from the remembrance of God and from prayer is *maysir***" [3].

**Test two: is there a good outcome attached to it?**

This is the most useful principle in the file, and it comes from the Hanafis' own explanation of why racing and archery are permitted at all.

*Bada'i'*: "the exception may be for a meaning not found in others — namely **exercise and preparation for the causes of struggle in general**. So it was play in form, and exercise and learning the causes of struggle [in substance], and therefore permitted when the conditions of permissibility are gathered. **And even if it is play — play to which a praiseworthy outcome attaches is not forbidden**" [4].

Ibn Qudama gives the same reason for why prizes are confined to horses, camels and archery: "these three were singled out for permitting a prize in them **because they are instruments of war, which one is commanded to learn and master and excel in**" [5]. And the hadith he cites: "God enters three people into Paradise by a single arrow — **its maker, who intends good in his making of it**; the one who shoots with it; and the one who hands him the arrows" [6]. The person who *made the equipment* is in the hadith.

The most striking wording is in the same passage: "**and there is nothing of amusement except three: a man's training of his horse, and his playing with his wife, and his shooting with his bow and arrows**" [7].

That is a narrow list — but look at what is on it. Skill training. Marriage. Marksmanship. Two of the three have no religious content whatever; they qualify because something good comes of them.

**Test three: what is it doing to your time and your prayers?**

Al-Qasim's criterion above is the whole of it, and it is the one that will decide your actual case. A game that ends when the prayer is called is in a different position from one that does not.

**The evidence that play as such is not the problem.**

The Abyssinians played with spears in the mosque and the Prophet ﷺ said *let them be*, while 'A'isha watched from behind him. Ibn Hajar: "**and playing with spears is not mere play** — rather in it is the training of the brave for the places of war and preparation for the enemy" [8]. And al-Muhallab's principle, drawn from the same incident: "the mosque is established for the safety of the community of Muslims, so **whatever actions combine a benefit for the religion and its people are permitted in it** — and in the hadith is the permissibility of looking at permitted amusement" [9].

A phrase like *permitted amusement* only exists because the category exists.

There is also a school position that treats play more warily without treating it as sin. Al-Qurtubi reports: "Abu Hanifa said: playing chess and *nard* and *al-arba'a 'ashar* and all amusement is disliked; **but if no major sin appears from the one playing them, and his good qualities are more than his bad, his testimony is accepted with them**" [10]. Disliked, and not disqualifying.

And a note on how wide the racing licence went: the majority restricted prize-contests to hoof, foot and arrow, "but **'Ata' permitted it in everything**" [11].

**So, practically.**

- **Anything with a stake from both sides is gambling** — entry-fee tournaments, wagered matches, betting on esports. This is the one clear prohibition and it does not depend on any school. A prize from a sponsor who is not competing is the lawful form.
- **Loot boxes and paid random rewards** are a paid unknown return; that is the structure the jurists guard against, and the free version of the same game is not the same question.
- **Nothing makes playing a game as such forbidden.** *Permitted amusement* is a category the commentators use without embarrassment.
- **The strongest thing you can say for a game is that something good comes of it** — the reflexes, the language, the problem-solving, the friendship, the rest that lets you work. *Bada'i'*'s principle is that play with a praiseworthy outcome is not forbidden, and that is a real argument, not a rationalisation.
- **Content is content.** A game whose material is obscenity, blasphemy or the celebration of cruelty is judged as that material, not as a game.
- **The prayer is the line.** Al-Qasim's test names anything that pulls you away from prayer and remembrance as *maysir* — with or without money, with or without a controller.

Nobody in these books ruled on this, and anyone who tells you they did is not reading them. What they left you is better than a ruling: a set of questions sharp enough to answer your own case.
