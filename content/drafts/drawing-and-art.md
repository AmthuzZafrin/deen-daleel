# Can I draw people or animals?

> Reviewer note: Same school-skew caution as photographs.

## Answer

This is the case the sources are actually about — a person making a likeness by hand — so here the evidence is direct rather than analogical. The short version: **everything that is not a living creature is open to you without restriction, and living creatures are restricted, with a real disagreement about how far.**

**The rule, and its exact scope.**

Al-Nawawi: "our companions and other scholars said: making an image of an animate being is forbidden with a severe prohibition, and it is among the major sins... and whether one makes it for something that will be degraded or for otherwise, making it is forbidden in every case — **because in it is an imitation of the creation of God Most High**" [1].

And immediately, the limit: "as for making an image of a tree, or camel saddles, or anything else that is not the image of an animate being — **that is not forbidden**" [2].

That single sentence is why Islamic art went where it went. Calligraphy, geometry, arabesque, landscape, architecture, textile pattern, illumination — none of it is touched by any position in this file. If you want to make art, the largest part of the field is simply open, and it is open by explicit statement rather than by inference.

**Why living things are singled out.**

Al-Khattabi: "the punishment of the image-maker was made grave only because **images used to be worshipped besides God**, and because looking at them causes temptation, and some souls incline toward them. He said: and what is meant by images here is statues that have a spirit" [3].

Ibn Hajar reinforces it: the prohibition is intensified for what is worshipped besides God, "for it imitates the form of the idols, **which are the origin of the prohibition of image-making**" [4].

And the intention grades the offence. Ibn Hajar: "one who makes an image of an ensouled thing **for worship** is more severely punished than one who makes it not for worship" [5]. Elsewhere: the severe threat is restricted by some to "one who makes an image *intending to imitate*... as for anyone other than him, it is forbidden to him and he sins, **but his sin is below the sin of the one who imitates**" [6].

There is a proof that this is a legislated prohibition rather than something intrinsically abhorrent: Solomon's craftsmen made statues at his command, and the Qur'an lists it among God's favours — *"They made for him what he willed: synagogues and statues"* [7]. Ibn Hajar's answer to the difficulty: "that was permitted in that law, and they used to make the shapes of the prophets and the righteous among them in their posture of worship, in order to worship as they worshipped" — and he cites Abu al-'Aliya: "**that was not forbidden in their law; then our law came with the prohibition of it**" [8].

The prohibition arrived with a purpose, at a time and place where images were being worshipped. That does not abolish it. It does tell you what it is for.

**How far does the prohibition reach? Four positions.**

Ibn Hajar quotes Ibn al-'Arabi's summary: "if they have bodies, it is forbidden by consensus; and if they are flat markings, there are four positions. First, permitted absolutely... Second, forbidden absolutely, even the flat marking. Third, if the image remains in its form, standing in shape, it is forbidden, and if the head is cut off or the parts are separated, it is permitted — he said, and this is the soundest. Fourth, if it is something degraded it is permitted, and if it is hung it is not" [9].

Ibn Hajar names the third as Ibn al-'Arabi's considered view: "Ibn al-'Arabi held it sound that a flat image, if it remains in its form, is forbidden — whether it is a degraded thing or not — and if its head is cut off or its shape is broken up, it is permitted; and this position is transmitted from al-Zuhri, and al-Nawawi strengthened it" [10].

That is a workable line for an artist: what makes the figure *complete and standing* is the object of the rule.

Against which, some jurists were far more permissive about the medium. Ibn Hajar: "opposing this severity is what Abu Muhammad al-Juwayni transmitted — that weaving an image into a cloth is not prevented, because it may be worn; and al-Mutawalli extended that to image-making on the ground and the like; **and al-Nawawi held the prohibition of all of that to be sound**" [11].

**Three limits on the strict reading that are worth knowing.**

*Children's toys.* The consensus on three-dimensional figures has a stated exception. Ibn Hajar, noting Ibn al-'Arabi's report of consensus on shadow-casting images: "**and this consensus applies to other than girls' dolls**" [12]. 'A'isha's toys are the basis, and it means a doll, a stuffed animal, a modelling-clay elephant made with a child is not the case anyone is describing.

*Things not made to last.* Al-Qurtubi "reported in *al-Mufhim*, regarding images not made for permanence such as fired clay, two positions — the more apparent of them being prohibition" [13]. Two positions, and Ibn Hajar goes on to wonder aloud whether sugar figures belong with pottery or with dolls. The point for you is that the boundary was genuinely uncertain, in the eleventh century as now.

*Plants, and how far the licence goes.* Mujahid tried to narrow it: he "restricted the permission of drawing trees to those that do not bear fruit; as for what bears fruit, he attached it to the ensouled. 'Iyad said: **no one said this except Mujahid**" [14]. And al-Tahawi refuted it directly: "since an image is permitted after cutting off its head — which, had it been cut from an ensouled thing, would not have lived — that indicates the permissibility of what has no spirit at all" [15].

So the licence on plants and inanimate things stands, on the strongest reading, without restriction.

**What this means if you draw.**

- **Calligraphy, geometric and floral pattern, landscape, architecture, still life, abstraction, and objects of every kind — permitted with no dispute at all.** This is not a grudging space; it is most of what art is, and it is the space the tradition's own masters worked in.
- **A free-standing sculpture of a person or animal is the agreed prohibition** — that is the case Ibn al-'Arabi calls consensus, and it is the one directly continuous with idols.
- **Drawing living creatures on a flat surface is genuinely disputed**, across four positions, none of them fringe. The strictest forbids it; the one Ibn al-'Arabi and al-Nawawi found soundest turns on whether the figure is complete; another permits it where it is not displayed for honour.
- **Anatomical, botanical, scientific and technical illustration** falls outside every reasoning given above — no imitation of creating is intended, no veneration is possible, and the prohibition's stated *'illa* is nowhere in it.
- **Children's toys are excepted by name.**
- **What is never permitted, on any position, is an image made to be venerated** — that is the case the whole rule exists for, and it is the one modern instance that lands squarely inside it.

If you follow a school, take its position and work inside it; you will not run out of subject matter. If you are choosing between the positions, the two things the sources make you weigh are what you are making and why — not whether you have talent, which nothing here calls into question.
