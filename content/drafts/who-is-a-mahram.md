# Who counts as a mahram?

## Answer

A *mahram* is someone you may never marry — permanently, by a relationship the law recognises. That
permanence is the whole definition, and everything else follows from it: travel, being alone together,
and how you may dress around them.

### The list

The Qur'an names them: **"Forbidden unto you are your mothers, and your daughters, and your sisters, and
your father's sisters, and your mother's sisters, and your brother's daughters and your sister's
daughters, and your foster-mothers, and your foster-sisters, and your mothers-in-law"** [1] — the verse
continues with step-daughters and sons' wives.

Al-Nawawi counts them: **"the women whom the Qur'an has explicitly forbidden are fourteen"** [2], and
sorts them by cause: **"seven of them are forbidden by lineage, two by breastfeeding, four by marriage
ties, and one by combination"** [3]. Al-Sarakhsi puts it as **"the forbidden are fourteen — seven from
the side of lineage and seven from the side of cause"** [4].

**By lineage — seven.** **"The seven forbidden by lineage are: the mother, the daughter, the sister, the
paternal aunt, the maternal aunt, the brother's daughter, and the sister's daughter"** [5].

For a woman, mirror it: father, son, brother, paternal uncle, maternal uncle, nephew.

Each extends in both directions. "Mother" reaches all the way up — **"there is no difference in the
prohibition between the mother in the literal sense, who bore him, and the mother figuratively, meaning
his grandmother, his mother's mother and his father's mother"** [6] — and "niece" all the way down:
**"the brother's daughter is forbidden to him, and everyone attributed to him through the brother's line,
from the daughters of his children and the children of his children, however far down"** [7].

**By breastfeeding — two.** The verse names them: **"your foster-mothers, and your foster-sisters"** [1].
A woman who nursed you in infancy, and her children. Milk kinship creates mahram status as birth does,
and this is where people are most often surprised.

**By marriage — four.** The verse continues past the portion quoted above to name mothers-in-law,
step-daughters "born of your women unto whom ye have gone in", and sons' wives; the fourth, a father's
wife, is stated in the verse immediately before it [1]. These persist after divorce or death — the tie is
"permanent" in the sense [3] requires.

### Who is *not* a mahram

Read the list in [5] for what it omits. **Cousins are not on it** — a first cousin is marriageable in
Islam, so the category does not reach them, whatever the family custom is. The same follows for a
sister's husband, a wife's sister, and an aunt's or uncle's spouse: each is marriageable in some
circumstance, so none is a mahram.

### What the category is for

Three things, in practice.

**Travel.** A woman travelling needs a mahram, in the schools that require one. And whose mahram matters
less than people assume: **"there is no difference between there being with her a mahram of hers — such
as her son, her brother, her mother, her sister — or someone who is a mahram to him, such as his sister,
his daughter, his paternal aunt, his maternal aunt"** [8]. A husband counts too: **"the hadith is also
specified as to the husband, for if her husband were with her he would be as a mahram"** [9].

**Being alone together**, which is prohibited with a non-mahram and permitted with a mahram.

**Dress**, since the covering required in front of unrelated men is relaxed with mahrams.

### One caution

Mahram status is about marriageability, not about trust. The classical books grant the concessions above
because the relationship is permanently non-marriageable — not because relatives are assumed safe. Where
a specific person is not safe, no ruling here obliges anyone to be alone with them or to travel with them,
and the general prohibition on harm overrides the concession.
