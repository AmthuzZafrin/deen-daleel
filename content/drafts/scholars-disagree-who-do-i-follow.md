# Scholars disagree on everything — how do I know who's right?

## Answer

**The short answer is: ask one, act on what they tell you, and stop auditing them against the internet.** But that only helps if you understand why the disagreement exists, so here is what the sources actually say — including an honest look at the line everyone quotes.

**The famous line, and its chain.** People will tell you "the disagreement of my community is a mercy". Ibn 'Abidin quotes it and then, to his credit, immediately grades it. The principle first: "**the disagreement of the imams of guidance is a breadth for the people**" — and this points to the hadith famous on people's tongues, namely *the disagreement of my community is a mercy*" [1]. Then the chain: al-Sakhawi's *al-Maqasid al-Hasana* says it was "narrated by al-Bayhaqi **with a broken chain** from Ibn 'Abbas" [2]; and al-Suyuti says it was transmitted by Nasr al-Maqdisi and by al-Bayhaqi "**without a chain** ... and perhaps it was documented in some of the books of the *huffaz* that have not reached us" [3].

So the slogan is weak. **The principle behind it is not**, and it is attested from people whose words nobody disputes.

'Umar b. 'Abd al-'Aziz: "**It would not please me that the Companions of Muhammad had not disagreed — because had they not disagreed, there would have been no latitude**" [4].

And the best story in the corpus on this question. Harun al-Rashid proposed to Malik that his books be copied and distributed across the Muslim world so that everyone could be held to them. Malik's answer: "**Commander of the Faithful, the disagreement of the scholars is a mercy from God upon this community. Each follows what has been established as sound in his view, and all of them are upon guidance, and every one of them intends God**" [5]. A caliph offering to end the disagreement by decree, and the jurist whose book it was talking him out of it.

**Why a layperson is not required to work it out.** Al-Ghazali is blunt: "**the layman is obliged to seek a fatwa and to follow the scholars**" — against those who said everyone must examine the evidence — "and this is void on two grounds. The first: the consensus of the Companions, for **they used to give fatwas to laypeople and did not command them to attain the rank of *ijtihad***, and that is known necessarily and by mass transmission, from their scholars and their laity" [6].

His second ground is practical and worth reading aloud: "**charging him with attaining the rank of *ijtihad* is impossible**, because it would lead to cultivation and offspring being cut off, and crafts and trades falling idle, and to the ruin of the world if people in their entirety occupied themselves with seeking knowledge — and that would drive the scholars back to seeking a livelihood, and lead to knowledge being effaced, indeed to the destruction of the scholars and the ruin of the world" [7].

**And he heads off the obvious objection — that this is just blind following.** "**Blind following is the acceptance of a statement without evidence**, whereas these people are obliged by what the *mufti* gives as a fatwa **by the evidence of consensus** — just as the judge is obliged to accept the testimony of witnesses, and just as we are obliged to accept a single-narrator report" [8]. Acting on a scholar's answer is not credulity; the obligation to act on it rests on evidence, even though you have not personally examined the evidence for that particular ruling.

Al-Jassas puts the same restriction the other way round: the layman is barred "**from saying anything about any of the cases of law — as to prohibition, permission, obligation or anything else** — and they were bound to refer it to the Messenger and to those in authority among them, so that they might derive its ruling by reasoning upon it from its parallels among what is textually stated" [9].

**A useful thing to know: the argument about *taqlid* is mostly an argument about scholars, not about you.** Al-Ghazali's whole disputed question is whether *a qualified jurist* may follow another rather than judge for himself: "**is it obligatory upon him to exercise *ijtihad*, or is it permissible for him to follow another? This is among what they disagreed about**" — with some claiming consensus that those after the Companions may not be followed, others extending it — "and among those who held the following of a scholar permissible are **Ahmad b. Hanbal, Ishaq b. Rahawayh and Sufyan al-Thawri**" [10]. Al-Ghazali picks a side and then marks its status honestly: "al-Qadi chose the prohibition of a scholar's following the Companions or those after them, and **this is the more apparent view in our estimation — and the question is speculative and open to *ijtihad***" [11].

**On the bad-tempered version of this, Ibn Taymiyya has the diagnosis.** On people ranking their imam or shaykh above others: "**most people speak in this area by conjecture and by what their souls desire**, for they do not know the true ranks of the imams and the shaykhs, and they do not aim at following absolute truth; rather every person's soul desires to give precedence to the one he follows, so he gives him precedence by a conjecture he entertains, **even though he has no proof of it**" [12]. He notes that this "may lead to their disputing, fighting and splitting apart".

His resolution is the framework to hold: "these are questions of *ijtihad* over which the early generations and the imams disputed — **and each of them acknowledged the other in his *ijtihad***. Whoever among them reached the truth has two rewards, and whoever exercised *ijtihad* and erred has one reward, **and his error is forgiven him**" [13]. He applies it directly: whoever finds it preferable to follow al-Shafi'i does not condemn one who finds otherwise.

**And there is a case in scripture of two authorities differing on the same facts.** Al-Nawawi's chapter is titled *the disagreement of those who exercise ijtihad*, on the two women disputing a child: David judged for the elder, Solomon devised a test and judged for the younger. Al-Nawawi's summary of how the commentators reconcile them: "**it is possible that David judged for the elder because of a resemblance he saw in her, or because giving weight to the elder was in his law, or because the child was in her possession and that was a ground of preference in his law**; whereas Solomon reached the inner truth of the case by a stratagem and gentleness" [14]. Two prophets, one case, two rulings, and the tradition's instinct is to find how both were being reasonable.

**So, practically.**

Pick a scholar you have reason to trust — for their learning, and for whether they know your circumstances — and ask them. Act on the answer. That is what the Companions' own practice authorises, and the alternative is a burden al-Ghazali calls impossible.

Do not shop for the answer you want. That is not what the latitude is for, and it turns a mercy into a technique.

When two qualified scholars differ, you have not caught the religion in a contradiction. You have found the ordinary state of a subject that has been argued about since the first generation, in which — on Ibn Taymiyya's framing — the one who got it wrong is still rewarded and still forgiven.

And be careful of the people Ibn Taymiyya describes: certain, partisan, without proof, and heading toward a fight. The scholar who tells you *this is disputed, and here is why I hold what I hold* is showing you something the confident one is hiding.
