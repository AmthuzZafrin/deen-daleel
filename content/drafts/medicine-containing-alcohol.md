# Can I take medicine that contains alcohol?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

## Answer

**Two different questions get asked under this heading, and the sources answer them differently. Separate them first.**

1. May I drink wine, or take an intoxicant as such, as a remedy?
2. May I take a prescribed medicine that contains a small amount of alcohol as a solvent or preservative — a cough syrup, a tincture, a liquid antibiotic?

The classical books answer the first squarely. They reach the second only through one narrow discussion, and they record it as **disputed**.

**On the first: treating with wine is prohibited, and the reasoning is specific.** Ibn Taymiyya: `التداوى بالخمر حرام بنص رسول الله وعلى ذلك جماهير أهل العلم ثبت عنه في الصحيح أنه سئل عن الخمر تصنع للدواء فقال ( إنها داء وليست بدواء` [1] — treating with wine is forbidden by the text of the Messenger's words and on that are the great majority of the scholars; he was asked about wine made for medicine and said: **it is a disease, not a remedy.** And the incident behind the other hadith: `قالت اشتكت بنت لي فنبذت لها في كوز فدخل النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم وهو يغلي فقال ما هذا فأخبرته فقال أن الله لم يجعل شفاءكم فيما حرم عليكم` [2] — a daughter of mine was ill and I steeped a drink for her in a jug; the Prophet came in while it was fermenting and asked what it was; I told him, and he said: God has not put your cure in what He has forbidden you.

**Why treatment is not covered by the necessity that makes carrion lawful.** This is the load-bearing argument and it is worth having exactly. Ibn Taymiyya: `وليس هذا مثل أكل المضطر للميتة فإن ذلك يحصل به المقصود قطعا وليس له عنه عوض والأكل منها واجب فمن اضطر إلى الميتة ولم يأكل حتى مات دخل النار وهنا لا يعلم` [3] — this is not like a starving man eating carrion: there the object is *certainly* attained and there is no substitute, and eating is obligatory, so that one compelled to eat carrion who refuses and dies has entered the Fire. But here — `حصول الشفاء ولا يتعين هذا الدواء بل الله تعالى يعافى العبد بأسباب متعددة والتداوى ليس بواجب عند جمهور العلماء ولا يقاس هذا بهذا` [4] — the cure is not known to result, this particular remedy is not the only route, God heals by many means, and treatment is not obligatory according to the majority; so the one cannot be measured against the other.

Al-Nawawi draws the line with unusual precision: `وكذا قال النووي في الفرق بين جواز إساغة اللقمة لمن شرق بها بالجرعة من الخمر فيجوز وبين التداوي بها فلا يجوز لان الإساغة تتحقق بها بخلاف الشفاء فإنه لا يتحقق` [5] — a gulp of wine to wash down a morsel someone is choking on **is** permitted; treating with it is not; because the swallowing is certainly achieved by it, whereas the cure is not.

So the operative test in these books is not "how impure is it" but **is the effect certain, and is there an alternative.**

**And why intoxicants are treated more strictly than other forbidden substances.** Ibn Hajar: the hadith "God has not put your cure in what He forbade you" `فجوابه ان الحديث محمول على حالة الاختبار وأما في حال الضرورة فلا يكون حراما كالميتة للمضطر` [6] — is to be read as applying in a state of choice; in necessity it is not forbidden, like carrion for the starving. But wine is carved out: `فان ذلك خاص بالخمر ويلتحق به غيرها من المسكر والفرق بين المسكر وبين غيره من النجاسات ان الحد يثبت باستعماله في حالة الاختيار دون غيره ولان شربه يجر إلى مفاسد كثيرة ولأنهم كانوا في الجاهلية يعتقدون أن في الخمر شفاء فجاء الشرع بخلاف معتقدهم` [7] — that is specific to wine, and other intoxicants attach to it; the difference between an intoxicant and other impure things is that the *hadd* punishment attaches to using it by choice, that drinking it drags in many harms, and that the people of the *jahiliyya* believed there was healing in wine, and the revealed law came contradicting their belief.

Three named reasons, one of which is that they were correcting a specific medical belief of their time.

**The qualification that the modern case actually runs through.** The Shafi'is state the general rule about impermissible remedies as conditional: `فهو حرام عند وجود غيره وليس حراما إذا لم يجد غيره` [8] — it is forbidden where something else is available; it is not forbidden where nothing else is found. And then a test that is unusually usable: `وإنما يجوز ذلك إذا كان المتداوي عارفا بالطب يعرف أنه لا يقوم غير هذا مقامه أو أخبره بذلك طبيب مسلم عدل ويكفى طبيب واحد` [9] — it is permitted only where the patient knows medicine himself and knows nothing else will serve in its place, **or an upright Muslim doctor tells him so; and one doctor suffices**.

For intoxicants specifically, though, the same school holds the line: `وغيرهما من المسكر فهل يجوز شربها للتداوي أو العطش فيه أربعة أوجه مشهورة (الصحيح) عند جمهور الأصحاب لا يجوز فيهما` [10] — four well-known positions on drinking them for treatment or for thirst; the sound one, in the majority's view, is that neither is permitted.

**Now the second question — alcohol as an ingredient — and this is the one passage that reaches it.** Ibn al-'Arabi: `اختلف العلماء فيما لو استهلكت في الأطعمة والأدوية هل يجوز استعمال ذلك الطعام أو ذلك الدواء أم لا فأجازه ابن شهاب ومنعه غيره وتردد علماؤنا في ذلك والصحيح أنه لا يجوز لقوله صلى الله عليه وسلم إنها ليست بدواء ولكنها داء` [11] — the scholars differed over the case where it has been *absorbed* into foods and medicines: is it permitted to use that food or that medicine? Ibn Shihab permitted it; others forbade it; our own scholars wavered. And the correct view, in his judgement, is that it is not permitted.

Read what that actually tells you. Ibn al-'Arabi reaches a restrictive conclusion — but he reports **al-Zuhri permitting it**, others forbidding, and his own school unable to settle. Anyone who tells you this question is closed in either direction is overstating the classical position. It is a live disagreement in the books themselves.

The argument on the restrictive side is the general rule: `ما أسكر كثيره فقليله حرام` [12] — what intoxicates in quantity, a little of it is forbidden — and `متى كان كثيره يسكر فهو حرام وهو خمر` [13].

**One further thread, which is where the modern chemical argument begins.** On whether transformation changes the ruling, Ibn al-'Arabi reports: `فإن تغيرت بالأحراق فقد قال ابن حبيب يجوز التداوي بها والصلاة وخففه ابن الماجشون بناء على أن الحرق تطهير لتغير الصفات` [14] — if it is altered by burning, Ibn Habib held treatment with it and prayer permissible, and Ibn al-Majishun was lenient about it, on the basis that burning purifies by changing the properties. His own view goes the other way: `والصحيح عندي أنه لا يتداوى بشيء من ذلك لأن منه عوضا حلالا ولا يوجد في المجاعة من هذه الأعيان عوض حتى لو وجد منها في المجاعة عوضا لم يأكلها كما لا يجوز التداوي بها لوجود العوض ولو أحرقت لبقيت نجسة لأن العين النجسة لا تطهر إلا بالماء الذي جعله الشرع مطهرا للأعيان النجسة` [15] — because a lawful substitute exists.

Note his stated reason, because it is consistent with everything above: **the availability of a substitute** is what decides it, not the substance in isolation.

**So, practically.**

- **Drinking alcohol as a remedy:** prohibited, by the great majority, and the reason is that the cure is not certain and alternatives exist.
- **A prescribed medicine with no viable alternative:** the framework in [8] and [9] is exactly the one that makes room for this — the condition is a doctor's judgement that nothing else will serve, and one doctor suffices.
- **A medicine with a trace of alcohol where an alcohol-free formulation exists:** this is the *istihlak* case, genuinely disputed in these books, and the substitute test in [15] points against it while al-Zuhri's position points the other way. Worth asking a scholar rather than deciding from a label.
- **Do the easy thing first.** Ask the pharmacist whether an alcohol-free preparation of the same drug exists. If it does, the question largely dissolves — and note that on Ibn al-'Arabi's own reasoning, its existence is what makes the other one problematic. If it does not, you are squarely in the case the sources make room for.

**And do not stop a prescribed medication over this while you work it out.** Nothing above requires that, the "no substitute" condition is met by a doctor's word on the majority-quoted formulation, and the corpus's own framing is that the object of the law here is your recovery.

**What this does not settle.** Whether the ethanol used as a solvent in modern pharmaceuticals is *khamr* at all — a chemical distinction between fermented drink and industrially produced or denatured alcohol that these authors did not have and could not have; topical and injected preparations; excipients in vaccines; and alcohol in perfumes and cleaning products, which is a different question again.
