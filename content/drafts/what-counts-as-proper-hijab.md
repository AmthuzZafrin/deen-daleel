# What actually counts as proper covering?

## Answer

The short version: everything except the face and the hands, in a garment that
covers rather than outlines, reaching the feet. The longer version is more
useful, because the sources are unusually frank that the *edges* of this were
disputed from the first generation onwards — and knowing which parts are settled
and which are not tells you which questions are worth asking a scholar.

**What is settled.** Al-Qurtubi states it as consensus: "the Muslims are agreed
that the private parts are 'awra of the man and the woman, and that a woman is
all 'awra except her face and her hands — for they differed over those two" [1].
Note the shape of that sentence: the *rule* is agreed, and the disagreement sits
precisely on the two exceptions. Ibn Qudama gives the practical form: "the
people of knowledge are agreed that a free woman covers her head when she prays,
and that if she prays with her whole head uncovered she must repeat it" [2].
Head covered, body covered, and the argument is over the face and hands.

**The disputed edge, and why it exists.** The verse permits "except what appears
of it", and the earliest authorities read that phrase three different ways. Ibn
al-'Arabi sets them out: "they differed over the outward adornment, on three
positions. The first, that it is the clothing — meaning that only her outer
garments show of her; this is Ibn Mas'ud's view. The second, kohl and the ring;
this is the view of Ibn 'Abbas and al-Miswar. The third, that it is the face and
the hands" [3]. Al-Tabari's chains preserve the raw variety: "the outward
adornment that she is permitted to display: the kohl, the ring, the two
bracelets, and the face" [4], and from Ibn 'Abbas by another route, "the outward
adornment is the face, the kohl of the eye, the dye on the hand, and the ring —
these show in her house to whoever of the people enters upon her" [5]. Ibn
al-'Arabi's own resolution is a good test to carry: "the sound view is that in
every respect it is what is on the face and the hands, because that is what
appears in the prayer and in ihram as an act of worship, and it is what appears
by custom" [6]. What the prayer and the pilgrimage require you to leave
uncovered is the measure of what may be left uncovered.

By contrast, "the inward adornment is the earring, the necklace, the armlet, the
anklet and the like" [7] — jewellery worn on the parts that are covered anyway.

**The schools, stated plainly.** Ibn Qudama: "Malik, al-Awza'i and al-Shafi'i
said: the whole of the woman is 'awra except her face and her hands, and
covering what is besides those is obligatory in the prayer" [8], with the
reasoning that "need calls for uncovering the face for buying and selling, and
the hands for taking and giving" [9]. Al-Qurtubi reports the same as the
majority position: "as for the free woman, all of her is 'awra except the face
and the hands — this is what most of the people of knowledge hold" [10].

**Where Abu Hanifa differs, and it matters practically.** "Abu Hanifa said: the
two feet are not part of the 'awra, because they normally show, so they are like
the face — and if less than a quarter of her hair, or a quarter of her thigh, or
a quarter of her belly is uncovered, her prayer is not invalidated" [11]. That
is a real, named, classical position, and it is the answer to a question people
ask constantly: a lock of hair escaping does not void the prayer in the Hanafi
school. Ibn Qudama disagrees with the feet ruling but reaches a similar place on
small slips: on a little showing, "I do not know of any sound, explicit
statement on it" [12], and he inclines to excuse it, "because guarding against
the small amount is difficult, so it is pardoned, by analogy with the small
amount of a man's 'awra" [13].

**One criterion that is not about extent at all.** Al-Qurtubi, on the end of the
same verse: "a woman is not to strike with her foot when she walks so as to make
the sound of her anklet heard — for making the sound of adornment heard is like
displaying the adornment, and worse" [14]. The principle generalises: covering
is about not drawing notice to what is covered, not only about square inches of
fabric. A covering that announces itself is doing the opposite of the job.

**So, practically.** Hair, neck, chest, arms, legs and feet covered; face and
hands may show, and covering them further is a separate question with its own
answer. Fabric that covers rather than reveals what is under it. Nothing that
turns the covering itself into the thing being looked at. Whether any particular
style — a turban wrap, a shorter scarf — meets that is something you can check
against these criteria directly: does it cover the hair, the neck and the chest,
and is it opaque? If yes, it satisfies what the sources ask for. The rest is
custom and preference, and the sources are silent on it by design.
