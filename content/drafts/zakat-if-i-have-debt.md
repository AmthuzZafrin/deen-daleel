# Do I pay zakat if I'm in debt?

## Answer

Debt reduces or removes what you owe. Ibn Qudama states it as the settled position: **debt prevents the obligation of zakat on hidden wealth, on a single narration** [1] — hidden wealth meaning cash, gold and trade goods, which for most people today is all of it. He lists Ata, Sulayman ibn Yasar, al-Hasan, al-Nakha'i, Malik, al-Thawri, al-Awza'i, Ishaq and the Hanafis on that side.

The evidence he gives is worth seeing, because it is unusually direct. Uthman used to announce at zakat time: **this is the month of your zakat — so whoever has debt, let him pay it, so that you may pay the zakat of your wealth** [2], and Ibn Qudama notes he said it in front of the Companions without any of them objecting. There is also a report from ibn Umar: **if a man has a thousand dirhams and owes a thousand dirhams, he owes no zakat** [3].

The underlying reasoning is about who zakat is for. The Prophet said: **I was commanded to take the charity from your rich and return it to your poor** [4]. Ibn Qudama's point is that a person whose debts equal their assets is not in the class the obligation targets: **the debtor needs to settle his debt as much as a poor person needs, or more** [5].

There is a dissenting view, and it is not obscure. **Rabi'a, Hammad ibn Abi Sulayman, and al-Shafi'i in his later position said it does not prevent zakat** [6] — the reasoning being that the conditions were met, so the duty attached.

What this looks like in practice is the arithmetic Ahmad gave: **he begins with the debt and settles it, then looks at what remains with him after taking out maintenance, and pays zakat on what is left** [7]. Malik puts the same rule the other way round: **if he has, of trade goods and cash, only enough to discharge his debt, he owes no zakat until he has cash in excess of his debt reaching the amount on which zakat is due** [8].

So: subtract what you owe from what you have, and test the remainder against the nisab. If nothing is left, you owe no zakat this year and you are not in arrears — you are in the category zakat exists to help.

One caution about applying this today. A mortgage or a student loan running for twenty years is not the kind of debt these authors had in mind; they are describing sums presently due. Deducting the whole outstanding balance of a long-term loan would wipe out most people's zakat permanently, and most contemporary scholars therefore have you deduct only the payments falling due in the coming year, not the entire balance. If your debts are of that kind, that is the question to put to a scholar — and it is a narrow, answerable one.
