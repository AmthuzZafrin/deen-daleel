# Is there a problem with dressing like everyone else?

## Answer

No — not with dressing like the people around you. The hadith everyone reaches
for here says less than it is usually made to say, and the classical
commentators are the ones who narrowed it.

**The text.** Ibn 'Umar: the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, "whoever imitates a
people is one of them" [1]. Read alone, that could forbid almost anything.

**The commentators would not read it alone.** Ibn Hajar quotes Abu Muhammad b.
Abi Jamra's summary, and it is the single most important sentence on this
question: "the apparent sense of the wording is a rebuke against imitation in
everything — but it is known from the other evidences that what is meant is
imitation in *outward style*, and in some traits and movements and the like, not
imitation in matters of good" [2]. The scope is narrowed twice over: to
appearance and manner, and away from anything praiseworthy.

And the reason he gives for the severity where it does apply: "the wisdom in
cursing the one who imitates is his taking a thing out of the characteristic
that the Wisest of the wise placed upon it" [3] — the same reasoning as the
prohibition on altering the creation. That tells you which cases the hadith is
really aimed at: not clothing that happens to be foreign, but a person
presenting himself as something he is not.

**What the prohibition actually attached to in practice.** Two things, in these
sources. First, the *religiously marked*: Ibn al-'Arabi, on the verse forbidding
taking intimates from outside the community, "there is no disagreement among our
scholars that what is meant by it is the prohibition of companionship with the
disbelievers among the People of the Book — so much so that he forbade imitating
them" [4], and he adds: "it is authentically established from the Prophet ﷺ that
he forbade imitating the non-Arabs" [5].

Second, the *deliberately contrastive*: "the Jews and the Christians do not dye,
so differ from them" [6]. Note the shape of that instruction — it does not
forbid a garment, it prescribes an act of distinction in a matter where the
communities were visibly telling themselves apart.

**And the category has grades, not a single ruling.** Ibn Daqiq al-'Id, on
whether a thing being the mark of non-Muslims makes it forbidden: "this may go
back to the first, since it is among the marks of the idolaters, and both
meanings may be considered — except that the second meaning does not entail
prohibition, because al-Shafi'i said in *al-Umm*: I do not dislike the wearing of
pearls except as a matter of manners, since it is women's attire" [7]. An
imam of a school treating an imitation objection as a question of *adab* rather
than of prohibition. The category is not all-or-nothing.

**So, what does this leave?** Wearing a shirt and trousers because that is what
people wear where you live is not the imitation the hadith names. It is not a
religious marker, it is not a deliberate identification with anyone's creed, and
under Ibn Abi Jamra's reading it does not even engage the wording, which is
aimed at *taking on a characteristic that is not yours*. There is no daleel
here requiring a Muslim in Manchester or Detroit to dress like a seventh-century
Arab, and the sources that discuss men's dress elsewhere give boundaries — 'awra
covered, no silk or gold for men, no ostentation — rather than a costume.

**What would engage it.** Wearing something that specifically marks a religion
not your own: a cross, a clerical collar, the vestments or emblems of another
faith. Dressing so as to be *taken* for something you are not. And, on the same
principle from the other direction, dressing in a way that deliberately erases
any sign that you are Muslim, out of embarrassment at being one — the reasoning
Ibn Abi Jamra gives, about removing a thing from its proper characteristic,
cuts that way too.

The honest summary: the hadith is about identity, not about fabric. The sources
narrowed it deliberately, and reading it broadly is a choice the commentators
themselves declined to make.
