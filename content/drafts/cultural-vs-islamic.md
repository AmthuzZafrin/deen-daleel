# How do I tell the difference between culture and Islam?

## Answer

**The short version.** Ask of any practice: *what is it resting on?* If it rests on a text — a verse, a hadith, or a ruling the scholars drew from these — it belongs to the religion. If it rests on nothing but "this is how we have always done it," it is a custom. Customs are not automatically wrong; most are simply permitted. A custom becomes a problem only when it contradicts a ruling, or when it starts being treated as though it were one.

**The religion has a stated content.** When Jibril came in the form of a man and asked what Islam is, the answer was a list — `الإسلام أن تشهد أن لا إله إلا الله، وأن محمدا رسول الله، وتقيم الصلاة، وتؤتي الزكاة، وتصوم رمضان، وتحج البيت إن استطعت إليه سبيلا` [1] — and the episode closes with `فإنه جبريل، أتاكم يعلمكم دينكم` [2], he came to teach you your religion. Hold on to that: the religion is something that can be itemised and taught. It is not co-extensive with everything Muslims do. And when the Prophet was asked which Islam is best, the answer was acts available to anyone anywhere — `تطعم الطعام، وتقرأ السلام على من عرفت، ومن لم تعرف` [3], feeding people, and greeting with peace both those you know and those you do not. Nothing in that belongs to a nationality.

**But custom sits inside the law, not outside it.** This is the half that usually gets missed, and it matters more for a revert than the first half does. The chapter on custom in *Al-Ashbah wa'l-Naza'ir* opens by saying that the fiqh falls back on `العادة والعرف` in cases beyond counting, and then names them: the age of menstruation and of puberty, the minimum and maximum of menses and of post-natal bleeding [4]. It keeps going. For goods whose weight or measure was not fixed in the Prophet's time, the soundest view is `أنه يراعى فيه عادة بلد البيع` [5] — the custom of the town where the sale takes place. Livestock are pastured by day and secured by night; but `ولو اطردت عادة بلد بعكس ذلك اعتبرت العادة في الأصح` [6] — if a town's settled custom runs the other way, the custom is what governs.

That is not the law tolerating local practice. That is the law running on it. Ibn al-'Arabi makes the mechanism explicit on the verse that fixes a father's maintenance `بالمعروف`: the amount is handed out to `العرف والعادة` for work of that kind, and `ولولا أنه معروف ما أدخله الله تعالى في المعروف` [7] — had it not been what people know as right, God would not have placed it under *ma'ruf*. Revelation left a measure open on purpose and pointed at local practice to fill it in.

So "cultural" is not a synonym for "wrong." A great deal of what a family does at a wedding, at a birth, at a gathering after a funeral, in dress, in food, in which language the du'a is made in, is simply the local way of doing something the law left open. Attacking it as though it were an innovation in religion is its own error, and a common one in the first year or two after conversion.

**Where the line actually falls.** Ibn al-'Arabi, glossing *'urf*, says what is meant is `المعروف من الدين المعلوم من مكارم الأخلاق ومحاسن الأعمال المتفق عليه في كل شريعة` [8] — what is known from the religion, from noble character and good deeds, agreed on in every revealed law. Custom fills in the measure of something the religion already commends. It does not issue a new command. And the limit is set by the hadith he cites immediately after: a commander, angry, ordered his men into a fire they had built for him; they hesitated, `فهموا وجعل بعضهم يمسك بعضا ويقولون فررنا إلى النبي من النار فما زالوا حتى خمدت النار` [9] — holding one another back and saying *we fled to the Prophet from the fire* — until it burnt out. The Prophet's ruling on it was `إنما الطاعة في المعروف يريد الذي يجوز في الدين موقعه ويثبت فيه حكمه` [10]: obedience is only in what is right, meaning what the religion permits and gives a ruling to. That is the answer to "but this is what our family does" when what the family does is something the law forbids. A father's authority, an elder's, a community's, runs out at precisely that point — and not before it.

**The Qur'an condemns an argument, not an inheritance.** Read carefully what is being rejected in `We follow that wherein we found our fathers` [11]. Al-Tabari gives the occasion: when the Prophet called the Jews of Madina to Islam, Rafi' b. Kharija and Malik b. 'Awf answered `بل نتبع ما ألفينا عليه آباءنا فإنهم كانوا أعلم وخيرا منا` [12] — they were more knowledgeable and better than us. And al-Tabari specifies what was being defended: `وقالوا: بل نأتم بآبائنا فنتبع ما وجدناهم عليه من تحليل ما كانوا يحلون وتحريم ما كانوا يحرمون` [13] — the fathers' own making-lawful and making-unlawful. That is the fault. It is not inheritance as such; it is custom that has begun to legislate. Al-Qurtubi sets the same verse against the Arabs' `البحيرة والسائبة والوصيلة` [14] — rules about livestock they had invented themselves and then defended as ancestral.

Al-Tabari then draws out the test that is actually usable: `وإنما يتبع المتبع ذا المعرفة بالشيء المستعمل له في نفسه، فأما الجاهل فلا يتبعه فيما هو به جاهل إلا من لا عقل له ولا تمييز` [15] — one follows a person who knows the thing and practises it; as for the ignorant, only someone without sense follows him in what he is ignorant of. So the question to put to an inherited practice is not "is this cultural?" It is: *does anyone I can ask know where this came from?* If the chain ends at "because this is what we do," you have your answer — not that the practice is forbidden, but that nobody is claiming it as religion, and you are not bound by it.

**Do not over-correct.** Al-Qurtubi is careful in a way that is easy to miss. Yes: `وقوة الفاظ هذه الآية تعطي إبطال التقليد` [16] — the force of this verse's wording abolishes *taqlid*. But he immediately restricts it: that holds `في الباطل صحيح أما التقليد في الحق فأصل من أصول الدين وعصمة من عصم المسلمين يلجأ إليها الجاهل المقصر عن درك النظر واختلف العلماء في جوازه في مسائل الأصول على ما يأتي وأما جوازه في مسائل الفروع فصحيح` [17] — in falsehood, yes; but *taqlid* in the truth is a foundation of the religion and a protection the Muslims rely on, a refuge for someone who lacks the training to derive rulings himself, and in matters of the branches it is simply sound. Deferring to qualified scholars on the detail of the law is not what the verse attacks. Someone who reads these verses and concludes that all received practice is suspect and that they will work everything out from the text alone has walked into a different error. What is condemned is defending a practice *only* by who did it before you — above all when the practice is declaring things lawful or unlawful.

**In practice, take one thing at a time.** Three outcomes, and most disputes come from confusing them:

1. It has a daleel. Keep it, and now you know why you keep it.
2. It has no daleel but contradicts none — it fills in something the law left open. It is permitted, it is your family's, and it does not need to be fought.
3. It contradicts a ruling, or it is being enforced as obligatory when nothing made it obligatory. That is the one to leave, and `إنما الطاعة في المعروف` is the ground you leave it on.

Most of the friction in a convert's family is category 2 being mistaken for category 1 or 3 — a permitted custom denounced as un-Islamic, or a permitted custom insisted on as a religious duty. Both are the same mistake in opposite directions.

**What this does not settle.** Which particular practice falls into which category is a question about that practice, and the sources here do not supply a list; some cases are genuinely disputed between the schools. If the practice is a matter of worship, or if leaving it will break something in the family, that is worth putting to a scholar who knows both the fiqh and your circumstances rather than deciding alone.
