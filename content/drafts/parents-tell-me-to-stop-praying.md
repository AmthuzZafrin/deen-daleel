# My parents tell me to stop practising — what do I do?

## Answer

You keep praying, and you keep being good to them. Those are not in tension in these sources, and holding both is the whole task.

**Why you do not obey them in this.**

Al-Jassas: "**obedience to parents is obligatory in what is good, not in disobedience to God — for there is no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator**" [1].

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr states the principle in its widest form, where it is being applied to a ruler people have pledged allegiance to: "obedience is not binding except in what is good, because the Messenger of Allah ﷺ never commanded anything but good, and he said: **obedience is only in what is right**. **And the scholars are agreed that whoever commands a wrong, obedience to him is not binding**" [2].

And the warning attached to obeying anyway is severe. Someone asked 'Ubada b. al-Samit what would happen if he did obey such a command: "**he said: your limbs will be taken and you will be cast into the Fire** — and let that one come and rescue you" [3].

If the pledge of allegiance to a ruler does not compel obedience in a wrong, a parent's word certainly does not.

**And why your duty to them is unchanged.**

Ibn Kathir, on *"and thy Lord hath decreed... that ye be kind to parents"*: "**and for this reason He joined kindness to parents with worship of Him** — as He said in the other verse: *give thanks to Me and to your parents; unto Me is the journeying*" [4].

The verses that follow are the ones to hold onto when they are at their worst: *"say not to them 'uff' nor repel them, but speak to them a gracious word; and lower to them the wing of humility out of mercy, and say: my Lord, have mercy on them as they reared me when I was small."*

And the Qur'an describes what a mother did before it describes what you owe: *"His mother beareth him with reluctance, and bringeth him forth with reluctance, and the bearing of him and the weaning of him is thirty months"* [5].

**What this looks like in practice, day to day.**

- **Do not stop praying.** Not for a week, not to keep the peace, not until things calm down. Prayer is the thing being asked of you and it is the one thing that cannot be given.
- **Do not make it a confrontation.** You are not obliged to pray where they will see it, or to announce each prayer, or to argue about it. Pray in your room. Pray at work. Move the argument off the table without moving the prayer.
- **Be conspicuously better to them, not worse.** The most persuasive thing available to you is that their child became more helpful, more patient, more present. Most parents' real fear is losing you, and only your behaviour can answer that.
- **Do not say *uff*.** The verse forbids the smallest sound of exasperation, and it was revealed about parents in old age, not about parents who agree with you.
- **Say the du'a for them** — *my Lord, have mercy on them as they reared me when I was small* — especially after a bad conversation. It is hard to stay resentful of someone you have just asked God to have mercy on.
- **Give it time.** Most parental hostility to a revert's practice softens over years as it becomes ordinary. Almost none of it survives a child who keeps turning up.

**If it escalates.**

If you are being threatened, thrown out, or physically harmed, that is a different situation from disapproval, and none of the above requires you to stay in danger. Get safe first; the obligation of kindness does not oblige you to be harmed, and there is nothing in these texts that says otherwise. Seek help from people who can actually help — and if the household is unsafe, from the authorities.

**One thing to hold onto.** Nothing in these sources treats this as a sign you have chosen wrongly. The obedience exception exists precisely *because* the situation was foreseen: the Qur'an describes parents actively pressing their child toward disbelief and, in the same verse, orders the child to keep them company kindly. You are inside a case the revelation already accounted for.
