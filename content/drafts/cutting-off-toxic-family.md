# Can I cut off family who treat me badly?

## Answer

The honest shape of this answer is: the obligation is real and heavy, the standard held up as ideal is very high, **and the minimum is much lower than most people think** — and it is that last point, which is the one people actually need, that gets left out when this question is answered from the headline texts alone.

Start with the headline texts, because they are not in doubt. Qadi 'Iyad, quoted by al-Nawawi, states it without qualification: "there is no disagreement that keeping ties of kinship is obligatory in general, and cutting them is a major sin" [1]. Al-Jassas cites the Prophet: "there is nothing God is obeyed in whose reward is swifter than keeping ties of kinship, and no act by which God is disobeyed whose punishment is swifter than oppression and the false oath" [2]. Ibn Hajar cites 'A'isha's report that "keeping ties of kinship, being a good neighbour, and good character build up homes and lengthen lives" [3].

And the standard held up as the real thing is not reciprocity. "The one who keeps ties is not the one who repays in kind; rather, the one who keeps ties is the one who, when his kinship is severed, restores it" [4]. 'Umar's version is blunter: "keeping ties is not that you keep ties with whoever keeps ties with you — that is settling a score; keeping ties is that you keep ties with the one who cut you off" [5]. Al-Jassas carries the same idea in a different currency: "the best charity is to a hostile relative" — al-Humaydi glossing *kashih* as "the enemy" [6].

**Now the part that answers your question.** Ibn Hajar, commenting on that very hadith, refuses to let it collapse into a binary: "it does not follow from the negation of *keeping ties* that *cutting* is established. They are three degrees — the one who keeps ties, the one who repays in kind, and the one who cuts. The one who keeps ties is the one who gives without being given to; the one who repays in kind is the one who does not exceed in giving what he receives; and the one who cuts is the one who is given to and does not give" [7]. Failing to reach the highest degree is not falling into the lowest one.

Qadi 'Iyad makes the same point as a rule about the minimum, and this is the single most useful sentence on the sheet: "the ties are degrees, some higher than others, and **the lowest of them is refraining from shunning, and keeping the tie by speech, even if only by a greeting**; and that differs according to capacity and need, so some of it is obligatory and some is recommended. If someone keeps part of the tie but does not keep it to its furthest extent, he is not called a cutter — though if he falls short of what he is able to do and ought to do, he is not called a keeper of ties either" [8].

Read that carefully, because it does a great deal of work:

- **The floor is *tark al-muhajara*** — not shunning — plus contact by speech, "even if only by a greeting". That is the obligation. Not intimacy, not frequent visits, not access to your home, not tolerance of how you are treated.
- **It scales with capacity and need.** 'Iyad says so explicitly. What is obligatory for someone with resources and safety is not the same as what is obligatory for someone without them.
- **Partial ties are still ties.** Someone who maintains a limited, distant, greeting-level relationship is, in his own words, "not called a cutter".

The severe warning has a matching limit. On "no cutter of ties enters Paradise", al-Nawawi gives the first of two readings as applying to "one who deems the cutting of ties lawful **without cause and without doubt**, while knowing it is forbidden" [9]. The condition "without cause" is his, not mine, and it is doing real work in the sentence.

**Who counts as kin at all is itself disputed**, and the disputed zone is wide. Al-Kasani reports it as a school difference: "there is no disagreement that severing the tie of parentage is forbidden, and no disagreement that kinship which does not bar marriage, such as that between cousins, is not forbidden to sever — al-Shafi'i attaches [sibling, uncle and aunt kinship] to cousin kinship, and we attach it to parentage" [10]. So on the Shafi'i side of that, siblings, uncles and aunts fall outside the strict prohibition; on the Hanafi side they fall inside it. Al-Nawawi and al-Qurtubi both take the wider view instead — al-Nawawi calls it "the correct position" that every relative in the inheritance sense counts, mahram or not [11], and al-Qurtubi says "the correct view is that everything the term *rahim* includes and covers must be kept, in every case" [12]. What every one of them agrees on is the parent–child tie. The further out you go, the thinner the obligation gets and the more the scholars disagree that it exists at all. Al-Qurtubi adds the ordering rule for when they compete: "when the claims crowd one another, one begins with the nearest, then the next nearest" [13].

**What this sheet does not contain, and you should know that it doesn't:** there is nothing here on abuse — no passage addressing violence, coercion, or a relative who is a danger. Not one of the works retrieved for this question rules on that situation, so nothing above should be read as speaking to it. Its silence is not permission and it is not prohibition; it is silence, and a question that serious deserves a scholar who knows the specifics, alongside whatever protection the situation actually requires.

What the sheet does support is narrower and still worth having. The obligation is to keep a tie, at a level that varies with what you are able to do. Its floor is not shunning and not refusing to speak. Nowhere in these passages is the obligation defined as unlimited access, unlimited contact, or accepting mistreatment as the price of not being a *qati'* — and the jurist who set the floor also said, in the same breath, that a partial tie is still a tie.
