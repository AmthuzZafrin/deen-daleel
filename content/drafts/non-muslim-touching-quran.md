# Can a non-Muslim touch or hold the Quran?

## Answer

This is one of the questions where the classical discussion is about something quite different from what people are usually asking, so it is worth separating the two.

**The classical question was about *capture*, not about a friend picking a book off your shelf.** The governing text is Ibn 'Umar's report, in *Sahih Muslim*: "Do not travel with the Qur'an, for I do not feel safe that the enemy may get hold of it" — and the narrator Ayyub adds a remark that has aged strikingly: "**the enemy did get hold of it, and disputed with you using it**" [1]. Ibn Hajar makes the object explicit: "*do not travel with the Qur'an to the land of the enemy*, out of dislike that the enemy should get hold of it — and what is meant is not what is in the breasts, but what is in the written pages" [2].

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr reports the scope agreed and disputed: "the jurists are agreed that one does not travel with the Qur'an to the land of the enemy in raiding parties and the small army that is feared for; and they differed over the permissibility of that in the large army that is secure. Malik said: one does not travel with the Qur'an to the land of the enemy, and he did not distinguish between the large army and the small. Abu Hanifa said it is disliked to travel with the Qur'an to the land of the enemy except in the great army, in which case there is no harm in it" [3]. Ibn Hajar adds that the Shafi'is "made the dislike turn on the presence or absence of fear" [4]. So even the strictest version is a rule about a risk, and two of the three schools let it vary with whether the risk is present.

**The strict argument for keeping a *mushaf* away from a non-Muslim is set out by Ibn 'Abd al-Barr**, and stated fairly: "the argument of those who disliked it is God's saying *the idolaters are unclean* and the Messenger of God's saying *none shall touch the Qur'an but one who is pure*; and it is known that part of holding the Qur'an above defilement, and of honouring it, is keeping it away from filth and impurities — and its being with the people of unbelief exposes it to that, and is a degradation of it" [5]. He extends it to coins: "Malik and others disliked that a disbeliever be given a dirham or a dinar with a *sura* or a verse of God's Book on it, and I know of no disagreement in this when it is a complete verse or a *sura*" [6]. Ibn Hajar reports the sale question as settled: "it was argued from this for the prohibition of selling a *mushaf* to a disbeliever, because the meaning mentioned is present in it — namely the possibility of degrading it. **And there is no disagreement about the prohibition of that**" [7].

**But the verse everyone reaches for is read quite differently in the chapter arrangement Ibn Hajar is commenting on.** He is explaining a chapter that pairs *none toucheth it save the purified* with *the likeness of those who are entrusted with the Law of Moses, yet apply it not, is as the likeness of the ass carrying books*: "the upshot of this interpretation is that the meaning of *none shall touch the Qur'an* is: **none finds its taste and its benefit except one who believes in it and is certain that it is from God** — so he is the one purified of unbelief; and none carries it as it deserves except one purified of ignorance and doubt, not the one heedless of it who does not act, who is then like the donkey carrying what it does not understand" [8]. On that reading the verse is not about hands at all. It does not settle the question either way — but it does mean the verse cannot simply be produced as though it did.

**Teaching a non-Muslim the Qur'an is a live and wide disagreement**, and it is the part most relevant to a reader with non-Muslim family and friends. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr: "they differed in this chapter over teaching the disbeliever the Qur'an. Abu Hanifa's position is that there is no harm in teaching the *harbi* and the *dhimmi* the Qur'an and *fiqh*; and Malik said they are not to be taught the Qur'an nor writing" [9]. Ibn Hajar adds the Shafi'i position and a Maliki refinement: "Malik prohibited absolutely, the Hanafis permitted absolutely, and from al-Shafi'i there are two positions; and some of the Malikis distinguished between the small amount, for the benefit of establishing the argument against them — which they permitted — and the large amount, which they prohibited" [10].

Ibn Hajar's own resolution is the most useful sentence on the sheet, because it names the actual variable: "what appears is that the preferable view is the distinction between one from whom a desire for the religion and entering it is hoped, with safety from his using it to attack it, and one of whom it is established that this will not benefit him, or who is thought likely to use it as a means of attacking the religion" [11].

**And the Prophet's own practice cuts in the permissive direction.** He wrote to Heraclius, a non-Muslim ruler, a letter containing Qur'anic verses. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr treats that as the answer to the objection: "*is it permissible for a Muslim to write to a disbeliever a letter containing a verse from God's Book?* It is said to him: as for when he is being called to Islam, or there is a necessity for it — then there is no harm in it" [12]. Ibn Hajar goes further and reports it as agreed: "and the story of Heraclius supports it, where the Prophet wrote to him some of the verses ... **and al-Nawawi transmitted the agreement on the permissibility of writing to them with the like of that**" [13].

**So, practically.**

If someone is genuinely interested — a friend who wants to read it, a family member who asks — the sources are not against you. That is the Heraclius case, and it is the case Ibn Hajar's test is designed to permit. Even Malik's stricter school produced a distinction allowing enough to make the argument.

If someone wants it in order to mock it or mine it for attacks, that is the situation every one of these jurists had in mind, and their caution is not unreasonable.

Handing over a bound Arabic *mushaf* is the narrowest case and the one where the strict position is strongest — Ibn Hajar reports no disagreement on selling one to a non-Muslim. **What none of these passages address is a translation**, which is what someone who does not read Arabic would actually be given. The rulings here are about the Arabic codex; the sheet is silent on the other, and the silence should be read as silence.

And nothing here restricts reading it aloud to someone, explaining it, or discussing it. The concern throughout is possession of the physical book by someone who may degrade it — not the message reaching a non-Muslim, which the Prophet's own letter shows was the point.
