# Can I have a normal savings account?

## Answer

**Yes to having the account; no to taking the interest. And the reason is worth following, because it turns on what a bank deposit actually is in these books' terms — which is not a deposit.**

**Your money in a bank is a loan, not safekeeping.** The distinction is a real one in the law. A `وديعة` — something left with someone for keeping — stays yours: `وقال ابن المنير أدخل هذه الثلاثة إما لان الحديث مطلق وإما لأنه وارد في البيع والآخران أولى لان ملك الوديعة لم ينتقل` [1] — the ownership of a thing deposited for safekeeping does not transfer. The keeper may not use it; he holds your property.

A bank does the opposite. It takes ownership of the money, lends it on, and owes you the amount back. That is `قرض`, a loan — and you are the lender, the bank the borrower. Every rule below follows from that one identification, and if it is right, your question is not about a novel modern instrument at all. It is the oldest question in this chapter.

**The rule for loans.** Ibn Taymiyya states it with worked examples: `كل قرض جر منفعة فهو ربا مثل أن يبايعه أو يؤاجره ويحابيه في المبايعة والمؤاجرة لأجل قرضه` [2] — every loan that draws a benefit is usury; as when he sells to him or rents to him and gives him favourable terms **on account of the loan**. He also reports the agreement, and its extension: `وقد اتفق العلماء على أن المقرض متى اشترط زيادة على قرضه كان ذلك حراما وكذلك اذا تواطآ على ذلك في أصح قولي العلماء` [3] — the scholars agreed that when the lender **stipulates** an increase on his loan that is forbidden, and likewise where the two merely have an understanding about it.

That second clause is the one that closes the door on any clever framing. A published interest rate is exactly a prior understanding between lender and borrower about an increase.

**How stringent this got, in cases small enough to be startling.**

'Umar lent Ubayy b. Ka'b ten thousand dirhams, and `أن عمر أسلف أبي بن كعب عشرة آلاف درهم، فأهدى إليه أبي بن كعب من ثمرة أرضه، فردها عليه، ولم يقبلها` [4] — when Ubayy sent him fruit from his land, he sent it back and would not accept it.

'Abdullah b. Salam's advice to someone travelling to Iraq: `إنك تأتي أرضا فاش فيها الربا، فإن أقرضت رجلا قرضا، فأتاك بقرضك ومعه هدية، فاقبض قرضك، واردد عليه هديته` [5] — you are going to a land where usury is widespread; if you lend a man money and he brings your loan back along with a gift, take your loan and **return his gift to him**.

And Ibn 'Abbas did the arithmetic. A man had lent a fishmonger twenty dirhams and been given a fish worth thirteen: `وقال رجل لابن عباس أنى أقرضت سماكا عشرين درهما فأهدى لي سمكة فقومتها ثلاثة عشر درهما فقال لا تاخذ منه الا سبعة دراهم` [6] — take only seven dirhams from him. The benefit was valued and deducted from the principal.

If a fish is counted, a percentage is not a borderline case.

**Now the part that decides whether you may hold the account at all — and it is a live classical disagreement, on precisely this point.**

The old instrument closest to a bank is the `سفتجة`: you hand over money in one town and take a written note redeemable in another, so that your money need not travel the roads. Al-Nawawi's school forbids it: `ولا يجوز أن يقرضه دراهم على أن يعطيه بدلها في بلد آخر، ويكتب له بها صحيفة (كمبيالة) فيأمن خطر الطريق ومؤونة الحمل، وهو مذهبنا، وخالفنا أحمد وغيره` [7]. And the stated reason is remarkable: `دليلنا أن أمن الطريق منفعة، وكل قرض جر نفعا فهو ربا` [8] — **our proof is that safety of the road is a benefit**, and every loan drawing a benefit is usury.

On that view, the mere fact that your money is safer with the borrower than with you is already the forbidden gain.

The other side is the larger one. Ibn Qudama: `وكذلك إن كتب له بها سفتجة، أو قضاه في بلد آخر، جاز. ورخص في ذلك ابن عمر، وسعيد بن المسيب، والحسن، والنخعي، والشعبي، والزهري، ومكحول، وقتادة، ومالك، والشافعي، وإسحاق` [9] — permitted, and licensed by that long list of authorities.

So the tradition has already argued about whether safekeeping and convenience are themselves a usurious benefit, and most of it said no. **That disagreement, not any modern reasoning, is the ground on which holding an account for safety and convenience stands.** It is also why the interest is a separate matter: nobody in that dispute was arguing about a stipulated percentage.

**The line the books draw, stated exactly.** An extra given afterwards, unstipulated and unexpected, is lawful — Ibn Qudama's reason being `ولأنه لم يجعل تلك الزيادة عوضا في القرض، ولا وسيلة إليه، ولا إلى استيفاء دينه، فحلت` [10] — because that increase was not made a consideration in the loan, nor a means to it, nor a means to recovering the debt.

Apply that test to bank interest and it fails on every clause. It is a consideration, it is a means to it, and it is agreed in advance.

What is owed back is the principal and nothing more. Malik: `فأما الربا فإنه لا يكون فيه الا الرد أبدا ولا يجوز منه قليل ولا كثير ولا يجوز فيه ما يجوز في غيره لأن الله تبارك وتعالى قال في كتابه وإن تبتم فلكم رؤوس أموالكم لا تظلمون ولا تظلمون` [11] — in usury there is only return, always; neither a little of it nor a lot is allowed. And the verse behind the whole chapter: *"Those who swallow usury cannot rise up save as he ariseth whom the devil hath prostrated by (his) touch"* [12].

**Where this reasoning has to stop.**

It rests on the identification in the first paragraph, and that identification is a judgement rather than a text. Whether a modern bank deposit is really a `قرض` in the classical sense is arguable — the contract is defined by statute rather than between two people, the money is guaranteed by a scheme rather than by the borrower, and you cannot negotiate its terms. Nothing here settles that, and a scholar who reasons differently about it will reach different conclusions further down.

Nor do these books weigh the erosion of a currency's value over time against the prohibition. That argument — that interest merely restores what inflation removed — is modern, and there is no text in this collection to test it against. Treat anyone who presents it as settled, in either direction, with caution.

**What to do.**

If your bank offers a current account that pays no interest, use that; on the majority view above, holding it for safekeeping is not the problem. Some banks will let you switch interest off on an existing account — ask. If you have already been credited interest, that is a separate question with its own answer, and the short version is that it is not yours to enjoy and not to be simply left with the bank.

If the only accounts available to you pay interest automatically and cannot be changed, that is a question of need, and need is assessed by someone who knows your situation. Take it to a scholar with the actual product terms in hand rather than deciding it from a page.
