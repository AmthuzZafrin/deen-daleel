# Does reading a translation count as reading Quran?

## Answer

Two questions get run together here and they have opposite answers. **Reading a translation to understand the Qur'an is not only permitted, it is something Ibn Taymiyya treats as an obligation on the community.** *Reciting* a translation in place of the Arabic — in the prayer — is the thing the schools argued about, and even there one of them permitted it.

**A translation is not the Qur'an, and the sources say so plainly.** Al-Qurtubi, on *and if We had appointed it a Lecture in a foreign tongue*: "and when this is established, in it is evidence that the Qur'an is Arabic, that it was revealed in the language of the Arabs, that it is not foreign, **and that when it is transferred from that language to another it is not Qur'an**" [1]. He gives the reason the Arabic mattered: it "was revealed in their tongue so that the meaning of its inimitability would be established, since they are the people most knowledgeable about the kinds of speech, in verse and in prose — and when they were unable to match it, that was among the clearest evidence that it is from God" [2].

Ibn Taymiyya draws the practical consequence, and it is a permission rather than a restriction: "for this reason, when the commentator explains it and the translator translates it, his commentary and his translation are not called *Qur'an* — rather **the Muslims are agreed on the permissibility of one in a state of impurity touching books of *tafsir***, and they are agreed that the prayer is not valid with its commentary" [3]. That is the whole practical answer to whether you may pick up a translation: it is a book about the Qur'an, and the rules attaching to a *mushaf* do not attach to it.

**And the positive case for translation is made by Ibn Taymiyya at length.** He first distinguishes three levels of the work: "translation and commentary are three tiers. The first is the translation of the bare wording, as in rendering a word by a synonymous word ... the second is the translation of the meaning and its exposition, by depicting the meaning for the addressee ... the third tier is showing the soundness of that and verifying it by mentioning the evidence and the reasoning that establishes that meaning" [4]. Then the duty: "it is known that the community is commanded to convey the Qur'an, its wording and its meaning, as the Messenger was commanded to do; and conveying God's message can only be thus; **and conveying it to non-Arabs may require translating it for them, so it is translated for them as far as is possible**" [5].

So: a translation is not the Qur'an; producing translations for people who do not read Arabic is part of conveying the message; and reading one carries none of the *mushaf*'s handling rules.

**The dispute was about prayer, and it is a genuine four-way disagreement.** Al-Sarakhsi lays it out: "the root of this question is: if he recites in his prayer in Persian, it is permitted according to Abu Hanifa, though disliked; and according to the two of them it is not permitted if he knows Arabic well, and if he does not know it well it is permitted; and according to al-Shafi'i, recitation in Persian is not permitted in any case — but if he does not know Arabic and is unlettered, he prays without recitation" [6].

Each side's reasoning is preserved. Abu Yusuf and Muhammad: "the Qur'an is inimitable, and the inimitability is in the composition and the meaning; so when he is capable of both, the obligation is discharged only by both. And when he is incapable of the composition, he brings what he is capable of — like one incapable of bowing and prostrating, who prays by gesture" [7]. Ability, not language, is the criterion for them.

Abu Hanifa's evidence is the most interesting thing on this sheet for anyone learning: "Abu Hanifa argued from what is narrated, that the Persians wrote to Salman asking him to write the Fatiha for them in Persian — **and they used to recite that in the prayer until their tongues became supple for the Arabic**" [8]. A transitional accommodation for people who could not yet manage the Arabic, with the expectation that it was transitional.

Even Abu Hanifa put a condition on it that rules out using a commentary: "then, according to Abu Hanifa, it is only permitted when he recites in Persian if he is certain that it is the meaning of the Arabic; but if he prays with the *tafsir* of the Qur'an, it is not permitted, because it is not established with certainty" [9].

Ibn al-'Arabi records the Hanafi argument from *Lo! this is in the former scrolls, the Books of Abraham and Moses* and answers it: "God sent no messenger save with the tongue of his people, as He informed, and He sent down no Book save in their language ... all of that being an easing from Him upon them and a bringing near for their understanding; and everyone whom He made to understand in his own language is bound to worship by his own law. And each Book has a name in their language: its name in the language of Moses is the Torah, its name in the language of Jesus is the Gospel, and its name in the language of Muhammad is the Qur'an. So we were told: *recite the Qur'an* — and it therefore binds us to worship God with what is named *Qur'an*" [10].

And Ibn Taymiyya notes that the Hanafi position may not even have stood: "the statement narrated from Abu Hanifa — it is said that he retracted it, and it is said that it was conditional on calling the translation *Qur'an*" [11].

One curious consequence from within the Hanafi school shows how seriously the meaning is taken even by those who distinguish it from the wording. Qadi Khan, on the prostration of recitation: "if it is recited in Persian, the prostration becomes obligatory on him and on whoever hears it, whether the hearer understood or did not understand" [12].

**So, practically.**

Read translations. Read several — they disagree, and the disagreements are where the interesting questions are. Nothing here restricts that, and the case for producing them is made by one of the tradition's most-cited authorities as part of conveying the message.

Understand what you are holding: a book *about* the Qur'an, not the Qur'an. That is why the handling rules do not follow it, and it is also why no translation should be treated as settling a disputed meaning on its own.

For the prayer, learn the Arabic. The majority position requires it, and even the position that allowed otherwise framed it — in the one historical case it rests on — as something to do until the tongue comes right. If you cannot yet manage a *sura*, that is a specific question with school-specific answers on this very page, and worth asking a scholar rather than resolving from a summary.
