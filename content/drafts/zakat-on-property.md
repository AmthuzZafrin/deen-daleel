# Do I pay zakat on a house or land I own?

## Answer

No — not on the house you live in, and not on land or a building you simply own. Zakat is not a property tax. It falls on wealth that is held for growth, and a home is held for use.

Al-Marghinani states the rule and its reason in one sentence: **there is no zakat on dwelling houses, clothing worn on the body, household furnishings, riding animals, servants, or weapons in use, because these are taken up by essential need and are not growing either** [1]. Two conditions are doing the work — the thing meets a need, and it does not increase. A house you live in fails both tests for zakatability.

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr reports this as the settled view across the schools, and puts houses explicitly on the list: **the scholars ranked alongside slaves and horses — clothing, bedding, vessels, jewels, all trade articles, houses, and everything one keeps** [2]. He adds the exception that decides most real cases: **this is so among the scholars as long as trade was not intended by it or by part of it; if trade is intended by any of it, zakat is obligatory on it in the view of most scholars** [3].

**So the question is not what you own. It is why you hold it.** The same building is outside zakat as a home and inside it as stock.

That makes intention the deciding fact, and the jurists tightened it so that a passing wish does not turn a house into merchandise. Al-Nawawi gives the Shafi'i test: **a chattel does not become trade goods except by two conditions — first, that he came to own it by a contract involving a counter-exchange, such as sale, hire, marriage or khul'; and second, that he intended at the moment of the contract that he was acquiring it for trade** [4]. It follows that **if he came to own it by inheritance, bequest, or gift without a stipulated return, it does not become trade goods by intention** [5] — inheriting a flat and later thinking you might sell it does not make it zakatable stock. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr states the same negative directly: **it makes no difference whether a person inherited them, or they were given to him, or he bought them to keep** [6].

**In practice:**

- **The house you live in:** no zakat, ever, however much it is worth.
- **A second home, a plot, a garage you keep:** no zakat on the asset itself.
- **Property bought as stock to resell** — the developer's site, the flipper's flat: this is trade goods. Its market value on your zakat date joins the pool you pay 2.5% on.
- **Rent you receive:** the sources tax money, not buildings. Rent, once it reaches you, is money. It sits in your account and is counted like any other cash on your zakat date. Al-Jassas records the rate the early governors actually applied to traders' capital: **for this reason 'Umar b. 'Abd al-'Aziz wrote to his agents to take from what a Muslim passes with of merchandise, from every twenty dinars, half a dinar** [7] — a quarter of a tenth, the same 2.5%.

One thing the classical texts do not settle, because the arrangement did not exist in their form: property bought specifically to let out for income, held indefinitely rather than to resell. The reasoning above pulls two ways — the building is not merchandise, but it is plainly held for growth rather than use. Contemporary scholars divide on whether zakat is then owed on the rental income only, or on a portion of the asset. If your case is a buy-to-let portfolio rather than a home, that is worth asking as its own question.
