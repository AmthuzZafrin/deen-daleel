# Why are men and women treated differently in Islam?

## Answer

**Start where the sources start, not where the argument usually starts.** On standing before God there is no differentiation at all, and the Qur'an makes the point by grammar: ten categories, each named twice, once in the masculine and once in the feminine, with one shared outcome — `men who surrender unto Allah, and women who surrender, and men who believe and women who believe, and men who obey and women who obey, and men who speak the truth and women who speak the truth, and men who persevere (in righteousness) and women who persevere, and men who are humble and women who are humble, and men who give alms and women who give alms, and men who fast and women who fast, and men who guard their modesty and women who guard (their modesty), and men who remember Allah much and women who remember - Allah hath prepared for them forgiveness and a vast reward` [1]. Belief, obedience, truthfulness, endurance, humility, charity, fasting, chastity, remembrance: same obligations, same reward, itemised in pairs so the point cannot be missed.

Nor is there differentiation in the law protecting the person. Malik, on the verse of retaliation: `ان القصاص يكون بين الأناث كما يكون بين الذكور والمرأة الحرة تقتل بالمرأة الحرة كما يقتل الحر بالحر والأمة تقتل بالأمة كما يقتل العبد بالعبد والقصاص يكون بين النساء كما يكون بين الرجال والقصاص أيضا يكون بين الرجال والنساء` [2] — retaliation runs between women as it runs between men, and between men and women. He states the consequence flatly: `فنفس المرأة الحرة بنفس الرجل الحر وجرحها بجرحه` [3] — her life for his life, her wound for his wound.

So the differentiation, wherever it lies, does not lie in worth before God or in the value of a life.

**Where it does lie, and the reason the texts give.** The verse everyone reaches for is `Men are in charge of women, because Allah hath made the one of them to excel the other, and because they spend of their property (for the support of women)` [4]. Ibn Kathir's gloss is worth reading as he actually wrote it rather than in a softened form: `الرجل قيم على المرأة، أي هو رئيسها وكبيرها والحاكم عليها ومؤدبها إذا اعوجت` [5] — the man is the one who stands over the woman, her head, the one in authority over her, correcting her when she deviates. And on "excel": `لأن الرجال أفضل من النساء، والرجل خير من المرأة؛ ولهذا كانت النبوة مختصة بالرجال وكذلك الملك الأعظم` [6] — men are superior to women, and the man is better than the woman, which is why prophethood was restricted to men, and likewise supreme rule. That is what the text says. It is not helpful to be told otherwise and then discover it later.

But note that the verse gives a second ground alongside the first, and Ibn Kathir spells it out as the obligations that run the other way: `من المهور والنفقات والكلف التي أوجبها الله عليهم لهن في كتابه وسنة نبيه` [7] — dowries, maintenance, and the burdens God imposed on men for women's sake. The authority in the verse is tied to a liability that is not reciprocal: he owes her the *mahr* and her upkeep whatever her own means, and she owes him nothing of the kind.

(The remainder of that verse, on what a husband may do in a case of *nushuz*, is a separate and heavily disputed question, and is not addressed here.)

**And the same corpus states the relation as reciprocal, in the same verse that states the asymmetry.** `And they (women) have rights similar to those (of men) over them in kindness, and men are a degree above them` [8] — both clauses together. Al-Tabari collects what the early commentators made of the first clause. Al-Dahhak: `إذا أطعن الله، وأطعن أزواجهن، فعليه أن يحسن صحبتها، ويكف عنها أذاه، وينفق عليها من سعته` [9] — he must keep good company with her, hold his harm back from her, and spend on her from his means. Ibn Zayd put it as a symmetry of God-consciousness: `يتقون الله فيهن كما عليهن أن يتقين الله فيهم` [10] — they fear God concerning them as the women must fear God concerning them. And Ibn 'Abbas drew a personal conclusion from it that is startling in its plainness: `إني لأحب أن أتزين للمرأة، كما أحب أن تتزين لي` [11] — I like to make myself attractive for my wife just as I like her to make herself attractive for me, *because* of this verse.

Ibn al-'Arabi presses the word `عاشروهن` on its root, which means completeness: `فأمر الله سبحانه الأزواج إذا عقدوا على النساء أن يكون أدمة ما بينهم وصحبتهم على التمام والكمال` [12] — God commanded husbands that what is between them be whole and complete. Then he adds the sentence that is the key to this whole question: `وهذا واجب على الزوج ولا يلزمه ذلك في القضاء` [13] — this is obligatory on the husband, but a court will not compel him to it. The enforceable legal rules are a floor, not a description of what is owed. Almost everything that makes marriage in these books liveable sits above the line a judge can reach.

Ibn Qudama, gathering the same material, does not conceal the asymmetry either: `وحق الزوج عليها أعظم من حقها عليه` [14], the husband's right over her is greater than hers over him, and he grounds it in the "degree" clause.

**The testimony rule is narrower and far more disputed than it is usually reported.** Al-Jassas, on the verse of debt, notes first what the verse actually does: it names one man and two women *as* "two witnesses" — `فأفادنا إثبات هذا الاسم للرجل والمرأتين حتى يعتبر عمومه في جواز شهادتهما مع الرجل في سائر الحقوق إلا ما قام دليله` [15] — so that combination counts wherever "two witnesses" are required, except where a specific proof excludes it. Then he reports that the jurists never settled the scope: `وقد اختلف أهل العلم في شهادة النساء مع الرجال في غير الأموال` [16]. On one side, 'Ali is reported as allowing women's testimony in contracts, and Ibn 'Umar as allowing it in marriage — `عن علي رضي الله عنه قال تجوز شهادة النساء في العقد وروى حجاج عن عطاء أن ابن عمر كان يجيز شهادة النساء مع الرجل في النكاح` [17]. On the other, `وقال مالك لا تجوز شهادة النساء مع الرجال في الحدود والقصاص ولا في الطلاق ولا في النكاح ولا في الأنساب` [18]. That range is the answer to "what does Islam say about a woman's testimony": it says several things, and the disagreement is old and internal, not a modern revision.

And there is a case running the other way that rarely gets mentioned. In *li'an*, where a husband accuses his wife, Ibn Hajar explains why her sworn statements are called "testimonies": `أقيمت مقام أربع شهود في غيره ليقام عليها الحد ومن ثم سميت شهادات` [19] — her oath is put in the place of the four witnesses that would otherwise be needed to punish her. Her word alone defeats the accusation.

**Some of the differentiated rules are small, and disputed too.** Ibn 'Abd al-Barr on what you do if something happens during prayer: for men, say *subhan Allah*, and there is no disagreement. For women, `وأما النساء فإن العلماء اختلفوا في ذلك فذهب مالك وأصحابه إلى أن التسبيح للرجال والنساء جميعا لقوله صلى الله عليه وسلم من نابه شيء في صلاته فليسبح ولم يخص رجالا من نساء` [20] — Malik and his companions hold the same rule for both, because the hadith did not single out men from women. Others, `وقال آخرون منهم الشافعي والأوزعي وعبيد الله بن الحسن والحسن بن حي وجماعة من نابه من الرجال شيء في صلاته سبح ومن نابها من النساء شيء في صلاتها صفقت إن شاءت` [21], hold that she may clap if she wishes. A differentiated rule, and the jurists split on whether it is differentiated at all.

**So, honestly summarised.** Three separate things get run together in this question, and separating them is most of the work:

1. **Standing before God** — not differentiated, and the Qur'an says so in matched pairs.
2. **The value and protection of the person** — not differentiated; retaliation runs both ways, life for life and wound for wound.
3. **Roles within the household, and certain procedures of evidence** — differentiated. The sources give two reasons: an ordering claim about men, stated in Ibn Kathir in the frank terms quoted above, and a financial one, the unreciprocated obligation of dowry and maintenance. And the differentiated rules mark the enforceable minimum, not the relationship the same books actually require.

**What this does not settle.** Every work here is premodern and answers premodern questions. Nothing in the corpus addresses what *qiwama* means when a wife earns her own living or earns more, or women in employment, the judiciary or public office beyond the two offices Ibn Kathir names, or how inheritance shares work in a wage economy rather than one where a man's kin were liable for his blood-money. A reader who wants those answered needs a living scholar, not this corpus. And on the contested items above, "what Islam says" is a range of positions, which is the honest report even when a single answer would be more comfortable.
