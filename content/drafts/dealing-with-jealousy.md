# How do I deal with envy?

## Answer

First, work out which of two things you actually have — because they are not the same, and one of them is not blameworthy at all.

**The distinction.**

Al-Fatawa al-Hindiyya: "the blameworthy [envy] is that he sees a blessing upon another and wishes for the removal of that blessing from that other and its being his own. But if he wishes it for himself [without that], it is not called *hasad*; rather it is called *ghibta*. And Shaykh al-Islam used to say: if he wishes for that very blessing for himself, it is forbidden and blameworthy; but if he wishes the like of it for himself, there is no harm in it" [1].

Al-Sarakhsi sharpens it further: "*hasad* is that the envier wishes the blessing of the envied to depart from him, **and exerts himself for that**, and believes that this blessing is in the wrong place. And the meaning of *ghibta* is that he wishes the like of it for himself, without exerting himself and without wishing its departure from the other" [2].

Three tests, then. Do you want him to *lose* it? Do you find yourself *working towards* that — a word here, an obstacle there? Do you think he *doesn't deserve* it? If all three are no, and what you feel is "I want that too" — that is *ghibta*, and Ibn Kathir calls it "the praiseworthy envy in the Law: wishing for a state like that of one who is in a happy state" [3].

**How serious the real thing is.**

Ibn Kathir: it "is wishing for the removal of the blessing from the envied — whether it comes to the envier or not — and this is blameworthy in the Law, destructive; and it was the first of Iblis's sins, when he envied Adam, peace be upon him, for the honour, respect and esteem Allah bestowed on him" [4].

Ibn Taymiyya traces its root to *shuhh*, grasping miserliness, and analyses what it contains: "and it produces envy, which is disliking what another has been singled out with — and in envy there is miserliness and injustice, for it is miserliness with what was given to another, and injustice against him by seeking its removal from him" [5].

That is worth pausing on. Envy is stingy with something that was never yours to be stingy with.

Ibn Hajar also closes an escape route: the verse "and from the evil of the envier when he envieth" indicates "that the prohibition of mutual envy is not confined to its occurring between two or more; rather envy is blameworthy and forbidden even if it occurs from one side — because if it is blamed when it occurs with reciprocity, it is blamed in the singular case with greater reason" [6].

**But the thought itself is not the sin.**

Ibn Hajar, exactly parallel to what the commentators say about anger: "its possessor is blameworthy **when he acts by what it requires**, whether by a settled resolve, or a word, or a deed. And whoever has that occur to him should dislike it, as he dislikes what has been placed in his nature of love for forbidden things" [7].

So: the pang when a friend announces the job, the house, the child — that arrives uninvited, and it is not what you are accountable for. You are accountable for what you do with it. Dislike it, do not feed it, do not act on it, and say nothing.

**The one blessing you may wish removed.**

Ibn Hajar records the exception: "they excepted from that the case where the blessing belongs to a disbeliever or an open sinner who uses it to help him in disobedience to Allah" [8]. Narrow, and about the *use*, not the person.

**The hadith people quote, and what it actually says.**

"There is no envy except in two: a man to whom Allah gave wealth and He set him over its destruction in the truth, and a man to whom Allah gave wisdom, so he judges by it and teaches it" [9].

Ibn Hajar: "as for the envy mentioned in the hadith, it is *ghibta*, and envy was applied to it metaphorically — and it is that he wishes to have the like of what another has, without its departing from him" [10]. Ibn al-Munir: "what is meant by envy here is *ghibta*, and what is meant by the negation is not its literal sense — otherwise it would entail a falsehood, since people have envied in other than these two traits… rather what is meant is the ruling, and its meaning is confining the highest rank of *ghibta* to these two traits" [11].

Ibn Hajar also sorts the wider category: "the eagerness for this is called *munafasa*; if it is in obedience it is praiseworthy — and of it is *so let the competitors compete*; if it is in disobedience it is blameworthy — and of it is *do not compete*; and if it is in permissible things, it is allowed" [12].

**The cure, which is also a promotion.**

The version in Ibn Kathir shows the move: "There is no competing between you except in two: a man to whom Allah gave the Qur'an, so he stands with it through the hours of the night and the day and follows what is in it — and a man says: *If Allah had given me the like of what He gave so-and-so, I would stand with it as he stands.* And a man to whom Allah gave wealth, and he spends it and gives charity — and a man says: *If Allah had given me the like of what He gave so-and-so, I would give charity with it*" [13].

And the payoff, in the hadith of Abu Kabsha: "a servant whom Allah has provided with knowledge and not provided with wealth, and he is truthful in intention, saying: *if I had wealth I would do as so-and-so does* — **the reward of the two of them is equal**" [14].

That is the actual technique. Envy says *he should not have it.* Aspiration says *I want to be that.* The second earns you the reward of the thing you cannot afford to do — for the price of meaning it sincerely.

**Two supplications.**

'Abd al-Rahman b. 'Awf "was seen circling the House saying: *My Lord, protect me from the greed of my soul; my Lord, protect me from the greed of my soul.* He was asked about that, and he said: When I am protected from the greed of my soul, I am protected from miserliness, injustice and the severing of ties" [15].

And the Qur'an's praise of the Ansar, as Ibn Taymiyya reads it: "*they find no need in their breasts for what they have been given*" — "that is, they find no envy for what their brothers among the Muhajirun were given, and they prefer them over themselves even though poverty is theirs. Then He said: *and whoever is protected from the greed of his soul, those are the successful*" [16].

**In practice.** When it comes: name it accurately — is this *I want that too* or *I want him not to have that*? If the first, say *ma sha' Allah* out loud and mean it, and make du'a for him; then ask what step towards the same thing is available to you. If the second, treat it as you would any bad thought: dislike it, refuse it, tell nobody, and ask Allah for the like rather than for its removal. Congratulate the person, genuinely, in words. Doing the outward act tends to drag the heart along after it — and either way you have stopped short of the thing you would actually be answerable for.
