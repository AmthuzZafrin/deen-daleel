# I committed zina — what do I do?

## Answer

**Repent to Allah, privately, and tell no one** [3][4][5]. That is not a
softening of the answer — it is what the sources actually instruct, and one of
them says so in as many words. You do not confess this to a scholar, to a mosque, to a court, or to
your family.

**Start with the verse.** **"Say: O My slaves who have been prodigal to their own
hurt! Despair not of the mercy of Allah, Who forgiveth all sins"** [1]. Not most
sins. And **"Know they not that Allah is He Who accepteth repentance from His
bondmen"** [2]. Whatever else is true, that is the frame the rest of this sits
in.

**Now the specific instruction, which people are often not told.** Ibn Taymiyya,
asked precisely this question, answered: **"إذا تاب توبة صحيحة تاب الله عليه من
غيرحاجة إلى أن يقر بذنبه حتى يقام عليه الحد"** — *if he repents a sound
repentance, Allah accepts his repentance, without any need for him to confess his
sin so that the prescribed penalty be carried out on him* [3]. He cites the
hadith: **"من ابتلى بشيء من هذه القاذورات فليستتر بستر الله فانه من يبد لنا
صفحته نقم عليه كتاب الله"** — *whoever is afflicted with any of these
defilements, let him conceal himself with Allah's concealment; for whoever shows
us his face, we apply the Book of Allah to him* [4] — and the report
**"من أذنب سرا فليتب سرا ومن أذنب علانية فليتب علانية"** — *whoever sins in
private, let him repent in private; whoever sins openly, let him repent openly*
[5].

Read that carefully. The instruction is to **cover it**, and the penalty in the
fiqh books attaches to a case brought before a court, not to a person who has
turned back to Allah on their own. Ibn Hajar gives the reasoning:
**"وإذا تمحض حق الله فهو أكرم الأكرمين ورحمته سبقت غضبه فلذلك إذا ستره في الدنيا
لم يفضحه في الآخرة"** — *when it is purely Allah's right, He is the most generous
of the generous and His mercy precedes His wrath; so when He has covered a person
in this world, He does not disgrace them in the next* [6].

**Do not tell people.** Ibn Taymiyya quotes: **"كل أمتى معافى إلا المجاهرين وان
من المجاهرة أن يبيت الرجل على الذنب قد ستره الله فيصبح يتحدث به"** — *all of my
community are pardoned except those who make it public; and part of making it
public is that a man passes the night having done something Allah covered for
him, and wakes up talking about it* [7]. He gives the reason:
**"فما دام الذنب مستورا فعقوبته على صاحبه خاصة وإذا ظهر ولم ينكر كان ضرره
عاما"** — *while the sin stays covered its harm falls on the one who did it
alone; once it becomes public and goes unchallenged, its harm becomes general*
[8].

**There is one exception, and it is the useful one.** Ibn Taymiyya distinguishes
who you may speak to: **"فان شكى ذلك إلى طبيب يعرف طب النفوس ليعالج نفسه بعلاج
الايمان فهو بمنزلة المستفتى وهذا حسن وان شكى إلى من يعينه على المحرم فهذا
حرام"** — *if he takes his complaint to a physician who understands the medicine
of souls, so as to treat himself with the treatment of faith, he is in the
position of one asking for a ruling, and this is good; but if he complains to
someone who will help him toward the forbidden, that is forbidden* [9]. So:
someone who will help you stop, yes — and framed as seeking help, not as
confession. Someone who will draw you back into it, no. And that is the whole
list.

**If this happened before you became Muslim, it is already gone.** 'Amr b.
al-'As, before pledging allegiance, tried to make forgiveness a condition, and
was answered: **"أما علمت أن الإسلام يهدم ما كان قبله؟ وأن الهجرة تهدم ما كان
قبلها؟ وأن الحج يهدم ما كان قبله؟"** — *do you not know that Islam demolishes
what came before it, that hijra demolishes what came before it, and that Hajj
demolishes what came before it?* [10]. Muslim gives the chapter that title [11].
Al-Nawawi states the effect plainly: **"فهذا يغفر له ما سلف في الكفر بنص القرآن
العزيز والحديث الصحيح الاسلام يهدم ما قبله وباجماع المسلمين"** — *what preceded
in disbelief is forgiven him, by the text of the Qur'an, the sound hadith "Islam
demolishes what came before it", and the consensus of the Muslims* [12].
Al-Qurtubi even settles the legal point: **"ولو زنى وأسلم أو اغتصب مسلمة ثم أسلم
سقط عنه الحد"** — *if he committed zina and then became Muslim, the penalty falls
away from him* [13]. There is nothing here to carry, and no one to tell.

**On the seriousness, briefly, because it is real.** The Qur'an lists it among
the grave matters: those who **"nor commit adultery - and whoso doeth this shall
pay the penalty"** [14]. Naming it as serious is not the same as saying it cannot
be repented from — the same body of texts does both, and holds the second more
firmly than most people are taught.

**What repentance actually consists of, in practice.** Stop. Regret it. Resolve
not to return. Ask Allah's forgiveness directly — no intermediary is involved,
and none of the texts above suggests otherwise. Then change what made it
possible: the situation, the access, the private setting, whatever it was. If
there is a person still in the picture, that usually means ending it or marrying,
and it is worth deciding which rather than drifting.

**On the difficulty of holding on.** Ibn Taymiyya's advice to someone struggling
with an attachment they cannot act on is to **"فلهذا أمر من يبتلى بالعشق أن يعف
ويكتم ويصبر فيكون حينئذ ممن قال الله فيه ( إنه من يتق ويصبر فان الله لا يضيع
أجر المحسنين )"** — *keep chaste, keep it to himself, and be patient, so that he
becomes one of those of whom Allah said: whoever fears Allah and is patient,
Allah does not waste the reward of those who do good* [15]. On that reading, the
struggle itself is credited, not merely tolerated.

**And on despair, which is the actual danger.** Al-Qurtubi tells the story of the
man who had killed ninety-nine people and asked a worshipper whether repentance
was open to him; told it was not, he killed him too and made it a hundred.
Al-Qurtubi's comment: **"فالتنفير مفسدة للخليفة والتيسير مصلحة لهم"** — *driving
people away is a harm to them, and making it easy is a benefit to them* [16]. If
someone has told you there is no way back, that is the error the story is about.
The door described at [1] does not have conditions attached that this answer has
left out.

**On the language above:** "zina" is sex outside marriage; "tawba" is turning
back to Allah in repentance; "satr" is the covering or concealment these texts
instruct; "hadd" is a court-imposed penalty, which attaches to a proven case
before a judge, not to private repentance [3]; "mujaharah" is making a sin
public.

**A caution, and a limit on this answer.** This is about your relationship with
Allah. If the situation involves someone else's rights — a marriage, a child, a
person who was coerced — those are separate obligations that repentance does not
by itself settle, and they need advice specific to the case. And if you are
carrying this to the point of despair or self-harm, that needs a person, not a
web page: speak to a counsellor, a doctor, or a crisis line today. The sources
above are unanimous that the door is open; getting through it is easier with
someone alongside you.
