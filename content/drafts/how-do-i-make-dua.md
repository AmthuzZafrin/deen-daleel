# How do I make du'a properly?

## Answer

There is a shape to it, and the Prophet ﷺ taught it by correcting someone who
got it wrong — which makes this one of the few etiquette questions with a
demonstration attached rather than a list of rules.

**The correction.** Fadala b. 'Ubayd: "the Messenger of Allah ﷺ heard a man
supplicating in his prayer who had not glorified Allah nor sent blessings on the
Prophet ﷺ, so the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: *you have been hasty, O you who
pray* — then the Messenger of Allah ﷺ taught them. Then the Messenger of Allah ﷺ
heard a man praying who glorified Allah and praised Him and sent blessings on
the Prophet ﷺ, and the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: *supplicate and you will be
answered; ask and you will be given*" [1].

So: praise, then blessings on the Prophet ﷺ, then ask. Going straight to the
request is what he called haste.

**Why the praise is not a formality.** Ibn Taymiyya: "praise contains the object
of the supplication — as in the hadith, *the best remembrance is there is no god
but Allah, and the best supplication is praise be to Allah*. For the
supplicant's praise of the One he supplicates, in terms that imply the granting
of what he seeks, may be more eloquent than mentioning the thing sought" [2].

His two examples are the ones to learn from. Ayyub, in the Qur'an, does not ask
at all — he says *harm has touched me, and You are the most merciful of the
merciful*: "and his saying this is better than his saying *have mercy on me*"
[3]. And 'A'isha's du'a for Laylat al-Qadr has the same shape: "O Allah, You are
Pardoning and love to pardon, so pardon me" [4]. Name the attribute of Allah
that your need falls under, and the request follows from it.

**A sharper observation from the same passage.** "What makes clear the merit of
praise over petition is that legislated praise entails faith in Allah, whereas
supplication may not entail it — since the disbelievers ask Allah and He gives
them, as Allah has reported in the Qur'an in more than one place" [5]. Asking
for provision or health is something anyone does, believer or not. Praise is
what only a believer can offer. That is worth knowing if your du'a has become a
list of requests.

**Praise on its own counts as du'a.** Al-Qurtubi: "glorification, praise and
declaring Allah's oneness may be called supplication" [6], and he cites the
hadith qudsi through Ibn 'Uyayna: "*when My servant is occupied with praising Me
away from asking Me, I give him the best of what I give those who ask*" [7]. The
strongest case is Yunus in the belly of the whale: "the supplication of Dhu'l-Nun
when he called with it in the belly of the fish — *there is no god but You,
glory be to You, I was among the wrongdoers* — no Muslim ever calls with it in
anything but that he is answered" [8]. That du'a contains no request.

And a closing: "it is recommended for the supplicant to say at the end of his
supplication what the people of Paradise said: *and the last of their call is,
praise be to Allah, Lord of the Worlds*" [9].

**The bodily manner.** A hadith of al-Muttalib describes it: "the prayer is two
by two, you make the tashahhud every two rak'ahs, and you show neediness, and
you show humility, and you spread out your hands and say: O Allah, O Allah —
whoever does not do that, it is deficient" [10]. Three words carry the manner:
*tab'as*, *tamaskan*, *tuqni' yadayk* — be needy, be lowly, hold your hands open.
The narrator asked what *iqna'* meant and the transmitter simply spread his hands
as though supplicating.

**Wiping the face afterwards — a practice with a weak chain, honestly reported.**
"When the Prophet ﷺ supplicated and raised his hands, he would wipe his face
with them" [11]; and 'Umar's report, "when the Messenger of Allah ﷺ raised his
hands in supplication he would not lower them until he had wiped his face with
them" [12]. Al-Tirmidhi grades his own narration: "this is a *gharib* hadith; we
do not know it except from the hadith of Hammad b. 'Isa, and he was alone in
narrating it, and he has few hadiths" [13]. So the practice has a basis, and it
is a thin one; do it or leave it without pressing anyone either way.

**Two final notes.** On what to ask for, al-Qushayri offers a distinction some
find useful: "what has in it a share for Allah or for the Muslims — supplication
for that is better; and what has in it a portion for oneself — silence about
that is better" [14]. It is a counsel of high spirituality, not a rule; the
Prophet ﷺ himself asked for ordinary things.

And on the answer: "every supplicant is answered, but the response varies —
sometimes it comes as the very thing he asked for, and sometimes as its
substitute" [15]. Which is a different question, with a longer answer of its own.
