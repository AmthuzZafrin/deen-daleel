# Is music haram?

> Reviewer note: Contested, and retrieval leans prohibitive because that is what the corpus holds. The answer must present the range, including the permissive position, and must not present the retrieved side as settled.


## Answer

This is one of the genuinely contested questions in Islamic law, and any answer that presents it as settled is hiding something from you. The corpus in front of us contains scholars who forbid it, scholars who permit it, and — most usefully — scholars on the prohibitive side who say plainly that the matter is disputed.

So rather than give you a ruling, here is the map: what is agreed forbidden, what is agreed permitted, and where the disagreement actually sits.

**Start with the concession, because it comes from the strictest quarter.**

Ibn Taymiyya, listing matters Muslims dispute, includes "listening to singing and instruments and the like — matters in which people hold two positions, prohibition and permission" [1]. He is not neutral about it; a line later he says "the majority of Muslims hold that they are forbidden, and some of them permitted them, and no one said they are an act of drawing near" [2]. But note what he has conceded while arguing: two positions exist, held by scholars, and his own objection is aimed at something narrower — at people who treat listening as a *devotion*, a way of drawing near to God.

Ibn Hajar reports the same standoff on instruments specifically: "some people have reported consensus on their prohibition, and some have reported the opposite" [3]. When consensus is claimed in both directions, there is no consensus.

**What is agreed forbidden.**

There is no real dispute about this part. Al-Qurtubi defines the target: "the singing customary among those known for it, which stirs the souls and rouses them to desire and flirtation... this kind, when it is in poetry that dwells on mentioning women and describing their beauty, and mentions wines and forbidden things — there is no disagreement about its prohibition" [4]. Ibn Hajar says the same in a sentence: poetry containing "description of the beauty of women, and wine, and other forbidden matters — there is no disagreement about its prohibition" [5].

Read that carefully, because it is doing more work than it appears to. The prohibition is being attached to **content and effect**, not to sound. Both authors are describing lyrics.

**What is agreed permitted.**

Also not really in dispute. Al-Nawawi: "the Arabs call recitation *ghina'*, and it is not the singing that is disputed; rather it is permitted. The Companions permitted the singing of the Arabs, which is mere recitation and humming, and they permitted the caravan chant, and did it in the presence of the Prophet ﷺ. In all of this is the permission of the like of it and what is in its meaning — and this and its like is not forbidden" [6].

Al-Qurtubi allows more: "as for what is free of that, a little of it is permitted at times of joy, such as the wedding and the 'Id, and when encouraging hard work — as happened at the digging of the trench, and the chanting of Anjasha and Salama b. al-Akwa'" [7]. Three permitted settings, one of them simply *work*.

The *duff* at a wedding or 'Id is agreed. Al-Nawawi: "beating the Arabs' duff is permitted on a day of open joy — that is, the 'Id and the wedding" [8]. Al-Muhallab, on the Ansari women singing at a wedding: "in this hadith is the announcing of the marriage with the duff and with permitted singing; and in it is the leader's attending a wedding even though there is amusement in it, so long as it does not exceed the bound of the permitted" [9].

**Now the evidence, and its weak points — on both sides.**

The prohibitive reports. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr collects them: "'Abdullah b. Mas'ud said: singing grows hypocrisy in the heart"; and al-Qasim b. Muhammad, asked about singing, "said: it is falsehood — [the questioner] said: I know it is falsehood, but what do you hold about it? Al-Qasim said: tell me, falsehood — where is it? He said: in the Fire. He said: then that is it" [10].

Then Ibn 'Abd al-Barr does something a partisan would not do. Having gathered the prohibitive material, he writes: "this is what has come regarding the dislike of singing; and there has come **what is firmer than this from the standpoint of chain** in the specific matter of the licence for it at the 'Ids and at weddings in particular" [11] — and he cites 'A'isha: "Abu Bakr entered upon her and with her were two girls singing on the day of 'Id, or during the days of Mina, and beating the duff, and the Messenger of Allah ﷺ was listening to that and not forbidding them. Abu Bakr rebuked them, and the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: *leave them, Abu Bakr, for these are days of 'Id*" [12].

That is a scholar of hadith telling you which side of this file has the stronger chains.

He also names who held the licence: "the licence has been narrated regarding the melodies the Arabs know, and raising the voice with them — as against the disliked melodies of the non-Arabs — from a group of the scholars of the *salaf*, whom we would lengthen the book by naming; and Sa'id b. al-Musayyab and Muhammad b. Sirin suffice you among them" [13].

The verse. The proof-text produced most often is the one about the man who buys *lahw al-hadith* — idle talk — read as meaning music. Ibn Hajar, commenting on how the chapter is arranged, notes that its compiler "seems to have hinted at the weakness of what has come in interpreting *lahw* in this verse as singing" — and then goes through the material: the hadith that selling singing-girls is unlawful, "its chain is weak"; the report from Ibn Mas'ud interpreting the verse as singing, "in its chain is weakness also" [14].

So the most-quoted verse rests, in Ibn Hajar's assessment, on two weak chains. That does not make music permitted. It does mean the verse should not be produced as though it closed the question.

The claimed consensus. Al-Qurtubi transmits it: "al-Tabari said: the scholars of the regions have agreed on the dislike of singing and the prevention of it; and none departed from the body except Ibrahim b. Sa'd and 'Ubaydullah al-'Anbari" [15]. Note two things. The consensus as stated is on *karaha* — dislike — as much as on prohibition. And it names its own exceptions, which is what a consensus claim does when it is not quite a consensus.

**The one clear principle underneath it all.**

Ibn Hajar, reading the chapter heading: "the implication of the heading is that if the amusement does not distract him from obeying God it is not falsehood — but the generality of this implication is restricted by the explicit texts, so everything whose prohibition is stated among the things that divert is falsehood, whether it distracted or did not distract" [16].

That is the whole disagreement in one sentence. Everyone agrees that what distracts from God is *batil*. The dispute is whether there are texts specific enough to forbid this particular thing on its own, regardless of distraction — and that is a dispute about the strength of chains, which is exactly where Ibn 'Abd al-Barr and Ibn Hajar both found the prohibitive material wanting, and where Ibn Taymiyya and al-Qurtubi did not.

The Qur'an's own framing is about the life you build, not about a category of sound: *"Know that the life of the world is only play, and idle talk, and pageantry, and boasting among you, and rivalry in respect of wealth and children"* [17].

**What to actually do.**

Nobody can hand you a consensus here, because there isn't one. But the disagreement has a shape, and you can act inside it honestly:

- **Content is not disputed.** Lyrics about sex, drinking, contempt for religion, or degradation of people fall under what al-Qurtubi and Ibn Hajar both say has no disagreement. That covers a great deal of what is on a modern playlist, and it is the part of this question you can settle today without waiting for the scholars to agree.
- **The *duff* at a wedding or 'Id is agreed permitted**, as is *nasheed*, chant, and unaccompanied recitation — al-Nawawi puts these outside the dispute altogether.
- **Instruments beyond the *duff* are the actual disputed ground.** The majority of scholars prohibit them. A real minority — including Ibrahim b. Sa'd and 'Ubaydullah al-'Anbari, with Sa'id b. al-Musayyab and Ibn Sirin named for the licence on melody — did not. If you follow a school, follow it. If you are asking because you want to know whether you have done something terrible by listening to an instrumental track, the honest answer is that scholars of the first rank disagreed about it.
- **The test everyone shares** is Ibn Hajar's: what is it doing to you? What crowds out prayer, or fills the hours, or trains the heart on things it should not want, is condemned by every party to this dispute — and by that measure a phone can be worse than a lute.

Ibn Battal's line about poetry generalises well to the whole subject: "what contains in poetry and rajaz the remembrance of God and His exaltation and His oneness, and preferring obedience to Him and submission to Him, is good and encouraged... and what is lying and obscenity is blameworthy" [18].
