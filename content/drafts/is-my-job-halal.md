# Is my job halal?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

> Reviewer note: Cannot be answered generically. Explain what it turns on; refer.

## Answer

**Start from the right default: work is good, and lawful earning is treated in these books as something to be admired rather than tolerated. The question is not whether your job clears a suspicion, but whether any of three specific things is true of it.**

**The baseline.** Al-Nawawi reports the standard classification: `قال الماوردي أصول المكاسب الزارعة والتجارة والصنعة وأيها أطيب فيه ثلاثة مذاهب للناس (أشبهها) بمذهب الشافعي ان التجارة أطيب قال والأشبه عندي أن الزراعة أطيب لأنها أقرب إلى التوكل` [1] — the roots of earning are **cultivation, trade, and craft**; they differed over which is best, some preferring trade, al-Mawardi preferring farming. Ibn Hajar records the same discussion: `وقد اختلف العلماء في أفضل المكاسب قال الماوردي أصول المكاسب الزراعة والتجارة والصنعة والأشبه بمذهب الشافعي أن أطيبها التجارة` [2].

Notice what the disagreement is about. Not whether working is respectable, but **which honest livelihood is most excellent.** Al-Nawawi settles it on a text: `ما أكل أحد طعاما قط خيرا من أن يأكل من عمل يده وأن نبي الله داود صلى الله عليه وسلم كان يأكل من عمل يده` [3] — no one has ever eaten food better than what he eats from the work of his own hand, and the prophet Dawud ate from the work of his hand. `فالصواب ما نص عليه رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم وهو عمل اليد` [4] — the correct view is the one the text states: work of the hand.

And ordinary productive work is itself counted as charity: `ما من مسلم يغرس غرسا إلا كان ما أكل منه له صدقة وما سرق منه له صدقة` [5] — no Muslim plants a planting but that what is eaten from it is charity for him, and even what is stolen from it is charity for him.

That is the register. A job is not under suspicion because it is a job.

**The three questions that actually decide it.**

**1. Is the thing itself forbidden?** Are you producing, selling, or providing something the law prohibits — alcohol, pork, gambling, interest-bearing loans, deception? This is the direct case, and it is the only one where the answer is usually clear-cut. The specific common cases have their own pages here: banking, alcohol, retail with mixed stock.

**2. Are you assisting in something forbidden?** Ibn Taymiyya divides cooperation into two, and names the wrong side plainly: `والثانى تعاون على الاثم والعدوان كالاعانة على دم معصوم أو أخذ مال معصوم أو ضرب من لا يستحق الضرب ونحو ذلك فهذا الذي حرمه الله ورسوله` [6] — the second is cooperation in sin and transgression: assisting in shedding protected blood, or taking protected property, or striking one who does not deserve it, and the like — **this is what God and His Messenger forbade.**

The examples are worth noticing: they are all cases where your act materially contributes to a specific wrong done to a specific party. That is a narrower test than "my employer does some things I disapprove of," and it is the test the books actually apply.

**3. Is the work itself honest?** Not everything unlawful in a job is about the product. Al-Nawawi, discussing the market inspector's duties, includes preventing sellers colluding to fix prices and equally `وكذلك يمنع والى الحسبة المشترين من الاشتراك في شئ لا يشتريه غيرهم، لما في ذلك من ظلم البائع` [7] — the inspector also prevents **buyers** from combining so that no one else buys, because of the wrong done to the seller.

That is a reminder that the law's concern runs to conduct, not only to commodities. A job selling entirely lawful goods can still involve misrepresentation, exploiting people's ignorance, or squeezing a counterparty unfairly — and those are addressed on the page about honest trade.

**What these books do not settle.**

They have no concept of employment by a large organisation with many activities, no framework for shareholding employers, and nothing about a person whose individual contribution is far removed from anything objectionable. Where your work is peripheral to a problematic activity rather than constitutive of it, the classical materials give you a principle — the cooperation test above — and not a verdict.

**How to use this.**

Write down what you actually do, task by task, and run the three questions:

- **Is the object of my work itself forbidden?** If yes, that is the answer, and the question becomes how to change it.
- **Does my specific act materially bring about a forbidden thing?** Drafting the contract is different from cleaning the office. Being paid to pour the drink is different from being paid to fix the plumbing.
- **Is what I do honest?** Would the other party still deal with me if they knew everything I know?

If all three come back clean, your job is lawful and you do not need to keep re-examining it. If one of them is genuinely in doubt, take the specific task — not the job title, and not a general worry — to a scholar you can speak with.

And do not let scrupulosity do the work of piety here. The tradition's attitude to earning a living is the one in the reports above: a man eating from his own labour is doing something excellent, and a person who plants something is being credited with charity while he sleeps.
