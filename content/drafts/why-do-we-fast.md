# Why do Muslims fast?

## Answer

**Because God commanded it — and the sources give three reasons for the command
that are worth knowing, none of which is "to feel what the poor feel".**

**The obligation first.**

Ibn Qudama: **fasting Ramadan is obligatory, and the basis of its obligation is
the Book, the Sunna, and consensus** [1] — and he closes: **the Muslims are agreed
on the obligation of fasting the month of Ramadan** [2].

The hadith he cites is the one that sets its place among the duties. A man came
asking: **tell me what God has made obligatory on me of fasting — he said: the
month of Ramadan** [3], and nothing beyond it except voluntarily.

Ibn Qudama also gives the legal definition, which is drier than the English word
suggests: **fasting in the law is an expression for abstaining from specific
things, at a specific time** [4]. It is not self-denial in general; it is a defined
act of worship with a start, an end, and a list.

**Why, according to the jurists.**

Al-Kasani gives three purposes, drawn from the wording of the fasting verses
themselves.

**One — gratitude.** Food, drink and health are among the greatest blessings, and
you do not notice them while you have them. Withholding them for a measured time
makes their worth visible, **and that carries him to discharge their due by
thanks; and thanking for blessings is obligatory both rationally and by the law —
and to this the Lord pointed in His words in the verse of fasting, "that you may
give thanks"** [5].

**Two — taqwa.** This is the reason the Qur'an gives at the end of the fasting
verse, and al-Kasani unpacks the mechanism: **it is a means to God-consciousness,
because if his soul submits to abstaining from the lawful, seeking God's pleasure
and fearing His painful punishment, then it is more fit that it should submit to
abstaining from the forbidden** [6].

That is the practical logic of the whole month. You spend thirty days proving to
yourself that you can decline something permitted — water, in daylight, when
nobody would know — and the capacity you build transfers.

**Three — mastering appetite.** **In fasting is the subduing of the nature and the
breaking of desire, because the soul when it is full craves desires, and when it
is hungry it holds back from what it wants** [7]. Al-Kasani connects this to the
Prophet's advice to the young man who could not marry: fast, for it is a shield.

**What is striking about that list** is that empathy with the poor is not on it.
It is a real and often-mentioned benefit, and giving in Ramadan is strongly
encouraged — but the jurists' account of *why* is about your relationship with
your own appetites and with God. The month is training, not a simulation.

**If this is your first Ramadan**, see the separate answer on how to start. The
short version: it is easier than it looks from outside, and everyone around you is
doing it at the same time, which is most of the point of it being a month rather
than a private discipline.
