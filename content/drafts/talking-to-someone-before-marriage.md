# Can I talk to someone I'm considering marrying?

## Answer

**Short answer: yes — talking is not what the sources prohibit.** What they
prohibit is being alone together, looking with desire, and a private
understanding kept out of sight. Conversation with a serious purpose, conducted
openly, is not only allowed; the proposal itself is defined as an act of speech.

**Speech is what a proposal is made of.** Al-Qurtubi defines *khitba* — the
proposal — as **"الخطبة ( بكسر الخاء ) : فعل الخاطب من كلام وقصد واستلطاف بفعل
أو قول"** — *the act of the one proposing: speech, intent, and courteous
approach by deed or word* [1]. Speech is in the definition. He also records, as
an instance of it, the Prophet ﷺ speaking to Umm Salama while she was widowed —
mentioning his standing among his people — and comments **"كانت تلك خطبة"**
— *that was a proposal* [2]. So the classical picture is not silence until the
contract.

**The distinction the fiqh actually draws is between hinting and a secret
promise.** Commenting on the verse that permits a man to hint at his interest in
a woman still in her waiting period, Ibn Hajar sets out how the scholars read
it: **"فمنع التصريح وأجيز التعريض مع أن
المقصود مفهوم منهما"** — *explicit statement was forbidden and hinting was
permitted, even though the meaning is understood from both* [3]; and of a private
undertaking he says **"ولا شك أن المواعدة على ذلك تزيد على التعريض المأذون
فيه"** — *there is no doubt that making a pact about it goes beyond the hinting
that was permitted* [4]. That is a case about the 'idda specifically, but the
shape of the reasoning is the point: what the texts object to in that case is a
private mutual promise, not conversation as such.

**And there is a purpose the talking is meant to serve.** Al-Nawawi explains why
looking is recommended in terms of *judging* the person — **"ولأنه يستدل بالوجه
على الجمال أو ضده وبالكفين على خصوبة البدن أو عدمها"** — *because the face
indicates beauty or its opposite, and the hands the body's condition* [5] — and
holds that mentioning a prospective spouse's shortcoming is legitimate when the
purpose is honest counsel: **"وفي هذا دلالة لجواز ذكر مثل هذه للنصيحة"** — *this
indicates the permissibility of mentioning such a thing for the sake of sincere
advice* [6]. On that reasoning the process is an assessment, and an assessment
that could not include talking would not be much of one. Ibn Qudama likewise
permits more than a glance, on the ground that a single look does not achieve
the purpose: **"وله أن يردد النظر إليها، ويتأمل محاسنها ; لأن المقصود لا يحصل
إلا بذلك"** [7].

**Now the limits, which are firm.** Ibn Qudama attaches one directly to the
permission to look: **"ولا يجوز له الخلوة بها ; لأنها محرمة ولم يرد الشرع بغير
النظر"** — *he may not be alone with her, since she is [still] forbidden to him
and the Sharia permitted nothing beyond the looking* [8]. And on the manner:
**"ولا ينظر إليها نظر تلذذ وشهوة، ولا لريبة"** — *he is not to look at her with
enjoyment and desire, nor with suspect intent* [9]. Al-Nawawi calls the
seclusion rule a point of agreement: **"وأما إذا خلا الأجنبي بالأجنبية من غير
ثالث معهما فهو حرام باتفاق العلماء"** [10], and heads a chapter of his Muslim
commentary **"باب تحريم الخلوة بالأجنبية والدخول عليها"** [11]. The hadith is in
Sahih Muslim from Ibn 'Abbas: **"لا يخلون رجل بامرأة إلا ومعها ذو محرم"** [12],
and Al-Hidaya carries the wording that gives the reason: **"ألا لا يخلون رجل
بامرأة ليس منها بسبيل فإن ثالثهما الشيطان"** — *the third of them is Shaytan*
[13].

**Two details from these texts bear directly on how people do this now.** First,
a token chaperone does not count. Al-Nawawi: **"وكذا لو كان معهما من لا يستحى
منه لصغره كابن سنتين وثلاث ونحو ذلك فإن وجوده كالعدم"** — *if the one with them
is someone before whom no reserve is felt, on account of his youth — a child of
two or three or so — his presence is as though he were absent* [14]. Al-Nawawi
makes the same requirement in *Al-Majmu'*: **"واعلم أن المحرم الذي يجوز القعود
مع الأجنبية مع وجوده يشترط أن يكون ممن يستحي منه فإن كان صغيرا"** [15]. Second,
the prohibition does not lift because the setting looks respectable: **"ولا فرق
في تحريم الخلوة حيث حرمناها بين الخلوة في صلاة أو غيرها"** — *there is no
difference, where we have prohibited seclusion, between seclusion in prayer and
otherwise* [16]. Ibn Hajar records the report **"لا تدخلوا على المغيبات فان
الشيطان يجرى من ابن آدم مجرى الدم"** — *do not enter upon women whose husbands
are away, for Shaytan runs in the son of Adam as blood runs* [17], where the
stated reason is what the situation does to a person, not what either of them
intends.

**The framing the Qur'an gives it.** 4:24 describes the lawful pursuit of a wife
as one **"so that ye seek them with your wealth in honest wedlock, not
debauchery"** [18], and 4:25 requires that the women married be **"they being
honest, not debauched nor of loose conduct"** [19]. On the Pickthall rendering,
the verses set marriage — open, with a dower, with her family's permission —
against two other arrangements, and the second of those is the informal
liaison.

**One more thing worth knowing.** Ibn Hajar explains the prohibition on
proposing over another man's proposal: **"حتى ينكح) أي حتى يتزوج الخاطب الأول
فيحصل اليأس المحض وقوله أو يترك أي الخاطب الأول التزويج فيجوز حينئذ للثاني
الخطبة"** — *until the first suitor marries, at which point hope is entirely
gone, or he abandons the marriage, at which point the second may propose* [20].
If someone is already in a process with another person, that is a reason to
step back.

**So, in practice.** Talk — about religion, character, family, expectations,
money, children, where you will live, everything you need to decide with. Do it
where a third adult is present or the conversation is visible: in a family
setting, with a relative or friend in the room, in a public place — bearing in
mind that the third person has to be someone before whom reserve is actually
felt [14][15]. Keep the purpose stated rather than implied, since what these
texts object to is the private undertaking rather than the speech [4]. Avoid the
register that turns it into courtship for its own sake — the flirtation, the
late-night private messaging, the emotional dependence — which is what the
prohibition on looking with desire is pointed at [9]. And set a timeframe: this
is meant to reach a decision.

Al-Nawawi's advice about looking before the proposal applies to the talking too,
for the same reason he gives — **"يستحب أن يكون نظره إليها قبل الخطبة حتى أن
كرهها تركها من غير ايذاء"** — *so that if he is not taken with her he may
withdraw without causing hurt* [21]. Deciding early costs less than deciding
late.

**On the language above:** "khitba" is the formal proposal, distinct from
"khutba", a sermon. "Khalwa" is the seclusion these texts prohibit [8][10]. "'Idda" is
the waiting period after a husband's death or a divorce. "Ta'rid" is hinting;
"tasrih" is stating outright. A "mahram" is a relative one may never marry.

**A caution about the modern form of the question.** None of these works
addresses text messages, phone calls or video calls, and scholars differ over
whether a written or recorded exchange is "seclusion" at all, since the physical
element the hadiths describe is absent while the emotional one is not. That
disagreement is real, and this answer does not settle it. If your situation
turns on that — a long-distance introduction, an online match, a family that is
not available to supervise — ask a scholar or imam who can take your
circumstances into account.
