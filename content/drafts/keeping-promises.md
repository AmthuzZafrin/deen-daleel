# How serious is breaking a promise?

## Answer

Serious enough that the Prophet ﷺ listed it among the marks of a hypocrite — and yet most jurists held that fulfilling a promise is *recommended*, not legally enforceable. Holding those two facts together is the whole answer, and the resolution turns on what was in your heart when you made the promise.

**The hadiths.**

Abu Hurayra: "The sign of the hypocrite is three: when he speaks he lies, when he is entrusted he betrays, and when he promises he breaks it" [1]. And a wording that closes the escape route: "the sign of the hypocrite is three — even if he fasts and prays and claims that he is a Muslim" [2].

'Abdullah b. 'Amr's version is longer and graded: "Four, whoever has them in him is a pure hypocrite; and whoever has one trait of them has in him a trait of hypocrisy until he leaves it: when he speaks he lies, when he makes a pact he betrays, when he promises he breaks it, and when he disputes he is foul" [3].

**What "hypocrisy" means here.**

Not disbelief. Al-Nawawi, quoted by Ibn Hajar: "what the verifying scholars said is that its meaning is that these are traits of hypocrisy, and the one who has them resembles the hypocrites in these traits and is formed by their character" [4]. Ibn Taymiyya names the category: "as for the lesser hypocrisy, it is hypocrisy in deeds and the like — such as that he lies when he speaks, breaks his promise, betrays when entrusted, or is foul when he disputes" [5].

So a Muslim who breaks a promise has not left Islam. He has taken on a hypocrite's habit, which is bad enough to be worth naming.

**The qualification that decides most real cases.**

Ibn Hajar, citing al-Ghazali: "breaking a promise does not impugn a person unless the resolve upon it was concurrent with the promise. But if he was resolved [to keep it] and then an obstacle arose, or a new view occurred to him — then the form of hypocrisy is not found in him. Al-Ghazali said this in the *Ihya*" [6].

And the hadith of Zayd b. Arqam supports it: "When a man promises his brother and his intention is to fulfil it for him, and then he does not fulfil it, there is no sin upon him" [7].

Read that carefully, because it is the difference between a moral failing and an accident. **The sign of hypocrisy is promising while already intending not to deliver.** Meaning it at the time and then being defeated by circumstances is a different thing, and the hadith says plainly: no sin.

This does not license carelessness. A person who repeatedly means it and repeatedly fails has a problem with promising too readily, and the fix is to promise less. But the person lying awake because they said they would help a friend move and then got called into work has not been convicted of hypocrisy.

**Is fulfilling a promise legally obligatory?**

Here the jurists genuinely differ, and Ibn Hajar handles the question with unusual candour.

Al-Muhallab claimed agreement the other way: "fulfilling a promise is commanded and recommended in everyone's view, and is not obligatory — because of their agreement that the one promised does not rank alongside creditors for what he was promised" [8]. The argument is procedural: a promise does not create a debt a court will enforce, therefore it is not *fard*.

Ibn Hajar rejects the claim of consensus: "the transmission of consensus on that is rejected, for the disagreement is well known, though those who hold it are few. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr and Ibn al-'Arabi said: the most eminent of those who held it is 'Umar b. 'Abd al-'Aziz" [9]. And a middle position among the Malikis, which is the one most relevant to ordinary life: "if the promise is tied to a cause, fulfilling it is obligatory, and otherwise not — so if one says to another, *marry, and you shall have such-and-such*, and he marries on that basis, fulfilling it is obligatory" [10].

That is the principle for a promise someone has acted on. If they turned down another job, spent money, or made arrangements because of what you said, you are no longer in the territory of the optional.

Then Ibn Hajar records his own father's objection, and it is the sharpest thing on the page: "the indication of obligation from them is strong — so how did they carry it onto mere dislike, given the severe threat? And let it be considered whether it can be said: *breaking it is forbidden while fulfilling it is not obligatory* — that is, he sins by breaking it, even if he is not compelled to fulfil it" [11].

That distinction is worth holding onto. A court will not force you to deliver. That is a fact about courts, not about whether you have wronged the person. You can be entirely within your legal rights and still be blameworthy before Allah — and this is one of the clearest places where those two come apart.

**What the tradition actually thinks of promise-keeping.**

Al-Qurtubi: "truthfulness of promise is praiseworthy, and it is among the character of the prophets and messengers; and its opposite, breaking, is blameworthy, and that is among the traits of the corrupt and the hypocrites" [12]. He cites the sayings in circulation: "*a promise is a debt*, and in the report: *the word of the believer is binding*" [13].

The Qur'an singles out Isma'il for it, and al-Qurtubi's verdict on why is simple: "it has been said that Isma'il never promised anything but that he fulfilled it — and this is a sound position, and it is what the apparent sense of the verse requires" [14].

And there is a story about the Prophet ﷺ from before his prophethood that carries this better than any argument. He had agreed to meet a man at a certain place; the man forgot and remembered only three days later, and came to find him still there. "Young man, you have put me to hardship — I have been here three days waiting for you" [15]. Three days. And no rebuke beyond that sentence.

**One promise you should break.**

Ibn Hajar: "what is meant by the promise in the hadith is a promise of good. As for evil, breaking it is recommended, and may be obligatory, so long as no harm follows from failing to carry it out" [16].

So a threat is not a promise you owe anyone. If you told a child you would never take them anywhere again, or told a relative you would never help them, you are not bound by it — you are encouraged to break it.

**In practice.** Say *in sha' Allah* and mean it as a genuine dependence rather than a hedge you can hide behind. Promise less than you can deliver rather than more. If you cannot keep a promise, tell the person early rather than letting the day arrive — that costs you something, which is exactly why it counts. And if you find you routinely intend, at the moment of promising, to see whether you feel like it later, that is the trait the hadith names, and the remedy is to stop making the promise, not to get better at excuses.

فتأويل الكلام إذن: فتبدل من بعدهم بدل سوء، ورثوا كتاب الله: تعلموه، وضيعوا العمل به فخالفوا حكمه، يرشون في حكم الله، فيأخذون الرشوة فيه من عرض هذا العاجل الأدنى، يعني بالأدنى: الأقرب من الآجل الأبعد، ويقولون إذا فعلوا ذلك: إن الله سيغفر لنا ذنوبنا، تمنيا على الله الأباطيل، كما قال جل ثناؤه فيهم: {فويل للذين يكتبون الكتاب بأيديهم ثم يقولون هذا من عند الله ليشتروا به ثمنا قليلا فويل لهم مما كتبت أيديهم وويل لهم مما يكسبون} [البقرة: 79] {وإن يأتهم عرض مثله يأخذوه} [الأعراف: 169] يقول: وإن شرع لهم ذنب حرام مثله من الرشوة بعد ذلك أخذوه واستحلوه، ولم يرتدعوا عنه. يخبر جل ثناؤه عنهم أنهم أهل إصرار على ذنوبهم، وليسوا بأهل إنابة ولا توبة. وبنحو الذي قلنا في ذلك قال أهل التأويل وإن اختلفت عنه عباراتهم
