# I'm not sure whether my wudu broke — what do I do?

## Answer

**You still have wudu. Pray.**

This is not a lenient reading of a hard case — it is the case the Prophet was
asked about directly, and the answer he gave is unusually blunt. A man complained
of feeling something during prayer: **when one of you is in prayer and feels a
movement in his backside, whether he has broken wind or not, and it becomes
unclear to him — let him not leave until he hears a sound or finds a smell.** [1]
The same report reaches us in another wording: **let him not turn away until he
hears a sound or finds a smell.** [2]

Read what that is actually doing. It does not tell you to check, or to be
careful, or to redo it if unsure. It sets a *threshold of evidence* and puts it
deliberately high — sound or smell, one of the two — and rules that anything
below it does not count. Al-Tirmidhi records the hadith and then states the
settled position plainly: **this is the position of the scholars: that wudu is
not obliged upon him except by a hadath where he hears a sound or finds a
smell.** [3]

**The principle underneath it.** Certainty is not removed by doubt. Ibn Abd
al-Barr: **in it is that certainty is not to be abandoned for doubt, until a
certainty comes that removes it.** [4] You were certain you had wudu. You are
merely uncertain that you lost it. Certainty outranks uncertainty, so your wudu
stands.

**Degrees of doubt make no difference.** This is the part people get wrong.
Al-Nawawi: **there is no difference in doubt between the two possibilities being
equal as to whether the hadath occurred or not, or one of them being stronger, or
it predominating in his estimation — no wudu is due upon him in any case.** [5]
So "I think it probably broke" is still doubt, and still does not break it. He
generalises the rule beyond wudu — doubt about whether you prayed three rak'ahs
or four, whether you bowed, whether you made the intention — **all of these
doubts have no effect, and the default is that this new event did not occur.**
[6]

**The one case that reverses.** If you are certain you *lost* wudu and merely
doubt whether you made it again, then you must make wudu — and here there is no
disagreement at all: **as for when he is certain of the hadath and doubts the
purification, wudu is obliged upon him by the consensus of the Muslims.** [7]
The rule is not "always assume the easier thing." It is: hold to whichever state
you were certain of, and let the doubtful one go.

**May I renew anyway, just to be safe?** Once, yes, and it is even encouraged:
**it is recommended for him to perform wudu out of caution; and if he does so out
of caution while his doubt continues, his obligation is discharged.** [8] But
notice the shape of that permission — a single precautionary wudu, not a habit.

**If this happens to you constantly, that is a different problem, and this page
is the treatment for it.** Repeating wudu five and six times, restarting prayers,
never feeling clean — that is *waswasa*, and the cure is not more washing. It is
to act on the ruling above deliberately, against the feeling, until the feeling
loses its grip: you pray *without* renewing, precisely when everything in you says
renew. The Prophet's instruction to stay in the prayer was given to a man in
exactly your position, and obeying it is worship, not carelessness. Ibn
al-Mubarak drew the line as sharply as it can be drawn: **if he doubts about the
hadath, wudu is not obliged upon him until he is certain with a certainty he
could swear an oath upon.** [9]

Note finally what the Qur'an itself names as the things that break purity — a
short, concrete list: **and if ye are sick or on a journey, or one of you cometh
from the closet, or ye have had contact with women, and ye find not water** [10].
Events, not sensations. A feeling is not on the list.
