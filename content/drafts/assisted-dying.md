# What does Islam say about assisted dying?

> Reviewer note: The material on taking a life, including one's own, is explicit. Handle the reader gently — someone asking may be watching a relative suffer — and separate it from withdrawing futile treatment, which is a different question and is not settled here.


## Answer

**This page has to be direct about a severe ruling, and it should be read by someone who may be watching a person they love suffer. Both of those are true at once.** The material on ending a life, including one's own, is explicit and admits of very little argument. What it does *not* settle is a set of modern questions that people often confuse with it — and separating them is most of the practical help available here.

**The prohibition.**

`ولا تقتلوا أنفسكم` — do not kill yourselves. Al-Tabari records the reading that takes it as a prohibition on killing one another too: `ولا يقتل بعضكم بعضا، وأنتم أهل ملة واحدة ودعوة واحدة ودين واحد، فجعل جل ثناؤه أهل الإسلام كلهم بعضهم من بعض،` [1] — you are one community, and He made the people of Islam all part of one another.

That reading matters here, because assisted dying involves two people. On either reading of the verse, both are addressed.

The hadith is stark: `من قتل نفسه بحديدة، فحديدته بيده يجأ بها في بطنه في نار جهنم، خالدا مخلدا فيها أبدا` [2] — whoever kills himself with a blade, his blade is in his hand, stabbing himself with it, in the Fire.

**The report that speaks most directly to a wish to end suffering.**

There is a hadith of a man in earlier times with a wound, who was `جزع` — unable to bear it — and took his own life, and God said `بادرني عبدي بنفسه` [3] — my servant hastened ahead of me with his soul — with the consequence, in Ibn Hajar's words, that `حرمت عليه الجنة` [4].

Ibn Hajar's gloss on the first phrase is the one to sit with: `هو كناية عن استعجال المذكور الموت` [5] — it is an expression for his hastening death.

That is the case. Not despair in the abstract; pain a person judged unbearable, and a decision to bring the end forward. The tradition met that case and did not carve out an exception for it. Presenting the material otherwise would be dishonest.

**What this does not settle, and the distinction matters enormously.**

Declining a treatment that will not cure you is not in these texts. Neither is stopping a treatment that is only prolonging dying, nor giving pain relief in doses that control suffering even where they may shorten life as a side effect, nor a decision not to attempt resuscitation. Those are distinct questions, and the material above — which is about *hastening death as the object* — does not answer them.

There is a separate answer on withdrawing life support that takes up part of this, and both turn on medical facts these authors had no access to.

The distinction the tradition is drawing is between intending death and accepting it. That line is genuinely difficult in a modern intensive care unit, which is exactly why it needs a scholar and the treating doctors together rather than a page.

**What these books do not reach.**

Nothing here addresses a jurisdiction where assisted dying is lawful and a person is being offered it. Nothing addresses palliative sedation. Nothing addresses capacity, coercion, or a person who fears becoming a burden — which is very often the real reason and is a question about a family and a health system rather than about a ruling. Nothing addresses the position of a doctor asked to participate, which is its own matter.

**If this is about you.**

If you are asking because you want your own life to end, please treat this page as the wrong document. The ruling above is not addressed to a person in that state as an accusation, and reading it as one would be a misuse of it. Speak to a doctor and to someone you trust today. There is a separate answer here on suicide written with more care for that situation than this one, and it exists for a reason.

**If this is about someone else.**

Take to a scholar, alongside the medical team, the specific questions rather than the phrase:

- Is the proposal to *cause* death, or to stop an intervention that is no longer achieving anything?
- Is the pain actually being treated adequately? Very often the request changes when it is.
- Who is asking, and are they able to decide freely?
- What does the law where you live permit or require, and what does it not?

And know that the tradition's severity here about hastening death sits alongside an equally strong body of material on caring for the dying, on relieving suffering, and on the reward of sitting with someone through it. Those are not in tension. The second is what it asks of you when the first closes a door.
