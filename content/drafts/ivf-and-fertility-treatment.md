# Is IVF allowed?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

## Answer

**These books cannot tell you whether IVF is permitted, and this page will not pretend otherwise.** No author here imagined conception occurring outside a body, and nothing in the collection addresses it. But the silence is not even. On one part of the modern question — the part involving anyone outside the marriage — the classical material is dense, explicit and firm. On the other part, the couple using their own gametes, it is genuinely absent. Knowing which is which is the useful thing this page can give you, because it tells you what to actually ask.

**First, the easy part: wanting a child, and seeking help to have one, is not treated here with any suspicion.**

Al-Qurtubi has an unusually sharp aside on this. Against the claim, which he attributes to some ignorant ascetics, that seeking children is foolish: `وفي هذا رد على بعض جهال المتصوفة حيث قال : الذي يطلب الولد أحمق وما عرف انه هو الغبي الآخرق` [1] — a refutation of those who said *the one who seeks a child is a fool*, not realising that he is himself the dull and clumsy one. He notes that al-Bukhari gave the matter its own chapter heading: `وقد ترجم البخاري على هذا باب طلب الولد` [2] — the chapter on *seeking a child*. And he concludes: `والأخبار في هذا المعنى كثيرة تحث على طلب الولد وتندب إليه لما يرجوه الإنسان من نفعه في حياته وبعد موته` [3] — the reports on this are many, urging and recommending the seeking of a child.

The paradigm case in scripture is a man asking for a child when it was biologically out of the question, and being given one. On the objection that children are elsewhere called a trial, al-Qurtubi answers: `فالجواب أن الدعاء بالولد معلوم من الكتاب والسنة حسب ما تقدم في آل عمران بيانه ثم إن زكريا عليه السلام تحرز فقال` [4] — praying for a child is established in the Book and the Sunna; and Zakariyya took a precaution in *how* he asked, praying for offspring who would be good. `والولد إذا كان بهذه الصفة نفع أبويه في الدنيا والآخرة وخرج من حد العداوة والفتنة إلى حد المسرة والنعمة` [5].

So nothing here casts infertility treatment as an interference with decree, or as wanting what one has not been given. If you have been told that, it is not coming from these books.

**Second, the part the corpus decides — and it decides it hard.**

The whole apparatus of parentage in Islamic law rests on a definition, and Ibn al-'Arabi gives it in one line. `وهو عبارة عن مرج الماء بين الذكر والأنثى على وجه الشرع فإن كان بمعصية كان خلقا مطلقا ولم يكن نسبا محققا` [6] — lineage is the mingling of the two waters between male and female **in the manner the law prescribes**; if it occurs through disobedience, it is creation in an absolute sense but not established lineage. Al-Qurtubi reproduces the same definition with `خلط` for `مرج` [7].

Read that carefully, because it is the crux. `نسب` is not simply biological descent. It is a legal relation, and the law attaches it to a lawful union. Biology alone produces `خلق` — a creature made — but not `نسب`.

The consequence is spelled out, and it is not small. `وإذا لم يكن نسب شرعا فلا صهر شرعا فلا يحرم الزنى بنت أم ولا أم بنت وما يحرم من الحلال لا يحرم من الحرام` [8] — where there is no lineage in law there is no affinity in law either. The entire structure that follows from parentage — who may marry whom, who is `محرم` to whom, who inherits — hangs on that attachment and does not attach itself.

The hadith the jurists build on is `الولد للفراش وللعاهر الحجر` [9] — the child belongs to the marital bed, and the fornicator gets the stone. Al-Nawawi's gloss on the first clause: `فمعناه أنه إذا كان للرجل زوجه أو مملوكه صارت فراشا له فأتت بولد لمدة الامكان منه لحقه الولد وصار ولدا يجرى بينهما التوارث وغيره من أحكام الولادة` [10] — the child attaches to him, with inheritance and every other ruling of parentage following. And on the second: `ومعنى له الحجر أي له الخيبة ولا حق له في الولد` [11] — he gets disappointment, and **no claim on the child at all**.

Nor can that be fixed after the fact by agreement. Al-Fatawa al-Hindiyya: `إذا زنى رجل بامرأة فجاءت بولد فادعاه الزاني لم يثبت نسبه منه وأما المرأة فيثبت نسبه منها` [12] — if the man claims the child, lineage is not established from him; from the mother it is. Parentage is not something the adults involved can confer on themselves by wanting it.

Underneath all of this sits a stated posture. Al-Sarakhsi: `وأمر النسب مبنى على الاحتياط` [13] — the matter of lineage is built on caution — and, once established, `ومتى ثبت النسب بالشبهة لا يمكن نفيه بحال` [14], it cannot afterwards be undone.

**Now the honest statement of what that does and does not settle.**

Every one of those texts is about unlawful intercourse. Not one is about a laboratory, and no author here applied them to a case where no intercourse occurred at all. It would be dishonest to present them as a ruling on donor gametes or on surrogacy, because they are not that.

What they are is the reason those cases are the hard ones. The concern these rules encode — that a child's legal parentage be determinate, arise from the marriage, and not be assignable by the wishes of the adults — is exactly the concern that a third party's sperm, a third party's egg, or a third party's womb raises. Whatever a scholar tells you about those arrangements will be reasoning from this material, and this material leans in one direction. If someone tells you a donor arrangement is straightforward, ask them how the child's `نسب` is established on the definition above, and what becomes of the affinity rules that follow from it.

**Third, what the corpus simply does not reach.**

For a married couple using their own gametes, the classical materials run out entirely, and on the specific points where modern disagreement actually lives:

- **Fertilisation outside the body.** No text contemplates it. There is nothing to reason from about whether the place of conception matters when the two parties are married to each other.
- **Surplus embryos.** What may be done with fertilised eggs that are not transferred, whether they may be frozen, discarded, or kept after a marriage ends, has no analogue here. The nearest classical discussion is about the stages of formation in the womb, which belongs to the question about abortion and is disputed there — and in any case concerns a pregnancy, not a dish.
- **The procedures themselves.** Retrieval and handling involve exposure and third-party handling that no author here was asked about.

These are not gaps this page can close by cleverness. They are the actual questions, and the corpus is silent on all three.

**What to do.**

Ask a scholar you can speak with directly, and bring the protocol rather than the word "IVF" — the term covers arrangements the tradition would treat very differently. Specifically:

- Whose sperm and whose egg, with nothing from outside the marriage at any stage.
- Whether any third party carries the pregnancy.
- What the clinic does with embryos that are not transferred, and what happens to stored embryos if the marriage ends or one spouse dies.
- What safeguards exist against mix-up of samples. That last one is not a technicality: `أمر النسب مبنى على الاحتياط` is a principle about exactly this, and a clinic's chain-of-custody is the modern form of that caution.

And ask a fertility specialist about the medicine, which no book here can advise on.

The tradition wants you to have the child you are asking for — that much is unambiguous. What it insists on is that the child arrive with its parentage intact and unassignable to anyone else. That is the standard your questions should be aimed at, and it is the one a living scholar will apply.
