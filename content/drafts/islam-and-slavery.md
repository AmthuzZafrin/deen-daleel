# What does Islam say about slavery?

## Answer

**The honest starting point: these books do not abolish slavery.** Every work in this corpus was written before the nineteenth century, and slavery is an institution they assume, regulate, litigate and devote whole chapters of law to. Anyone who tells you the classical sources abolished it is not describing the sources. What they do instead is more specific, and it is worth reading carefully rather than being defended or attacked in the abstract.

**They classify slavery as a harm, and freeing as one of the highest acts of worship.** Ibn Qudama opens his book on manumission with the word itself: `وهو في الشرع تحرير الرقبة وتخليصها من الرق` [1] — in the law it is the liberation of a person and their extrication from bondage. Then his statement of why it ranks where it does: `والعتق من أفضل القرب إلى الله تعالى ; لأن الله تعالى جعله كفارة للقتل، والوطء في رمضان والأيمان، وجعله النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم فكاكا لمعتقه من النار، ولأن فيه تخليصا للآدمي المعصوم من ضرر الرق وملك نفسه ومنافعه، وتكميل أحكامه، وتمكنه من التصرف في نفسه ومنافعه، على حسب إرادته واختياره` [2] — manumission is among the best acts of nearness to God, because God made it the expiation for killing, for intercourse in Ramadan and for broken oaths; because the Prophet made it a ransom from the Fire; and because it is the extrication of an inviolable human being **from the harm of bondage** — giving him ownership of himself and of his own labour, completing his legal standing, and enabling him to act by his own will and choice.

Read the reason again. The tradition's own word for the condition is `ضرر`, harm; and what freeing restores is described as self-ownership and the capacity to act by one's own choice. That is the classical books' evaluation of slavery stated in their own vocabulary, not a modern gloss laid over them.

The reward attached is unusually heavy: `من أعتق رقبة مؤمنة، أعتق الله بكل عضو منه عضوا من النار، حتى يعتق فرجه بفرجه` [3] — for each limb of the person freed, God frees a limb of the one who freed him from the Fire. Al-Qurtubi cites the same idea in the plainest form: `بلغنا أنه ما من مسلم يعتق رقبة إلا كانت فداءه من النار` [4].

**Freeing is the built-in penalty for ordinary sins.** Note what is on Ibn Qudama's list [2]: manslaughter, breaking a fast in Ramadan by intercourse, breaking an oath. These are not exotic offences; they are the failures of an ordinary life. The effect of putting manumission at the head of the expiation scale is that the slave population is drained by the routine functioning of the religion.

**A fixed share of the community's wealth is permanently assigned to buying people out of it.** One of the eight categories of *zakat* is `وفي الرقاب` — "for the freeing of necks." Al-Qurtubi reports both the agreement and the dispute within it: `أنهم أجمعوا على أن المكاتب مراد واختلفوا في عتق الرقاب` [5] — all agree the contracted slave buying his own freedom is intended; they differ over whether outright purchase-and-manumission also qualifies. Al-Jassas argues for the assisting reading — `فثبت بذلك أن الصدقة على المكاتبين معونة لهم في رقابهم حتى يعتقوا وذلك موافق لقوله تعالى وفي الرقاب` [6] — and cites the hadith that separates the two acts explicitly. A man asked for a deed that would take him to Paradise, and was told to free a soul and to ransom a neck; when he asked whether these were the same, the answer was: `أعتق النسمة وفك الرقبة قال أو ليسا سواء قال لا عتق النسمة أن تفوز بعتقها وفك الرقبة أن تعين في ثمنها` [7] — no; freeing a soul is that you alone accomplish its release, ransoming a neck is that you help toward its price. Two distinct acts, both commanded.

**Several rules make holding a person structurally unstable.** Ownership of a close relative dissolves itself the moment it arises: `من ملك ذا رحم محرم فهو حر` [8]. Partial manumission drags the rest with it — `من أعتق نصيبا له في مملوك، أو شقصا، فعليه خلاصه من ماله، إن كان له مال. فإن لم يكن له مال، استسعى العبد في قيمته، غير مشقوق عليه` [9]: free your share and you owe the balance from your own wealth; if you cannot pay, the slave works toward his own remaining value, and not under hardship. You cannot free a fraction of a person and stop.

And once freedom is given it is total, with no residue. Malik: `من اعتق عبدا له فبت عتقه حتى تجوز شهادته وتتم حريته ويثبت ميراثه فليس لسيده ان يشترط عليه مثل ما يشترط على عبده من مال أو خدمة ولا يحمل عليه شيئا من الرق` [10] — once manumission is final, his testimony is admissible, his freedom complete and his inheritance established; the former master may not impose on him the conditions he imposes on a slave, whether money or service, nor load any part of bondage back onto him. And: `فهو إذا كان له العبد خالصا أحق باستكمال عتاقته ولا يخلطها بشيء من الرق` [11] — he has the greater right to complete his manumission and not adulterate it with anything of servitude.

**The treatment rules are strict and are phrased as kinship, not charity.** The most famous scene is Abu Dharr at Rabadha, wearing one half of a matched pair of cloaks while his slave wore the other. Asked why he had not kept both, he explained: he had once taunted a man about his non-Arab mother, and the Prophet said to him `يا أبا ذر، إنك امرؤ فيك جاهلية` [12] — you are a man with *jahiliyya* still in you — then: `هم إخوانكم، جعلهم الله تحت أيديكم، فأطعموهم مما تأكلون، وألبسوهم مما تلبسون، ولا تكلفوهم ما يغلبهم، فإن كلفتموهم فأعينوهم` [13] — they are your brothers whom God has placed under your hands; feed them from what you eat, clothe them from what you wear, do not burden them beyond their strength, and if you do, help them yourselves. Another version adds the prohibition directly: `من لاءمكم من مملوكيكم فاطعموه مما تأكلون، وأكسوه مما تلبسون، ومن لم يلائمكم منهم فبيعوه، ولا تعذبوا خلق الله` [14] — and do not torture God's creation. And a small domestic instruction that says a good deal about the intended register: when a servant has cooked your food and brings it to you having borne its heat and smoke, `فليقعده معه، فليأكل` [15] — seat him with you, and let him eat.

**Two things that should stop this being read as a defence.** First, the same works reason comfortably from inside the institution rather than against it. Ibn Hajar, weighing which manumission carries more merit, offers as a ground `أن عتق الأنثى غالبا يستلزم ضياعها ولان في عتق الذكر من المعاني العامة ما ليس في الأنثى كصلاحيته للقضاء وغيره مما يصلح للذكور دون الإناث` [16] — that freeing a woman usually leaves her destitute, and that freeing a man carries public benefits a woman's manumission does not, such as his eligibility for judicial office. That is an argument that takes the surrounding social order as fixed. Second, the same hadith that forbids torture also says `ومن لم يلائمكم منهم فبيعوه` [14] — sell the one who does not suit you. Buying and selling continue in the same chapters as the brotherhood language. Both things are on the page.

**What the corpus does not give you.** No work here argues that slavery as an institution must end. The abolition arguments Muslims make today — that the aims of the law and the whole weight of the manumission material point one way; that the conditions under which enslavement was permitted no longer exist; that no legitimate authority may reinstate it — are modern, and you will not find them in these books. Nor do these works speak to Atlantic chattel slavery, to the racialised forms it took, or to contemporary trafficking, all of which came later or elsewhere.

What you can take from the corpus itself is narrower and still worth having: the direction of every mechanism in it runs one way, out of bondage and never into it; the tradition's own name for the condition is harm; the treatment obligations are stated as brotherhood and are enforceable; and freeing a person is ranked among the highest acts available to a Muslim. Where you want the further step — that the institution is finished and cannot return — that argument exists, but it is later than these sources and has to be got from a living scholar rather than from this shelf.
