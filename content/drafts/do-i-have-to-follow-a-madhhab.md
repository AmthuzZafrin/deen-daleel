# Do I have to follow a madhhab at all?

## Answer

**Short answer: no, binding yourself to one school is not obligatory — that is
stated inside the Hanafi tradition itself — but the alternative is not "just me
and the texts". The alternative the sources allow is asking qualified people,
whoever they happen to be.** Following a madhhab and following the Qur'an and
sunna were never presented as competing options; the schools are how the texts
were worked out.

**Where the obligation actually sits.** The Qur'an gives an instruction to
someone who does not know: **"Ask the followers of the Remembrance if ye know
not!"** [1] — repeated in near-identical words elsewhere: **"And We sent not (as
Our messengers) before thee other than men, whom We inspired. Ask the followers
of the Reminder if ye know not?"** [2]. The command is *ask*. It is not "work it
out" and it is not "attach yourself to a name".

**Is committing to one school required?** Ibn 'Abidin transmits Ibn al-Humam's
treatment from *al-Tahrir*, and the reasoning is worth following. On whether a
person may follow a different scholar on a different question: **"وهل يقلد غيره
في غيره؟ المختار نعم للقطع بأنهم كانوا يستفتون مرة واحدا ومرة غيره غير ملتزمين
مفتيا واحدا"** — *and may he follow someone else in a different matter? The
chosen view is yes, because it is certain that they used to seek a fatwa once
from one person and another time from another, not binding themselves to a
single mufti* [3]. The precedent cited is the practice of the early community
itself.

On whether an existing commitment binds: **"فلو التزم مذهبا معينا كأبي حنيفة
والشافعي: فقيل يلزم، وقيل مثل من لم يلتزم وهو الغالب على الظن لعدم ما يوجبه
شرعا"** — *if he commits to a particular school such as Abu Hanifa's or
al-Shafi'i's: it is said it binds him, and it is said he is like one who did not
commit — and this is the more likely, because there is nothing in the law that
makes it obligatory* [4]. Note the ground of the judgement: not preference, but
the absence of a text imposing it.

His commentator Ibn Amir Hajj states the positive side: **"بل الدليل الشرعي
اقتضى العمل بقول المجتهد وتقليده فيه فيما احتاج إليه وهو: (فاسألوا أهل الذكر)
والسؤال إنما يتحقق عند طلب حكم الحادثة المعينة، فإذا ثبت عنده قول المجتهد وجب
عمله به، وأما التزامه فلم يثبت من السمع اعتباره ملزما إنما ذلك في الندب"** —
*rather the legal evidence required acting on the mujtahid's statement and
following him in what one needs, and that is "Ask the people of remembrance."
Asking is realised only in seeking the ruling of a specific occurrence. So when
the mujtahid's statement is established for him, acting on it becomes obligatory
— but as for binding oneself to him, no scriptural basis is established for
treating it as binding; that is only in the category of the recommended* [5].

Two obligations, cleanly separated: **acting on the ruling you obtain is
obligatory; tying yourself to one source of rulings is recommended, not
required.**

**"The layman has no madhhab."** Ibn 'Abidin states the maxim and its reason:
**"العامي لا مذهب له، بل مذهبه مفتيه، وعلله في شرح التحرير بأن المذهب إنما يكون
لمن له نوع نظر واستدلال وبصر بالمذهب على حسبه"** — *the layman has no madhhab;
rather his madhhab is his mufti. And it is explained in the commentary on
al-Tahrir that a madhhab belongs only to one who has some capacity for
examination and inference and insight into the school, according to his level*
[6]. He adds that he laboured the point so that people would not be misled by
loosely-worded statements in the books. Saying "I am Hanafi" while knowing
nothing of the school is, on this reading, a description of an affiliation rather
than a fact about one's fiqh.

**But there is a case for settling on one.** Ibn 'Abidin also transmits the
Nasafi fatwas: **"الثبات على مذهب أبي حنيفة خير وأولى، قال: وهذه الكلمة أقرب إلى
الألفة"** — *remaining firm on the school of Abu Hanifa is better and more
fitting; and this statement is closer to concord* [7]. The argument is practical
and social rather than obligatory: coherence in your own practice, and not
picking through the schools for whichever ruling is easiest.

**Now the other half: can you skip all of it and go to the sources yourself?**
The Hanbali manual *Sifat al-Fatwa* records Imam Ahmad being asked almost exactly
that. The questioner describes a man who has the compiled books containing the
Prophet's ﷺ sayings and the disagreements of the Companions and Successors:
**"وليس للرجل بصر بالحديث الضعيف المتروك ولا للإسناد القوي من الضعيف فيجوز أن
يعمل بما شاء ويتخير ما أحب من متنه فيفتي ويعمل به قال لا يعمل حتى يسأل ما يؤخذ
به منها فيكون يعمل على أمر صحيح يسأل عن ذلك أهل العلم"** — *and the man has no
discernment of the weak, abandoned hadith, nor of the strong chain from the weak
— may he act on whatever he wishes and select what he likes of the text, and give
fatwa and act by it? He said: he does not act until he asks which of them is to
be taken, so that he acts on something sound; he asks the people of knowledge
about that* [8].

That is the "just Qur'an and sunna" position put to Ahmad ibn Hanbal, and his
answer is *ask*. Not because the texts are off limits — because possessing the
texts is not the same as being able to tell which of them stands.

The same work draws the corresponding line for what you may do with a fatwa you
receive: **"فلو استفتى عامي فقيها في حادثة فأفتاه بشيء فاعتقده مذهبا لم يجز له
أن يفتي به ولا لغيره أن يقلده فيه وإن كان معتقدا له لأنه غير عالم بصحته لكن له
الاخبار به"** — *if a layman seeks a fatwa from a jurist on an occurrence and he
gives him a ruling which he takes as his position, it is not permitted for him to
give fatwa with it, nor for anyone else to follow him in it — even if he believes
it — because he does not know its soundness; but he may report it* [9]. You may
say "I asked, and I was told X." You may not turn that into "the ruling is X."

**What to do.**

- **Ask someone qualified when you do not know.** That is the command in the
  verse, and it is what the whole discussion is built on.
- **You are not obliged to enrol in a school.** The reasoning above comes from
  inside the Hanafi tradition, not from outside it.
- **Settling on one is still recommended, and practically wise** — it keeps your
  practice coherent and stops you assembling a private religion out of the
  easiest option in each school.
- **Do not treat "Qur'an and sunna only" as the shortcut it sounds like.** Ahmad's
  answer identifies the missing skill precisely: telling which report stands.
- **Do not pass on rulings as rulings.** Report what you were told, as something
  you were told.

**Where this stops.** The passages above address whether commitment to a school
is obligatory. They do not tell you *which* school, and they do not address
switching schools mid-question to obtain a preferred result — a practice the
jurists discussed separately and with more caution than this answer's evidence
covers.

```
11037 أنا إسحاق بن إبراهيم أنا سليمان بن حرب حدثنا حماد بن سلمة عن ثابت عن أنس قال * كانت اليهود إذا حاضت المرأة منهم لم يؤاكلوهن ولم يشاربوهن ولم يجامعوهن في البيوت فسألوا النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم عن ذلك فأنزل الله تعالى ويسألونك عن المحيض قل هو أذى فاعتزلوا النساء في المحيض فأمرهم رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم أن يؤاكلوهن وأن يشاربوهن وأن يجامعوهن في البيوت وأن يصنعوا بهن كل شيء ما خلا النكاح \ 10954 \
```
