# What should I name my baby?

## Answer

The sources on this are unusually tidy: a short list of recommendations, a short list of things to avoid, one clear criterion behind both, and — importantly — a low temperature. Almost nothing here is a prohibition.

**The recommendations** come in one hadith: "Name yourselves with the names of the prophets. The most beloved of names to God are 'Abd Allah and 'Abd al-Rahman; the truest of them are Harith and Hammam; and the ugliest of them are Harb and Murra" [1]. Al-Nawawi cites it in the *Majmu'* alongside the Prophet naming a man's son 'Abd al-Rahman, naming Abu Talha's son 'Abd Allah, and naming his own son Ibrahim [2].

There is a second, more general instruction: "You will be called on the Day of Resurrection by your names and your fathers' names, so make your names good" [3]. It is worth knowing that both of the works citing it flag a problem with its chain. Abu Dawud appends the note "Ibn Abi Zakariyya did not meet Abu al-Darda'" [4]; al-Nawawi weighs it and reports the disagreement: "the better-known view is that he heard from Abu al-Darda', but al-Bayhaqi and a group said he did not hear from him, so it would be *mursal*" [5]. It is quoted very widely without either caveat.

**The criterion is meaning, and al-Tabari states it in one sentence.** As Ibn Hajar transmits it: "one should not be named with a name of ugly meaning, nor with a name that implies self-praise, nor with a name whose meaning is abuse" [6]. Three tests — is it ugly, does it claim something for the child, is it an insult.

The middle one is where the best-known example sits. A man named his daughter *Barra* — "the pious one" — and was told: "Do not claim purity for yourselves; God knows best who among you are people of piety", and then, when he asked what to call her, "Name her Zaynab" [7], [8]. The same thing happened to the Prophet's wife Juwayriya: "Juwayriya's name was Barra, and the Prophet changed her name to Juwayriya, and he disliked its being said: *he came out from Barra*" [9]. The direction runs the other way too — 'Umar's daughter was called 'Asiya, "disobedient", and the Prophet renamed her: "You are Jamila" [10].

Al-Tabari explains why meaning matters even though a name is just a label: "if names were only markers for persons, the reality of the description not being intended by them — still the reason for the dislike is that a hearer hears the name and supposes it is a description of the one named; and for that reason he used to change the name to one which, when its bearer was called by it, would be true" [11]. So the concern is what the name asserts about the child in the ears of everyone who uses it.

**And here is the part that lowers the temperature on all of it.** Al-Tabari again, in Ibn Hajar's summary: "the Messenger of God changed a number of names, and what he changed of that was not by way of forbidding being named by them, but by way of preference. And for that reason the Muslims permitted that an ugly man be named handsome and a corrupt one named righteous. The evidence for it is that he did not compel Hazn when he refused to change his name to Sahl — and if that had been binding, he would not have left him at his statement: *I will not change a name my father gave me*" [12]. A Companion declined a name change from the Prophet himself and was left alone. That is the register.

The same holds for the one list that reads like a prohibition. "Do not name your servant Rabah, nor Yasar, nor Aflah, nor Nafi'" [13] — names meaning roughly *profit*, *ease*, *success* and *benefit*. The reason given is entirely practical: you ask "is Success here?" and the answer comes back "no". Al-Nawawi is explicit about the weight: "our companions said it is disliked to use these names mentioned in the hadith and what carries their meaning, and the dislike is not restricted to them alone — **and it is a dislike of avoidance, not of prohibition**; the cause of the dislike is what he explained in his words *you will say: is he there? and it will be said: no* — so he disliked it for the unpleasantness of the answer, and because it might drop some people into something of ill omen" [14]. And on the report that the Prophet intended to forbid these names but did not: "its meaning is that he intended to forbid them with a prohibition of interdiction, but he did not forbid" [15].

**Prophets' and angels' names are permitted**, with a couple of minority dissents worth knowing. Al-Nawawi: "our position and the position of the majority is the permissibility of naming with the names of the prophets and the angels, and no disagreement about it has been transmitted except from 'Umar b. al-Khattab, that he forbade naming with the names of the prophets; and from al-Harith b. Miskin, that he disliked naming with the names of the angels; and from Malik, dislike of naming with Jibril and Yasin" [16]. His argument against them: "our evidence is the Prophet's naming his son Ibrahim, and his naming a great many of his Companions with the names of the prophets in his lifetime and after — together with the reports we have mentioned; and no prohibition regarding that is established from the Prophet, so it is not disliked" [17].

**Two small extras.** The *kunya* Abu al-Qasim is treated separately: "Name yourselves by my name, but do not take my *kunya*" [18] — a rule about the by-name, not the given name. And what people call a child afterwards is its own matter: the verse *do not revile one another with nicknames* came down about a community where "there was not a man among us but had two or three names", and the Prophet would call someone by one of them and be told "he is angered by that name" [19].

**So, practically.** Go for meaning: something true, something that does not claim piety or success for a child who has not lived yet, something that is not an insult and will not become one. 'Abd Allah and 'Abd al-Rahman are singled out; prophets' names are explicitly fine. Nothing on this sheet makes an Arabic name as such a requirement — the tests the scholars actually give are about what a name *means* and how it will land on the people who say it. And on the strength of al-Tabari's reading, most of this is preference: the Prophet changed names he thought could be better, and left alone a man who preferred to keep his father's.
