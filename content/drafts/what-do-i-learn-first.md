# What should I learn first?

## Answer

Prayer. Then whatever your own life makes obligatory. Then, slowly, everything
else.

That is not a suggestion — it is the ordering the jurists worked out, and their
reasoning is worth having because it tells you what to ignore as much as what to
take up.

**The first tier is what you personally are obliged to do.** Ibn Nujaym defines
the knowledge that is individually obligatory as **what ordinary people need for
the obligations — like ablution, prayer and fasting.** [1] It falls due when the
obligation does, with one important rider: **if leaving it until the time comes
in would leave you unable, you are obliged to learn it beforehand.** [2] Prayer
arrives five times today. So it is first, and it is now.

**Then the sentence that should take the pressure off.** In Ibn Nujaym's own
words: **only the plain matters must be learned, not the fine points and the rare
cases.** [3] You are not behind because you cannot name the four schools'
positions on wiping over socks. That is not the tier you are in.

**The second tier is indexed to your life, not to a syllabus.** He spells it out:
**whoever has zakatable wealth is obliged to know the plain rules of zakat;
whoever buys and sells, the rules of transactions; whoever has a spouse, the
rules of married life** [4] — and likewise **what is lawful and unlawful in food,
drink and clothing.** [5] Which means your list is different from the next
person's, and shorter than you think. If you have no wealth above the threshold,
zakat can wait.

**What comes before extra memorisation.** Ibn Taymiyya, asked about this exact
ordering: **the knowledge obligatory on a person individually — knowing what God
commanded and what He forbade — comes before memorising more of the Qur'an than
is required**, because the first is a duty and the second a recommendation. [6]
He is not diminishing the Qur'an; in the same answer he says **what is sought
from the Qur'an is understanding its meanings and acting on it.** [7]

**What not to start with.** Ibn Taymiyya names it directly, and it is remarkably
current: people who **busy themselves with surplus knowledge — theology,
disputation, controversy, or rare sub-cases** [8] — while the basics go unlearned.
Six centuries later that is a phone at midnight, three scholars disagreeing in
your feed, and still no idea what to say in ruku'.

**A practical order**, drawn from the above: the prayer itself — its times, wudu,
the postures, al-Fatiha, what is said bowing and prostrating. Then purity in
enough detail to know when to repeat wudu. Then fasting, before Ramadan rather
than during it. Then whichever of zakat, transactions and marriage your own
circumstances have made obligatory. Everything else after that.

**One thing the texts cannot give you**: a teacher. They set out what is owed,
not who will sit with you on a Tuesday. Find a person or a class if you possibly
can — the ordering above is much easier to follow with someone to ask.
