# Can I trust halal certification labels?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

## Answer

A halal label is a claim made by an organisation. The sources have a great
deal to say about how you treat a claim like that, and nothing at all to say
about the organisations themselves — so this answer can give you the rule for
weighing the claim, but not a verdict on any particular certifier.

**Start from where the sources start.** Food is lawful unless something makes
it otherwise. "The beast of cattle is made lawful unto you (for food) except
that which is announced unto you (herein)" [1], and "This day are (all) good
things made lawful for you. The food of those who have received the Scripture
is lawful for you" [2]. Lawfulness is the default; a restriction has to come
from somewhere.

**The certificate is a report, and reports about lawfulness follow a known
rule.** Al-Sarakhsi treats this directly. If someone tells you that a food is
forbidden, "since he has reported a matter of religion, and the lawful and the
forbidden belong to the domain of religion" [3] — meaning the report is
accepted on the terms religious reports are accepted, not the stricter terms
of court testimony. Free or enslaved, man or woman, it makes no difference,
because the subject is religion and not a legal claim against anyone. And a
single trustworthy Muslim's word is enough to stop you: "because he has
reported that the substance itself is forbidden, and that is a religious
matter, so the proof is complete with a single report — and just as he does
not eat it, he does not feed it to anyone else" [4].

**When reports conflict, you weigh; when you cannot weigh, you fall back to
the default.** One says lawful, another says forbidden: you act on the stronger
impression. "And if he has no view on it and the two states are equal in his
estimation, there is no harm in eating it or drinking it" [5] — "if he has no
view, he holds to the original ruling of purity" [6]. This is the part most
often missed. Deadlock does not resolve to prohibition. It resolves to the
default, which is permission.

**Doubt about the paperwork is not the same as doubt about the food.**
Al-Sarakhsi separates the two carefully. If the informant says the substance
itself is unlawful, you stop. If he says only that the seller had no right to
sell it — a defect in the transaction, not in the meat — then "a single report
can place unease in his heart, and keeping clear of places of unease is
better; but if he does not keep clear of it, he is within his latitude in that"
[7]. A great deal of certification anxiety is of this second kind: not "is this
pork" but "was the audit thorough".

**And this is genuinely a case of the doubtful middle.** The Prophet ﷺ said:
"The lawful is clear and the unlawful is clear, and between the two are
doubtful matters" [8], and "leave what causes you doubt for what does not
cause you doubt" [9]. Ibn Hajar records that the scholars explained the
doubtful matters as four things, "the first of them being conflicting
evidence... the second being the disagreement of the scholars" [10]. Al-Nawawi
notes that where nothing has become clear to the qualified scholar and the
matter stays doubtful, "is it to be taken as lawful, or as forbidden, or is
judgement suspended? — three positions" [11]. So the uncertainty you feel in
the aisle is a recognised category, not a failure of piety.

**But scrupulousness has limits the sources also name.** Al-Khattabi, quoted
by Ibn Hajar: "everything you doubt, caution is to avoid it — then it falls
into three classes: obligatory, recommended, and disliked. The obligatory is
avoiding what entails committing something forbidden; the recommended is
avoiding dealings with someone most of whose wealth is unlawful; and the
disliked is avoiding the concessions the Law has granted, by way of
over-strictness" [12]. That third class exists. Refusing what the Law permits,
out of a scruple the Law does not impose, is itself named as blameworthy. Ibn
Taymiyya adds the other half of the same error: many people think caution is
only ever a matter of abstaining, "so they see caution only in leaving the
forbidden and not in performing the obligatory" [13] — a person who agonises
over a stock cube while neglecting a duty to a parent or a neighbour has the
balance backwards.

**One thing a label structurally cannot show you.** Ibn Hajar, on how the
outward form of an act can differ from its ruling: "an animal becomes lawful
if it is slaughtered for the purpose of eating, and forbidden if it is
slaughtered for other than Allah — and the outward form is one" [14]. The
carcass looks identical either way. What separates them happened at the moment
of slaughter and left no trace on the meat. This is precisely why the question
is a question about *testimony* — someone who was there has to tell you — and
why it cannot be settled by inspecting the product.

**Which is where this answer stops.** Whether a particular certifier is
reliable turns on facts that no classical text contains and that this library
cannot hold: who audits, how often, whether the audit is announced, whether
stunning is used and of what kind, whether the certifier is paid by the plant
it inspects, what the logo on that specific packet was actually certifying.
Those facts change year to year and differ country to country. The sources
above give you the *test* to apply — is the informant trustworthy, is the
report about the substance or about the paperwork, and what is the default
when you genuinely cannot tell — but the evidence to apply it to is not in
any book here.

So: ask someone who knows your local situation. A scholar or mufti in your
country will usually know which bodies are taken seriously there and why.
Many certifiers publish their standard — what they permit on stunning, on
enzymes, on the slaughterer — and reading it directly answers more than a
logo ever will. Where you cannot find out, remember the rule above: a
deadlock returns you to the default, and the default is permission.
