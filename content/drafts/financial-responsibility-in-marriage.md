# Who pays for what in a marriage?

## Answer

He pays. All of it — housing, food, clothing, and the children's upkeep. Her income, if she has any, is
hers.

That is not a cultural preference; it is the settled position. Ibn Hajar: **"consensus has been
concluded on the obligation, though they differed over its measure"** [1].

### What he owes

Ibn Qudama: **"she is owed from him all of her needs — food, drink, clothing, and housing"** [2]. The
Prophet ﷺ, in Jabir's report: **"and they have a right over you to their provision and their clothing in
kindness"** [3].

The Hanafi manual adds detail that answers most practical questions at once. Maintenance is owed **"to
the wife from her husband, and clothing, according to the state of the two of them — even if she is
withholding herself for the mahr"** [4]. Housing means **"lodging in a dwelling free of his family and
hers"** [5] — a separate home, not a room in his parents' house, if she wants it. A domestic helper is
included **"for her servant, if he is well off"** [6]. And the children's upkeep is on the father:
**"and for his poor child"** [7].

That last point is settled from the other direction too. Ibn Hajar, on the mother: **"this indicates
that maintenance of her sons is not obligatory upon her"** [8].

### How much

Three positions, and they matter in practice.

- **By his means alone** — al-Shafi'i: **"the consideration is by the husband's state alone"** [9].
- **By her need** — **"Abu Hanifa and Malik said: the woman's state is considered, according to the
  measure of her sufficiency"** [10].
- **By both** — the Hanbalis: **"her maintenance is measured by the state of the two spouses
  together"** [11].

Ibn Hajar's judgement on the underlying standard: **"the stronger view from the evidence is that what is
obligatory is sufficiency"** [12] — enough, judged by custom, not a fixed ration.

### Why it falls on him

Ibn Hajar gives the reasoning the jurists actually used: **"and from the standpoint of meaning, she is
withheld from earning for the husband's right"** [13]. The maintenance is the counterpart to her being
committed to the household. It is a structural exchange, not charity, which is why it is enforceable and
why it does not lapse if he is displeased with her.

### Her own money

Nothing above touches it. If she works, earns, inherits or is given something, it is hers, and no part of
the obligation transfers to her because she has means. A wife who chooses to contribute is giving a gift,
not paying a share. This follows from [2] and [8] read together: the duty is named as his, and explicitly
not hers.

### If he does not pay

She is not required to absorb it. The Prophet ﷺ told Hind bint 'Utba, whose husband was tight-fisted:
**"take what suffices you and your child, in kindness"** [14] — permission to take from his property
directly, without his knowledge.

And the hadith al-Qurtubi cites puts the ultimate recourse in the wife's own voice: **"the woman says:
either you feed me or you divorce me"** [15]. Non-maintenance is grounds, in the schools that allow
separation for it.

### The order of spending

Worth stating because it is where sincere people go wrong. **"'And begin with those you support' — that
is, with those whose maintenance is obligatory upon you"** [16]. Al-Tabari extends it inward: **"beginning
in spending with one's dependants includes oneself, because a person's own self is among his
dependants"** [17]. And Abu Qilaba on the same hadith: **"he began with the dependants — and what man is
greater in reward than a man who spends on his dependants, keeping them from want, and God benefits them
through him"** [18].

Charity, the mosque fund, and a relative's need all come after the household. A man who is generous
abroad and thin at home has the order inverted.

### In practice

Most couples today pool income and neither wants the ledger. That is fine — the rules above are a floor,
not a ceiling, and a wife may contribute whatever she wishes. What they establish is what she is
*entitled* to if the marriage becomes contested: the household costs are his obligation, her earnings are
her property, and the children's expenses do not fall on her while he is alive and able.
