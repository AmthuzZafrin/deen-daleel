# Can I take my phone into the toilet if it has Quran on it?

## Answer

**The hadith this worry is built on is graded defective by the man who narrated it.** That is the first thing to say, because it changes the whole shape of the question.

Abu Dawud narrates: "when the Prophet entered the privy, he would remove his ring" [1] — the ring in question being the one engraved *Muhammad, Messenger of God*. It is the standard evidence for not carrying anything bearing God's name into a lavatory, and by extension for leaving a phone with a Qur'an app on it outside. Immediately after narrating it, Abu Dawud writes: "**this is a *munkar* hadith.** What is known is only from Ibn Jurayj, from Ziyad b. Sa'd, from al-Zuhri, from Anas: that the Prophet took a ring of silver and then discarded it. The error in it is from Hammam, and none narrated it but Hammam" [2].

So the report exists, it is in a canonical collection, and its compiler records in the same breath that he thinks a narrator confused it with a different report about a silver ring being thrown away. That does not make the practice wrong. It does mean the practice is not resting on what people usually think it rests on.

Two lines earlier, the same page carries 'A'isha's report — "the Messenger of God used to remember God at all his times" — and Abu Dawud's chapter heading immediately after it is *the ring which has God's remembrance on it, that one enters the privy with* [3]. He put the two side by side deliberately.

**The wider question — remembering God in an unclean place — is an old disagreement.** Al-Qurtubi: "the scholars have differed over this. 'Abdullah b. 'Amr, Ibn Sirin and al-Nakha'i permitted it; Ibn 'Abbas, 'Ata' and al-Sha'bi disliked it — **and the first is sounder, because of the generality of the verse and the hadith**" [4]. Al-Nakha'i's phrasing is the memorable one: "there is no harm in remembering God in the privy, for it ascends" [5].

Al-Qurtubi also states, fairly, why the other side held what it held: "the dislike of those who disliked it is either to keep the remembrance of God far from places one would rather it were not mentioned in — as with the dislike of reciting the Qur'an in the bathhouse — or out of regard for the noble scribes, that they not be lodged in a place of filth and impurity to write down what is uttered" [6]. Two intelligible motives, neither of them a text.

**Recitation is treated more strictly than remembrance, and even there the leading jurists split.** Ibn Qudama: "there is no harm in remembering God in the bathhouse, for the remembrance of God is good in every place unless a prohibition has come regarding it; and it is narrated that Abu Hurayra entered the bathhouse and said: *there is no god but God*" [7]. On recitation: "as for reciting the Qur'an, Ahmad said: this has not been made clear to me. Reciting the Qur'an in it was disliked by Abu Wa'il, al-Sha'bi, al-Hasan, Makhul and Qabisa b. Dhu'ayb; and al-Nakha'i and Malik did not dislike it" [8]. His own conclusion is worth quoting in full because of how he reaches it: "the basis of the first is that it is a place of uncovering, and things are done in it that would not be thought seemly elsewhere, so it was preferred to keep the Qur'an from it — **and the preferable view is the permissibility of recitation in it, because we know of no evidence in the matter that prevents reciting**" [9].

Ibn Hajar reports al-Subki the elder reaching the same place by a different route: "al-Subki the elder preferred that there be no dislike, and argued that recitation is called for, and increasing it is called for, and impurity is frequent — so if it were disliked, much good would be lost" [10]. Ibn Hajar then gives the qualifier that actually decides cases: "the ruling on recitation in the bathhouse: if the reciter is in a clean place and there is no uncovering of nakedness in it, it is not disliked; and otherwise, it is disliked" [11].

**That qualifier matters more than it looks, because the classical *hammam* is not a modern bathroom.** It was a public bathhouse — a place of undress and of other people. Ibn Hajar's test is about cleanliness and exposure, not about plumbing. Applied to a modern private bathroom, the two halves of it can come apart: a clean, dry, empty bathroom is not the place the jurists were describing, and a lavatory in use is.

**The strict position, stated at its strongest.** Ibn 'Abidin's *adab* of entering the privy includes: "**let nothing accompany him bearing a venerated name**, nor let him be bare-headed, nor with a cap that has nothing on it" [12] — and among the same list, "he does not return a greeting, nor answer a caller to prayer; and if he sneezes he praises God in his heart" [13]. That is a coherent and demanding etiquette, and it is where the modern caution about phones comes from. Note that it is *adab* literature setting out a recommended manner, not a chapter establishing a prohibition — and note too that even here, praising God does not stop; it moves into the heart.

**So, practically.**

If your phone is in your pocket, switched off or showing something else, there is nothing here that speaks to it. The classical concern is about *carrying a written name* — a ring engraved with one, a *mushaf* — and even that rests on a report Abu Dawud himself calls defective. A phone is not engraved with anything.

If you would rather leave it outside, that is squarely within the etiquette Ibn 'Abidin sets out, and nobody would fault it. It is a good instinct and it costs nothing.

Actively reciting or reading Qur'an while relieving yourself is the case the scholars who disliked it had in mind, and the courteous thing is not to. But even there the argument on the other side is made by Ibn Qudama and al-Subki, and the reason Ibn Qudama gives — "we know of no evidence in the matter that prevents reciting" — is one you can check.

And remembering God silently, anywhere, in any state, is not in dispute. Al-Qurtubi ends his discussion with the report of Moses asking how he could call on God in states he thought too low for it, and being told: *remember Me in every state.*
