# Can I use a credit card?

## Answer

**Using the card and clearing the balance in full each month is a different act from carrying a balance, and the two get different answers. The first is a loan repaid in the same amount, which is unobjectionable; the second is a stipulated increase on a loan, which is the plainest case there is.**

**The rule, stated with the qualification that matters.** Al-Nawawi, discussing a borrower who repays better than what he took: `وليس هو من قرض جر منفعة فإنه منهي عنه لأن المنهي عنه ما كان مشروطا في عقد القرض ومذهبنا أنه يستحب الزيادة في الأداء عما عليه` [1] — this is not the "loan that draws a benefit" which is forbidden, **because what is forbidden is what was stipulated in the loan contract**; and our position is that it is recommended to give more than what is owed when repaying.

That single sentence sorts the whole subject. A voluntary extra, unstipulated, is not merely permitted but praised. An increase written into the agreement is the forbidden thing.

Apply it:

- **Paying the full statement balance each month.** The issuer paid the merchant on your behalf; you repay exactly that. No increase is stipulated and none arises. This is a loan repaid in kind.
- **Carrying a balance and paying interest.** The increase is written into the contract in advance, applied automatically, and calculated as a percentage of what is outstanding. There is no reading of the material above under which that is anything but the thing named.

**Now the late fee, which is the part people miss — and where the classical position is genuinely instructive.**

Delay by someone able to pay is a wrong, and the tradition takes it seriously. Ibn Taymiyya: `وقال النبى مطل الغنى ظلم أخرجاه في الصحيحين وقال لي الواجد يحل عرضه وعقوبته رواه أهل السنن واللي هو المطل والواجد هو القادر` [2] — the delay of one who has the means is oppression, and **the stalling of one able to pay makes his honour and his punishment lawful**; `لي` is stalling and `واجد` is one who is able. His conclusion: `فثبت أن عقوبة المماطل مباحة` [3] — the punishment of the staller is permitted.

**But look at what the remedy is.** `من وجب عليه حق وهو قادر على أدائه وامتنع من أدائه فإنه يعاقب بالضرب والحبس مرة بعد مرة` [4] — he is punished with correction and imprisonment, repeatedly, until he pays. And al-Nawawi's contemporaries applied it through the court: `إذا امتنع مما يجب عليه من إظهار ماله والتمكين من توفية الناس جميع حقوقهم وكان ماله ظاهرا واحتيج إلى التوفية إلى فعل منه وامتنع منه وأصر على الحبس فإنه يعاقب بالضرب حتى يقوم بالواجب عليه في ذلك في مذهب عامة الفقهاء` [5] — coercion applied by the authority, in the view of the generality of the jurists.

The classical answer to non-payment is **compulsion by a court**, not an automatic increase in the sum owed. That distinction is the whole point. A creditor who responds to delay by adding to the debt is doing exactly what the pre-Islamic practice did — *do you pay, or do you increase?* — and that is the origin of the prohibition, described on the page about riba.

So a percentage-based late charge that grows the balance is the objectionable case. A flat administrative charge that is genuinely the cost of chasing the payment is argued differently by contemporary scholars — but be honest about which one your card actually applies, since most compute it as interest.

**Other features of a card.**

- **An annual fee** is a charge for a service, not an increase on a loan, and is generally treated as unproblematic. Where a "fee-free" card recoups its cost through the interest rate, that is the interest question again.
- **Cashback and rewards** are genuinely disputed. If the reward comes from the merchant's fee, it is not a benefit drawn from your loan. If it accrues in proportion to a balance you carry, it is closer to a return on the lending relationship. Ask about the specific programme.
- **The merchant's fee** is not your transaction and is not your question.

**What is not settled here.** These books have no concept of a revolving credit facility, of a card that costs nothing if you never borrow, or of the practical position of someone living where a card is effectively required to rent a car, book a hotel or build a credit record. That last is an argument from need, and it is one a scholar who knows your circumstances can weigh — but note that it argues for *holding* a card, which is the part that is already unproblematic if you clear it, rather than for paying interest on it.

**What to do.**

1. **Set up a full-balance direct debit**, so clearing it is not a monthly decision. This single step removes almost the entire question.
2. **Treat the credit limit as not being money you have.** The interest only ever arises because a balance was carried.
3. **If you are already carrying a balance**, clear it as a priority. It is a stipulated increase accruing daily, and the material on debt describes what that position does to a person.
4. **Check what the late charge actually is** — a percentage of the balance, or a fixed fee. They are not the same question.
5. **If interest has already been paid or charged to you**, the page on interest money covers what to do with what you receive; what you *paid* is a loss, not something to be recovered.

If you keep the card and clear it every month, you are not doing the thing the prohibition names. If you carry a balance, you are — and no arrangement of the paperwork changes that, because the increase is stipulated, which is the exact criterion al-Nawawi gives.
