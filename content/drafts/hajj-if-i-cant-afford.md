# What if I can't afford Hajj?

## Answer

Then it is not obligatory on you, and there is no sin in not going. That is the plain classical answer,
and it is worth hearing it stated as flatly as the jurists state it.

The obligation in the Qur'an is conditional — upon whoever can find a way to it — and the Prophet ﷺ was
asked what that condition means. Ibn 'Abbas reports the answer: **"the Messenger of God ﷺ said,
'provision and a mount' — meaning His words, 'whoever is able to find a way to it'"** [1]. Provision is
the cost of the journey; the mount is the transport. If you have neither, the condition has not been met.

Al-Jassas is careful about *why* the obligation lapses, and the distinction matters: **"the obligation
falls away from the poor person only because he does not find the means, not because he is not among
those who own"** [2]. You are not excluded from the address. You are simply not yet the person it
speaks to. The day your means change, so does your obligation.

### What actually counts as affording it

Ibn Qudama sets the bar in ordinary terms. Provision is **"what he needs for his going and his
returning: food, drink and clothing"** [3] — not a comfortable trip, but the real cost both ways. And
crucially, on the price: **"if it would deplete his wealth, it is not obligatory on him, as we said
regarding buying water for wudu"** [4]. The comparison is exact and reassuring — you are not asked to
strip yourself to perform an act of worship. He also states the floor directly: **"as for provision,
there is no avoiding it; if he finds no provision and is not able to earn it, the Hajj is not binding
upon him"** [5].

Transport is judged by distance: **"the condition of a mount applies specifically to the distant person
between whom and the House is the distance permitting shortening of prayer; as for the near one who is
able to walk, the presence of a mount is not taken into account in his case"** [6]. Most of us are the
distant one.

Ability is also not only about money. Al-Kasani's example: **"do you not see that if between him and
Mecca there is a swelling sea with no ship there, or an enemy standing between him and reaching the
House, the Hajj is not obligatory on him despite the presence of provision and mount"** [7]. Visas,
closed borders, illness, a dependant who cannot be left — these fall on the same principle.

### What not to do about it

Do not manufacture the ability. Al-Qurtubi records the ruling on the obvious shortcut: **"as for if he
can obtain provision by begging from people on the road, it is disliked for him to perform Hajj, because
he becomes a burden upon people"** [8]. The same reasoning is why scholars have generally discouraged
borrowing at interest, or leaving a family short, in order to go. An obligation God has lifted from you
is not one to buy back at the cost of a debt or someone else's welfare.

If the barrier is permanent physical incapacity rather than money, the classical route is to fund
someone to perform it for you — but Ibn Qudama closes even that door gently: **"if he does not find
wealth with which to appoint a substitute, then no Hajj is upon him — without disagreement"** [9].

### If you go anyway

Should the means appear — a gift, a paid-for place, a change in circumstances — the Hajj you perform is
your obligatory one, not a lesser substitute. Al-Jassas: **"once he reaches Mecca he has become
independent of provision and mount, and has come to stand in the position of all the others who arrived
with provision and mount"** [10], and so **"the poor person is not like that, because he owns the use of
his own self, and if he performs Hajj with it, it suffices him, because he has become one of the
able"** [11].

Until then, hold the intention. It is recorded from Umm Salama that the Prophet ﷺ said, **"the Hajj is
the jihad of every weak one"** [12] — the effort is measured against what you have, not against what
someone else has.
