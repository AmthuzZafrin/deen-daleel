# What does jihad actually mean?

## Answer

The word means *striving*, and the classical books use it across a range that is wider than the English coverage suggests and narrower, in the armed sense, than that coverage assumes. Both halves of that are worth setting out with the texts attached.

**The structure Ibn Hajar gives is the fullest one on this sheet.** He divides it first in two: "**the first is the inward struggle and the second is the outward struggle**" — and then sets out the inward one in stages: "and the struggle against the self has four ranks: bringing it to learn the matters of the religion, then bringing it to act by that, then bringing it to teach one who does not know, then calling to the oneness of God and fighting whoever opposes His religion and denies His favours" [1]. He adds a fifth front: "the strongest aid in the struggle against the self is the struggle against Satan, by repelling what he casts of doubt and uncertainty, then his beautifying of what has been forbidden of the prohibited things, then what leads, by excess in it, to falling into doubtful matters" [2].

Note what that structure does. Learning, practising and teaching are not preliminaries to the real thing; they are the first three ranks of it.

**The instruments are three, and only one of them is a weapon.** "**Strive against the idolaters with your wealth, your selves and your tongues**" [3].

**And the highest-ranked form in the corpus is speech, not fighting.** "**The best striving is a word of justice before a tyrannical ruler**" [4] — al-Tirmidhi carries it as "among the greatest striving is a word of justice before a tyrannical ruler", grading it *hasan gharib* by that route [5]. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr gives a second line for it, and the setting is striking: "a man asked the Messenger of God **at the pillar during the pilgrimage**: which striving is best? And the Messenger of God said: **the best striving is that a man speak a word of truth before a ruler who is unjust**" [6]. He follows it with: "**let none of you be prevented by fear of people from speaking the truth when he knows it**" [7].

**The armed sense is real, and the books rank it very high — honestly reported.** Ibn Qudama: "because striving is the giving of one's lifeblood and wealth, and its benefit extends to all the Muslims — their small and their great, their strong and their weak, their male and their female — and other things do not equal it in benefit and in danger, so they do not equal it in merit and reward" [8].

But it is not at the top of every list, and Ibn Qudama's own page shows that. He cites Ibn Mas'ud asking which deeds are best: "**he said: the prayer at its proper times. I said: then which? He said: then kindness to parents. I said: then which? He said: then striving in the way of God** — al-Tirmidhi said: this is a *hasan sahih* hadith" [9]. Third, behind punctual prayer and being good to your parents.

**And on obligation, al-Nawawi is explicit and it is the crux of the whole question:** "this is evidence that **striving is not an individual obligation but a collective one** — if enough people perform it for the need to be met, the burden falls from the rest, and if they all abandon it they all sin. Our companions said: **striving today is a collective obligation**, unless the disbelievers descend upon a land of the Muslims, in which case it becomes an individual obligation upon them; and if there is not sufficiency among the people of that land, it becomes obligatory on those adjacent to them to complete the sufficiency" [10].

That is a defensive framing with a threshold, discharged on behalf of everyone by whoever meets it — not a standing personal duty.

**The line most worth remembering comes from ranking people rather than acts.** A man asked who is the best of people: "he said: **a believer who strives with himself and his wealth in the way of God**. He said: then who, Messenger of God? He said: **then a believer in one of the mountain passes, who fears God and spares people his harm**" [11].

Second place, out of all humanity, goes to someone who has withdrawn from everyone and whose distinguishing quality is that nobody is harmed by him.

**What this sheet does not contain, and you should know it.** There is nothing here permitting a private individual to take up arms on their own account. The material treats striving as a collective obligation, organised, discharged under an authority, with a threshold and a scope — al-Nawawi's whole discussion presupposes that structure. The classical law of war has extensive rules on who may not be fought, none of which are on this worksheet. Anyone presenting this subject to you as a personal licence is not reading these books; and if that is the context you are asking in, the people to talk to are a scholar and, plainly, the law.

**So, in short.** *Jihad* is striving, and the corpus's own ordering puts learning your religion, acting on it, teaching it, resisting your own worst impulses, and speaking honestly to power *inside* the concept rather than as metaphors for it. The armed sense is genuine and highly ranked, and it is a collective obligation with a threshold, not something an individual takes upon himself. And when the Prophet was asked which striving is best, the answer recorded from two separate routes was a sentence spoken to a ruler who did not want to hear it.
