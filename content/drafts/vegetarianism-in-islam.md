# Can I be vegetarian as a Muslim?

## Answer

**Short answer: yes — not eating meat is a food preference and nothing forbids
it, but declaring meat *forbidden to you* is a different act, and there is a
verse about exactly that.** The line the sources draw is between what you choose
to eat and what you claim about what is lawful.

**The verse, and the case it came down about.** **"O ye who believe! Forbid not
the good things which Allah hath made lawful for you, and transgress not, Lo!
Allah loveth not transgressors."** [1]

Al-Tabari explains what the "good things" are and what the warning is against:
**"يعني بالطيبات: اللذيذات التي تشتهيها النفوس وتميل إليها القلوب، فتمنعوها
إياها، كالذي فعله القسيسون والرهبان، فحرموا على أنفسهم النساء والمطاعم الطيبة
والمشارب اللذيذة، وحبس في الصوامع بعضهم أنفسهم، وساح في الأرض بعضهم"** — *by "the
good things" is meant the pleasant things which souls desire and hearts incline
toward, that you should withhold them from yourselves — as the priests and monks
did, forbidding themselves women, good foods and pleasant drinks, some of them
confining themselves in cells and some wandering the earth* [2].

And the occasion is startlingly close to this question: **"كان أناس من أصحاب
النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم هموا بالخصاء وترك اللحم والنساء، فنزلت هذه الآية"** —
*some of the Companions of the Prophet ﷺ resolved on castration, and on giving up
meat and women, and this verse came down* [3].

**Companions gave up meat as a spiritual discipline, and the Qur'an came down
against it.** Al-Tabari names them elsewhere — 'Uthman ibn Maz'un, 'Ali, Ibn
Mas'ud, al-Miqdad, Salim the client of Abu Hudhayfa — describing how **"فجلسوا في
البيوت واعتزلوا النساء ولبسوا المسوح وحرموا طيبات الطعام واللباس، إلا ما أكل
ولبس أهل السياحة من بني إسرائيل، وهموا بالاختصاء"** — *they sat in their houses,
withdrew from women, wore sackcloth, forbade themselves the good things of food
and clothing except what the wandering ascetics of the Children of Israel ate and
wore, and resolved on castration* [4]. And al-Nasa'i records Ibn Mas'ud on the
same impulse: **"ألا نستخصي فنهانا عن ذلك"** — *shall we not have ourselves
castrated? And he forbade us that* [5].

The Prophet's ﷺ response to them is the whole answer: **"إن لأنفسكم حقا، وإن
لأعينكم حقا، صوموا وأفطروا، وصلوا وناموا، فليس منا من ترك سنتنا"** — *your souls
have a right over you, and your eyes have a right over you; fast and break your
fast, pray and sleep; whoever abandons our way is not of us* [6].

**So what exactly is being forbidden?** Ibn Taymiyya states the two things the
verse rules out: **"ثم نهى سبحانه عن تحريم ما أحل من الطيبات وعن الاعتداء في
تناولها وهو مجاوزة الحد"** — *then He forbade declaring forbidden what He has
made lawful of the good things, and forbade transgression in partaking of them,
which is exceeding the limit* [7].

**Two errors, in opposite directions**: treating the lawful as forbidden, and
going past the limit in indulging it. Vegetarianism sits near the first only if
you make it into a prohibition.

**And declaring a lawful thing forbidden is a legal act with consequences.**
*Bada'i al-Sana'i*: **"لان الأصل في تحريم الحلال أن يكون يمينا"** — *because the
basic rule regarding declaring the lawful forbidden is that it constitutes an
oath* [8] — with the expiation of a broken oath if you then eat it. Saying "meat
is haram for me" is not a neutral phrase.

**So, practically.**

- **Not eating meat is fine.** Taste, health, cost, squeamishness, concern about
  how animals are raised — none of these are the thing the verse addresses.
- **Do not say meat is forbidden to you**, or believe it. That is the act named,
  and in one school it is treated as an oath.
- **Do not present it as piety.** The Companions who did precisely that were
  corrected, and the correction is severe: *whoever abandons our way is not of
  us.*
- **Do not impose it on your household**, and do not make it awkward for others
  to eat what is lawful in front of you.
- **Eid is worth thinking about specifically**, since the sacrifice involves meat
  and has its own rulings. That is a separate question and worth asking about.
- **A concern about industrial animal farming is not the same as declaring meat
  unlawful.** The Qur'an's pairing of *halal* with *tayyib* leaves room for
  caring how food is produced — see *What does halal actually mean?*

**Where this stops.** These passages address abstention undertaken as
renunciation. They do not weigh contemporary arguments about animal welfare or
environmental harm, which the classical sources do not take up in this form. And
if you have already made a vow or a statement forbidding meat to yourself, the
expiation rules apply, and that is worth asking a scholar about rather than
guessing.
