# Do I need an Islamic will?

## Answer

**Write one, and do it now.** `ماحق امرئ مسلم له شئ يوصى فيه يبيت ليلتين الا ووصيته مكتوبة عنده` [1] — it is not right for a Muslim who has something to bequeath to pass two nights without his will written down beside him. Ibn 'Umar, who transmitted it, took it literally: `ما مرت علي ليلة منذ سمعت رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم قال ذلك إلا وعندي وصيتي` [2] — not a night has passed since I heard him say that without my will being with me.

**Obligatory or recommended? Both, depending on your situation — and this is the practical test.** Ibn al-'Arabi: `والصحيح نسخها وأنها مستحبة إلا فيما يجب على المكلف بيانه أو الخروج بأداء عنه` [3] — the correct view is that it is recommended, *except* as to anything he is obliged to disclose or to discharge. Al-Qurtubi puts the same line concretely: `فواجب مهم عليه أن يكتب وصيته ويخبر بما عليه فأما من لا دين عليه ولا وديعة عنده فليست بواجبة عليه إلا أن يشاء قال بن المنذر : وهذا حسن لأن الله فرض أداء الأمانات إلى أهلها ومن لا حق عليه ولا أمانة قبله فليس واجب عليه أن يوصي` [4] — it is obligatory on him to write his will and state what he owes; but one who has no debt and holds nothing in trust is not obliged, unless he wishes. Ibn al-Mundhir: this is sound, because God made the discharge of trusts obligatory.

So: **if you owe anyone money, or hold anyone's property, or anyone owes you and your heirs would not know it, writing it down is obligatory.** Debts, a mortgage, a loan to a friend, money held for someone, a business share, a pension nobody knows about. Otherwise, recommended: `الوصية غير واجبة وهي مستحبة` [5].

**The cap is one third, and the scene it comes from is worth reading whole.** Sa'd b. Abi Waqqas, seriously ill: `إن لي مالا كثيرا، وليس يرثني إلا ابنتي أفأوصي بمالي كله؟ قال: لا. قلت: فثلثي مالي؟ قال: لا. قلت: فالشطر؟ قال: لا. قلت: فالثلث؟ قال: الثلث والثلث كثير، إنك إن تذر ورثتك أغنياء خير من أن تذرهم عالة يتكففون الناس` [6] — I have much wealth and only a daughter to inherit; shall I bequeath all of it? No. Two thirds? No. Half? No. A third? A third, and a third is a lot. That you leave your heirs wealthy is better than that you leave them destitute, begging from people.

Note the direction of that. He was pushed *down*, repeatedly, from generosity toward strangers to provision for his own family. And the sentence that follows: `وإنك لن تنفق نفقة إلا أجرت فيها حتى اللقمة ترفعها إلى في امرأتك` [7] — you will not spend anything but that you are rewarded for it, even the morsel you lift to your wife's mouth. Providing for your household is not the unspiritual option.

The scholars read the cap as a maximum, not a target. Ibn Qudama: `والأولى أن لا يستوعب الثلث بالوصية وإن كان غنيا` [8] — better not to use up the whole third even if wealthy. Al-Quduri: `ويستحب أن يوصي الإنسان بدون الثلث` [9].

**No bequest to an heir.** `أن الإنسان إذا وصى لوارثه بوصية، فلم يجزها سائر الورثة، لم تصح. بغير خلاف بين العلماء. قال ابن المنذر، وابن عبد البر: أجمع أهل العلم على هذا` [10] — if a person bequeaths to one of his heirs and the other heirs do not permit it, it is invalid, without disagreement among the scholars; Ibn al-Mundhir and Ibn 'Abd al-Barr report consensus. The text: `إن الله قد أعطى كل ذي حق حقه، فلا وصية لوارث` [11] — God has given every rightful claimant his right, so there is no bequest to an heir.

Ibn Qudama's reasoning is about families, not arithmetic: `ولأن النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم منع من عطية بعض ولده، وتفضيل بعضهم على بعض في حال الصحة، وقوة الملك، وإمكان تلافي العدل بينهم بإعطاء الذي لم يعطه فيما بعد ذلك، لما فيه من إيقاع العداوة والحسد بينهم` [12] — he forbade favouring one child over another even in health, when the imbalance could still be corrected later, because of the enmity and envy it sows; so at death, when it cannot be corrected, all the more.

Heirs *can* waive it, but only at the right moment. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr: `وأجمعوا أنهم لو أجازوا ذلك وهو صحيح لم يلزمهم وأجمعوا أنهم إذا أجازوا ما أوصى به موروثهم لوارث منهم أو أجازوا وصيته بأكثر من الثلث بعد موته لزمهم ذلك` [13] — permission given while he was in health does not bind them; permission given after his death does.

**Who may be a beneficiary — and this is the one that matters most for converts.** `ويجوز أن يوصي المسلم للكافر والكافر للمسلم` [14] — a Muslim may bequeath to a non-Muslim, and a non-Muslim to a Muslim. Non-Muslim parents do not inherit under the fixed shares, but a bequest to them is valid, and the third is available for exactly this. It is the standard mechanism, not a loophole. The exception in the same sentence: `ولا للقاتل` — not to the one who killed him.

**What a will cannot contain.** Ibn 'Abd al-Barr: `فإن أوصى بما لا يجوز مثل أن يوصى بخمر أو خنزير أو بشيء من المعاصي فهذا يجوز تبديله ولا يجوز إمضاؤه` [15] — a bequest of something impermissible may be altered and may not be carried out. An instruction the law forbids does not bind the executor.

**You can change it whenever you like.** `ويجوز للموصي الرجوع عن الوصية فإن صرح بالرجوع أو فعل ما يدل على الرجوع كان رجوعا ومن جحد الوصية لم يكن رجوعا` [16] — the testator may revoke; stating the revocation, or doing something that indicates it, counts as revocation; merely denying the will does not.

**Two specifics worth naming.** An outstanding obligatory hajj: `ومن أوصى بحجة الإسلام أحجوا عنه رجلا من بلده يحج عنه راكبا فإن لم تبلغ الوصية النفقة أحجوا عنه من حيث تبلغ` [17] — someone is sent from his town to perform it for him, and if the bequest does not cover the cost, from as far as it reaches. And the executor: `ومن أوصى إلى عبد أو كافر أو فاسق أخرجهم القاضي من الوصية` [18] — a judge removes an unsuitable executor. Naming someone competent and trustworthy is part of the job.

**So, concretely, an Islamic will contains:** what you owe and to whom; what is owed to you; what you hold in trust for others; your funeral costs; any outstanding hajj or expiations; bequests of up to a third — to anyone who is not an heir, which is how a convert provides for non-Muslim parents; a named executor; and a statement that the residue is divided according to the fixed shares.

**What this does not settle, and it is a large gap.** None of this tells you how to make a will that is valid where you live. In most non-Muslim jurisdictions a will must satisfy local formalities to have any effect at all, and some rules — forced heirship, spousal entitlements, intestacy defaults — can override or interact with what you have written. Whether a life insurance payout, a pension with a nominated beneficiary, or jointly-owned property even forms part of the estate is a question of local law that these books cannot reach. Get a will drafted by someone who knows both, and treat the material above as the instructions you hand them, not as the document. And the fixed shares themselves — who gets what from the remaining two thirds — are a separate question with their own detail.
