# Why do so many Muslims behave badly?

## Answer

**Two questions are tangled here, and they come apart cleanly.** Does the behaviour of Muslims tell you anything about Islam? And what are you supposed to do about it? The sources answer both, and the second answer is more concrete than most people expect.

**The tradition grades the religion; it does not treat "Muslim" as a certificate of character.** Ibn Taymiyya sets out the structure: `جعل النبي الدين ثلاث درجات أعلاها الإحسان وأوسطها الإيمان ويليه الإسلام فكل محسن مؤمن وكل مؤمن مسلم وليس كل مؤمن محسنا ولا كل مسلم مؤمنا` [1] — the Prophet made the religion three degrees, the highest *ihsan*, the middle *iman*, then *islam*; every *muhsin* is a believer and every believer is a Muslim, but not every believer is a *muhsin*, nor every Muslim a believer. Being Muslim is the floor of a graded thing. Nothing in the structure predicts that the people standing on the floor will behave well.

**And the definitions given are behavioural, not doctrinal.** `المسلم من سلم المسلمون من لسانه ويده` [2] — the Muslim is the one from whose tongue and hand the Muslims are safe — `والمؤمن من أمنه الناس على دمائهم وأموالهم` [3], and the believer is the one people trust with their lives and their property. Ibn Taymiyya draws out the difference, which is worth pausing on: `ومعلوم أن السلامة من ظلم الانسان غير كونه مأمونا على الدم والمال فإن هذا أعلى والمأمون يسلم الناس من ظلمه وليس من سلموا من ظلمه يكون مأمونا عندهم` [4] — being safe from a man's wrongdoing is not the same as being trusted by him with life and wealth; the second is higher. Everyone the trusted man deals with is safe from him, but not everyone who is safe from a man is willing to trust him. So even at the level of a definition, the religion is describing degrees of a person's reliability, not a badge.

Al-Nawawi adds the qualification that answers your question directly. `من سلم المسلمون من لسانه ويده` means, he says, `من لم يؤذ مسلما بقول ولا فعل وخص اليد بالذكر لأن معظم الافعال بها` [5] — one who harms no Muslim in word or deed, the hand singled out because most acts are done with it. And then: `قالوا معناه المسلم الكامل وليس المراد نفى أصل الاسلام عن من لم يكن بهذه الصفة بل هذا كما يقال العلم ما نفع` [6] — the meaning is *the complete Muslim*; it is not meant to deny the basic fact of Islam to someone who lacks that quality, in the way one says "knowledge is what benefits." The tradition already has a settled place for the person you are describing: falling short, and still inside. That is precisely why you cannot read the religion off them. If Islam only admitted people who lived it well, the hadith would not need the qualification.

**But nothing is waived.** `المفلس من أمتي من يأتي يوم القيامة بصلاته وصيامه وزكاته ويأتي قد شتم هذا وقذف هذا` [7] — the bankrupt of my community is the one who comes on the Day of Judgement with his prayer, his fasting and his zakat, and comes having insulted this one, slandered that one — `وأكل مال هذا وسفك دم هذا وضرب هذا، فيقعد فيقتص هذا من حسناته وهذا من حسناته، فإن فنيت حسناته قبل أن يقتص ما عليه من الخطايا أخذ من خطاياهم، فطرح عليه ثم طرح في النار` [8]: consumed this one's wealth, shed this one's blood, struck this one; so he is seated, and each takes from his good deeds, and when the good deeds run out before the debt does, their sins are taken and heaped on him, and he is thrown into the Fire. The observant, praying, fasting, zakat-paying man is the subject of that hadith, not an exception to it.

Ibn Hajar notes the obvious objection and answers it: `ولا تعارض بين هذا وبين قوله تعالى ولا تزر وازرة وزر أخرى لأنه إنما يعاقب بسبب فعله وظلمه` [9] — this does not conflict with "no bearer bears another's burden", because he is punished on account of his own act and his own wrongdoing. And on the scope of what counts as a *mazlima* owed to someone, he says it takes in `المال بأصنافه والجراحات حتى اللطمة ونحوها` [10] — property of every kind, and injuries, down to a slap.

**Religious standing confers nothing.** Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, discussing who is fit to lead, cites God's answer to Ibrahim about his descendants: `وأما أهل الجور والفسق والظلم فليسوا له بأهل ألا ترى إلى قول الله عز وجل لإبرهيم عليه السلام قال * (إني جاعلك للناس إماما قال ومن ذريتي قال لا ينال عهدي الظالمين` [11] — the people of injustice, corruption and oppression are not its people; My covenant does not reach the wrongdoers. And on the ideal: `فقالوا هذا هو الاختيار أن يكون الإمام فاضلا عدلا محسنا` [12] — the choice is that the leader be excellent, just and doing good. (In context this is a discussion of political leadership, and the same passage goes on to argue that enduring an unjust ruler is preferable to rebelling against him, which is a separate question and not the one asked here.) The point that carries across is that holding a religious position is not evidence of anything, and the sources say so.

**Now the part that matters practically — and where "don't expose other Muslims" stops.** Al-Nawawi comments on the hadith about relieving distress and concealing faults: `في هذا فضل إعانة المسلم وتفريج الكرب عنه وستر زلاته` [13] — there is merit in helping a Muslim, relieving his distress and covering his lapses. Then he places two limits on it, and they are the operative rulings.

First, on who may be covered for: `فالمراد به الستر على ذوي الهيئات ونحوهم ممن ليس هو معروفا بالأذى والفساد فأما المعروف بذلك فيستحب أن لا يستر عليه بل ترفع قضيته إلى ولي الأمر إن لم يخف من ذلك مفسدة لان الستر على هذا يطمعه في الايذاء والفساد وانتهاك الحرمات وجسارة غيره على مثل فعله` [14] — what is meant is covering for people of standing and the like who are *not known for harm and corruption*; as for one who is known for it, it is recommended **not** to cover for him, but rather to raise his case to the authorities, so long as no greater harm follows — because covering for such a person makes him greedy for more harm and corruption and violation, and emboldens others to do the same.

Second, on timing: `أما معصية رآه عليها وهو بعد متلبس بها فتجب المبادرة بانكارها عليه ومنعه منها على من قدر على ذلك ولا يحل تأخيرها فان عجز لزمه رفعها إلى ولي الأمر` [15] — a sin one sees him still engaged in must be objected to and stopped immediately by whoever is able, and delaying is not lawful; if he cannot stop it, he is obliged to raise it to the authorities. Concealment applies to a sin that happened and is finished, not to one in progress.

And al-Nawawi adds a third category: `وأما جرح الرواة والشهود والأمناء على الصدقات والأوقاف والأيتام ونحوهم فيجب` [16] — impugning narrators, witnesses, and those entrusted with charitable funds, endowments and orphans' property is *obligatory*. Warning people about someone who holds a trust is not backbiting in this tradition. It is a duty.

So if you are being told that speaking about a Muslim who is harming people is un-Islamic, that is not what these books say. They say the opposite, in this much detail, and they say it for exactly the reason you would give: silence emboldens him.

**One more observation, about the loudest voices.** `ما ضل قوم بعد هدى كانوا عليه إلا أوتوا الجدل` [17] — no people went astray after guidance they had held except that they were given to argument.

**What this does not settle.** It does not tell you whether to stay in a particular mosque or community, which depends on facts about that place. It does not substitute for reporting a crime to the police or a safeguarding authority — the "raise it to the authorities" in al-Nawawi is a general principle, not advice about a modern legal system, and the corpus has nothing to say about how those work. And it does not claim that Muslims behave better on average; the sources make no such claim, and the *muflis* hadith assumes the reverse. What the tradition offers is a set of criteria you can hold people to, including the ones who taught you, and a clear statement that failing them costs the person everything and costs the religion nothing.
