# If everything is decreed, do I have free will?

## Answer

**Short answer: the sources answer this by refusing the question's framing.**
Asked exactly what you are asking — if it is already written, why act? — the
Prophet ﷺ did not resolve the philosophy. He said: act. And the classical
commentators then explain why that is an answer rather than an evasion.

**The exchange itself.** Al-Nawawi records it: **"فقيل أفلا نتكل على كتابنا وما
سبق لنا من القدر فقال اعملوا فكل ميسر لما خلق له"** — *it was said: shall we not
then rely on what is written for us and what has preceded of the decree? He said:
act, for each is made easy for what he was created for* [1].

Al-Nawawi's gloss makes the logic explicit: **"وكمالا يحسن ترك الصلاة والصوم
والذكر اتكالا على القدر فكذا الدعاء بالنجاة من النار"** — *just as it is not
proper to abandon prayer, fasting and remembrance in reliance on the decree, so
too with supplication for deliverance from the Fire* [2]. The argument "it is
already decided, so why bother" would abolish every action, including the ones
nobody actually abandons. Nobody stops eating on the grounds that their
sustenance is written.

**And there is a positive instruction attached.** **"احرص على ما ينفعك واستعن
بالله ولا تعجز"** — *strive for what benefits you, seek Allah's help, and do not
fall short* [3]. Al-Nawawi: **"ومعناه احرص على طاعة الله تعالى والرغبة فيما عنده
واطلب الإعانة من الله تعالى على ذلك ولا تعجز ولا تكسل"** — *strive for obedience
to Allah and for what is with Him, seek His help in that, and do not fall short
or grow lazy* [4]. Three things in one sentence: effort, reliance, and no excuse
for slackness. That is the practical shape of belief in qadar.

**What is written, and when.** **"كتب الله مقادير الخلائق قبل أن يخلق السماوات
والأرض بخمسين الف سنة وعرشه على الماء"** — *Allah wrote the measures of creation
fifty thousand years before He created the heavens and the earth, and His Throne
was upon the water* [5]. Al-Bukhari places alongside it: **"إن الله كتب كتابا قبل
أن يخلق الخلق ان رحمتي سبقت غضبي"** — *Allah wrote a writing before He created
creation: My mercy has outstripped My wrath* [6]. What was written first, on that
report, is mercy.

**Now the hard part: if it is written, why am I blamed?** Al-Nawawi puts the
objection in its sharpest form — that a sinner might say "this sin was decreed
for me", and be telling the truth — and answers it: **"فالجواب أن هذا العاصي باق
في دار التكليف جار عليه أحكام المكلفين من العقوبة واللوم والتوبيخ وغيرها وفي
لومه وعقوبته زجر له ولغيره عن مثل هذا الفعل"** — *the answer is that this sinner
remains in the abode of moral responsibility; the rulings that apply to
responsible persons apply to him — punishment, blame, reproach and the rest — and
in blaming and punishing him there is a restraint for him and for others from
acting likewise* [7].

Read what that does. It does not claim the decree is unreal. It says the decree
is not the register you are living in. You are inside a situation where blame,
effort and consequence operate, and reasoning about the decree from the outside
does not remove you from it.

**The asymmetry that gives the game away.** Ibn Taymiyya: **"فالمؤمن يصبر على
المصائب ويستغفر من الذنوب والمعائب والجاهل الظالم يحتج بالقدر على ذنوبه
وسيئاته"** — *the believer bears misfortunes with patience and seeks forgiveness
for sins and faults, while the ignorant wrongdoer uses the decree as an argument
for his sins and misdeeds* [8]. And the observation that exposes it:
**"ولا يذكر القدر عند ما ييسره الله له من الخير فعكس القضية"** — *he does not
mention the decree when Allah makes some good easy for him — so he has inverted
the matter* [9].

That is the test. Nobody who does something good says "the decree made me". The
appeal to qadar arrives only after wrongdoing, and only to deflect — which tells
you it is a move, not a belief.

**What qadar is actually for.** Ibn Taymiyya: **"ومن الإيمان بالقدر أن يعلم
العبد أن ما أصابه لم يكن ليخطئه وما أخطأه لم يكن ليصيبه"** — *part of believing
in the decree is that a servant knows what befell him was never going to miss
him, and what missed him was never going to befall him* [10]. On this reading the
doctrine's work is done *after* the event: it is what stops you from being
destroyed by what you could not control.

Hence the instruction about hindsight: **"وإن أصابك شئ فلا تقل لو أني فعلت كان
كذا وكذا ولكن قل قدر الله وما شاء فعل"** — *if something befalls you, do not say
"if only I had done such and such"; say rather: Allah decreed, and what He willed
He did* [11].

**So, the shape of it.** Before you act: strive, seek help, do not fall short
[3][4]. After something happens: accept it and do not torment yourself with *if
only* [11]. What is forbidden is using the decree in the one place it does not
belong — as a licence beforehand or an excuse afterwards [8][9].

**On the language above:** "qadar" is the divine decree or measuring-out; "qada'"
is its execution; "dar al-taklif" is the abode of moral responsibility, the term
at [7]; "al-lawh al-mahfuz" is the preserved tablet in which the writing at [5] is
recorded.

**A note on scope, and an honest limit.** How divine decree and human
responsibility fit together was the most divisive question in Islamic theology,
and the passages quoted here are taking positions within that argument — Ibn
Taymiyya's discussion at [8] runs on to name the schools that erred in each
direction. The tradition's settled advice is that the doctrine is to be believed
and acted upon rather than solved; if the question is genuinely troubling you
rather than merely interesting you, that is worth taking to someone in person.

### Passage 7 — Qur'an 2:177

- kind: quran · source: The Meaning of the Glorious Koran · chunk: 6915

```
Qur'an 2:177 — Surah Al-Baqara, verse 177 of chapter 2
```
