# How do I greet other Muslims?

## Answer

*As-salamu 'alaykum* — peace be upon you. The reply is *wa 'alaykum as-salam*. That is the whole thing, and you can start using it today. What follows is the detail, because a few points genuinely matter and one of them people get wrong every time.

**The ruling, which is not what most people assume.**

Al-Nawawi: "know that initiating the greeting is a *sunna* and returning it is obligatory. If those greeting are a group, it is a communal *sunna* for them — if some of them greet, the *sunna* of greeting is attained for all of them. If the one greeted is a single person, returning it is specifically binding on him; and if they are a group, returning is a communal obligation for them, so if one of them returns it, the burden falls away from the rest" [1]. He notes the agreement: "Ibn 'Abd al-Barr and others transmitted the consensus of the Muslims that initiating the greeting is a *sunna* and that returning it is obligatory" [2].

So: greeting first is recommended and rewarded. Answering is a duty. Al-Nawawi elsewhere spells out the consequence: "returning the greeting is obligatory by consensus… and if none of them answers him, they all sin" [3]. Ignoring someone's salam is not a lapse of manners; it is leaving an obligation.

**The words, and the one common mistake.**

The Qur'an sets the standard: "When ye are greeted with a greeting, greet ye with a better than it or return it" (4:86) [4].

Al-Nawawi: "the least of the greeting is that he says *as-salamu 'alaykum*; and if the one greeted is one person, the least is *as-salamu 'alayk* — but it is better to say *as-salamu 'alaykum*, so that it takes in him and his angels. More complete than that is to add *wa rahmatullah*, and again *wa barakatuh*" [2].

There is a hadith with the arithmetic. A man came and said *as-salamu 'alaykum*; the Prophet ﷺ returned it and said "ten." Another came and said *as-salamu 'alaykum wa rahmatullah*; he returned it and said "twenty." Another added *wa barakatuh*; he returned it and said "thirty" [5]. Three added words, three times the reward.

Al-Qurtubi explains what "better than it" means in practice: "the better reply is that he adds — so he says *'alayk as-salam wa rahmatullah* to one who said *salamun 'alayk*; and if he said *salamun 'alayk wa rahmatullah*, you add in your reply *wa barakatuh*. And this is the limit; there is no more" [6]. Match, then add one step. At *wa barakatuh* you have reached the ceiling.

**The mistake:** do not open with *'alaykum as-salam*. Al-Nawawi: "it is disliked for the one initiating to say *'alaykum as-salam*… and it is authentically established that the Prophet ﷺ said: Do not say *'alayk as-salam*, for *'alayk as-salam* is the greeting of the dead" [7]. Reversed word order is for the *reply*, not the opening. Many people say it as an opener out of habit; correct it in yourself and leave others alone about it.

**Say it out loud.**

Ibn Hajar: "it was inferred from the command to spread the greeting that a silent greeting does not suffice — audibility is required, and the minimum is that it be heard, both in the initiation and in the reply; and a gesture with the hand and the like does not suffice" [8].

Al-Nawawi adds the calibration, including a case people rarely think about: raise your voice "so that the one greeted and the one replied to hear it with certain hearing, and he does not raise it beyond that. If he doubts their hearing, he raises it and makes sure. And if he greets waking people among whom there are sleepers, he lowers his voice such that the waking hear him and the sleepers do not wake" [9]. That last detail is from the Prophet's ﷺ own practice.

Two exceptions on the gesture rule: someone deaf — "if he greets a deaf person, he pronounces the words, since he is able to, and he gestures with the hand so that understanding is attained" [10] — and someone at a distance or in prayer, where returning by gesture is established.

The reply should be prompt: al-Nawawi's school required "that the reply be connected to the greeting, with the connection required between offer and acceptance in contracts" [11].

**Who greets first.**

The transmitted order is: the rider greets the walker, the walker greets the seated, the smaller group greets the larger, the younger greets the older. Ibn Hajar records the reasons offered — the rider is at risk of self-importance if he took both merits; the seated person cannot practicably track everyone passing.

But do not turn it into a rule of precedence. Al-Mazari: "these correspondences are not to be objected to by particulars that differ from them, because they were not set up as binding causes… so that even if the walker began and greeted the rider it would not be forbidden, since he is complying with the command to make the greeting manifest and to spread it — except that observing what is established in the hadith is better" [12].

And the real ordering is different anyway. Abu Umama: "It was said: Messenger of Allah, when two men meet, which of them begins with the greeting? He said: The one of them nearer to Allah. This is a *hasan* hadith" [13]. Being first to greet is a merit to compete for, not a duty to dodge.

**Why it matters more than politeness.**

"You will not enter Paradise until you believe, and you will not believe until you love one another. Shall I not direct you to something which, if you do it, you will love one another? Spread the greeting among yourselves" [14]. Ibn al-'Arabi: "among the benefits of spreading the greeting is the attainment of love between those who exchange peace — and that is because of the unity of word in it, so that the benefit becomes general" [15].

Ibn 'Umar took this literally: "I used to go out to the market with no need except that I greet and be greeted" [16].

Add the handshake. Al-Bara' b. 'Azib reported: "When two Muslims meet and shake hands, and praise Allah and seek His forgiveness, they are forgiven" [17].

**When not to greet.**

Al-Nawawi lists the exceptions: "one occupied with eating, drinking, or intimacy, or who is in the lavatory or the bathhouse, or asleep, or drowsy, or praying, or giving the adhan — for as long as he is engaged in any of what is mentioned" [18]. Al-Qurtubi cites the case that settled it: "one should not greet someone relieving himself, and if he does, it is not binding on him to return it. A man came upon the Prophet ﷺ in such a state and he said to him: When you find me or see me in this state, do not greet me — for if you greet me I will not return it to you" [19].

Ibn 'Abidin narrows the eating case sensibly: the exclusion "is specific to the state of putting the morsel in the mouth and chewing; as for before and after, it is not disliked, because there is no inability" [20]. So don't interrupt a mouthful. Otherwise greet.

**Greeting by message.**

Sending salam through someone is established and carries a duty: "it is recommended to send the greeting to one who is absent, and there are sound hadiths on it; and it is binding on the messenger to convey it, because it is a trust" [21].

Written greetings work the same way. Ibn 'Abidin quotes al-Nawawi: "if a person brings him a greeting from another person — that is, on a page — returning it immediately is obligatory; and it is recommended that he reply to the one conveying it as well" [22]. He backs it with verse: "When the friend writes to the friend, replying is a due obligation. When brothers are prevented from meeting, there is no bond finer than a letter" [23].

Which answers the modern form of the question directly: a salam in a message is a real salam, and it carries the real obligation to reply.

**Greeting non-Muslims — a genuine disagreement, and an important qualification.**

If you are a revert with non-Muslim family, colleagues and neighbours, read this part carefully, because the blunt version circulating is not the whole of it.

The restricting hadith is explicit: "Do not initiate the greeting to the Jews and Christians" [24]. But Ibn Hajar records scholars who permitted initiating anyway — Ibn 'Uyayna arguing from *Allah does not forbid you from those who did not fight you in religion*, and this exchange: "he asked 'Umar b. 'Abd al-'Aziz about initiating the greeting to the people of covenant, and he said: We return it to them and do not initiate to them. 'Awn said: I said to him, then what do you say yourself? He said: I see no harm in our initiating to them" [25].

Al-Tabari's reconciliation is the one to hold on to. There is no contradiction between the hadith of Usama, where the Prophet ﷺ greeted a mixed gathering, and the prohibition report, because the general is restricted by the specific: what is excluded from the prohibition is "where the initiating is for no reason and no need arising from a right of companionship, or neighbourliness, or requital, or the like" [26].

So the prohibition, on this reading, targets an unmotivated religious greeting — not a greeting owed to your mother, your neighbour, or a colleague who greeted you first.

On *returning* a greeting from a non-Muslim, al-Qurtubi records the split: "Ibn 'Abbas, al-Sha'bi and Qatada held it obligatory, as returning it to Muslims is, holding to the generality of the verse and to the command to return it to them in the sound Sunna; and Malik held that it is not obligatory — and if you do return it, say *'alayk*" [27].

**In short.** Say *as-salamu 'alaykum* out loud, add *wa rahmatullah* when you can, reply promptly and at least in kind, never open with the reversed form, and answer every salam that reaches you — spoken, relayed or typed. Then shake hands.

If you are new to this and the Arabic feels awkward in your mouth: say it anyway. It is four syllables, everyone will understand you, and the first person you say it to will be glad you did.
