# Do I pay zakat on my pension?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

> Reviewer note: Turns on the scheme's structure and whether the money is accessible — facts about a modern financial product. Explain what it turns on; refer.


## Answer

I can give you the principle the sources argue from, but not the ruling — and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. A pension is a modern instrument. None of the works in this collection describe anything like it, so what follows is the reasoning you would need, not a verdict.

**Why this is genuinely unsettled.** The classical texts pull in two directions, and a pension sits between them.

**The first line: wealth you cannot reach.** The jurists knew the case of money that is legally yours but practically out of your hands, and they generally suspended zakat on it. Ibn Qudama, discussing a man whose disposal of his own property has been restrained by a judge: **the judge restrained him after the zakat became due, and he was not able to pay it out, because his power to dispose of his wealth had been cut off** [1]. He raises the further possibility that the obligation lapses entirely — **it is possible that it drops away if he was restrained before he was able to pay it, just as if his wealth had been destroyed** [2]. Ibn Hajar states the same principle: **wealth, if it is destroyed before one is able to pay, the zakat drops away** [3]. Ibn Qudama also records the settled position that a debt weighing on you removes the obligation on hidden wealth: **debt prevents zakat becoming obligatory on hidden wealth** [4].

**The second line: wealth that will come to you.** Malik treats a long-absent debt not as gone but as deferred, owing a single payment when it lands: **the proof that a debt which is absent for years and then collected owes only a single zakat is that trade goods stay with a man for years and then he sells them, and he owes only a single zakat on their price** [5]. He adds a point that matters here — **he pays the zakat of each thing out of itself, and does not pay the zakat of one thing out of another** [6]. You are not asked to fund zakat on a locked pot out of money you can actually spend.

**Which line applies depends on facts the sources cannot supply.** A workplace pot you can see valued on a statement but cannot legally draw until a set age is not the same as a promise to be paid a monthly sum for life, and neither is the same as a fund you *can* access at a penalty. The relevant questions are: do you own an identified sum now, or a promise? Can you reach it at all? Are your employer's contributions yours before vesting?

**What I would ask a scholar, with your scheme's paperwork in hand:**

1. Is this a defined pot with a stated value, or a defined benefit — a promise of income?
2. If you can access it early at a penalty, does that count as being able to reach it?
3. Are unvested employer contributions yours yet?
4. If zakat is owed only when you draw it, is one payment due on the whole, or payments for the years it was locked?

Contemporary scholars do reach answers on this — the two most common are zakat annually on the accessible portion, and a single payment when the money is received. Both are built from the reasoning above. But the choice between them turns on the terms of your particular scheme, and that is a question for someone who can read those terms with you.
