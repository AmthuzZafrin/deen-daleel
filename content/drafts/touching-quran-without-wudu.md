# Can I touch the Quran without wudu?

## Answer

**The mainstream answer is no, and it is close to unanimous** — but the reason it is usually given for is not the reason the jurists actually gave, and that turns out to matter for several related questions.

Ibn 'Abd al-Barr reports the position and its holders: "the jurists of the great cities, in Madina, Iraq and Syria, did not differ that the *mushaf* is not touched except by one who is pure, in a state of *wudu* — and it is the position of Malik, al-Shafi'i, Abu Hanifa, al-Thawri, al-Awza'i, Ahmad b. Hanbal, Ishaq b. Rahawayh, Abu Thawr and Abu 'Ubayd" [1].

**The evidence is a letter, not the verse.** The verse everyone quotes is *which none toucheth save the purified* [2]. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr's evidence is instead the Prophet's letter to the people of Yemen: "in the letter the Messenger of God wrote to the people of Yemen concerning the practices, the shares of inheritance and the blood-monies: **that none shall touch the Qur'an but one who is pure** — and the evidence for the soundness of the letter of 'Amr b. Hazm is the generality of the scholars' receiving it with acceptance" [3]. He closes on the same footing: "the best of what has been said in this chapter is what the majority of the scholars hold, of complying with what is in the Messenger of God's letter to 'Amr b. Hazm" [4].

Ishaq b. Rahawayh states the distinction outright, and it is worth reading twice: "no one should read in the *mushaf* unless he is in a state of *wudu* — **and that is not because of God's saying *none toucheth save the purified*, but because of the Messenger of God's saying: none shall touch the Qur'an but one who is pure**" [5].

**Why he was careful to say that.** Because the verse, read on its own, is genuinely contested. Al-Qurtubi surveys it: "they differed over the meaning of *none toucheth it* — whether it is literal, touching with a limb, or a meaning; and likewise they differed over *the purified*, who they are. Anas and Sa'id b. Jubayr said: none touches that Book except those purified of sins, and they are the angels" [6]. Abu al-'Aliya, Ibn Zayd and al-Kalbi are reported the same way, and Malik's own preferred reading takes the verse as parallel to *in honoured scrolls, exalted, purified, by the hands of scribes noble and righteous*. Al-Qurtubi does report the other reading — "and it is said the Book intended is the *mushaf* which is in our hands, and this is the more apparent" [7] — but he reports it as one view among several. Qatada takes it further in the other direction: "with God none touches it except the purified; but as for in this world, the Magian, the impure and the hypocrite touch it" [8].

Al-Jassas sets out exactly how the two possibilities resolve: "if the wording is taken on the reality of a statement, the more likely is that what is meant is the Qur'an that is with God and *the purified* are the angels; and if it is taken as a prohibition, though in the form of a statement, it is general concerning us — **and this is preferable**, because of what is narrated from the Prophet in mutually corroborating reports, that he wrote in his letter to 'Amr b. Hazm: *and none shall touch the Qur'an but one who is pure*; so his prohibition must be by the verse, since it carries another possibility" [9]. The hadith is what settles which reading of the verse to adopt — not the other way round.

**The dissent, named.** Ibn 'Abd al-Barr does not hide it: "and to their view went Dawud b. 'Ali, who said there is no harm in the person in a state of major impurity, or the menstruating woman, touching the *mushaf* and the dinars and dirhams that have God's remembrance on them. He said the meaning of *none toucheth it save the purified* is: they are the angels. He said: **had that been a prohibition, He would have said *let none touch it***. And he argued also from the Prophet's words: *the believer is not impure*" [10]. Two of Dawud's arguments are grammatical and one is theological, and Ibn 'Abd al-Barr answers the grammatical one on its own terms: "a prohibition may come in the form of a statement while its meaning is prohibition, and that is found in the Book of God frequently — as in His saying: *the adulterer shall not marry save an adulteress or an idolatress*" [11].

**Now the practical questions, where the schools actually differ.**

*Touching through a barrier, or carrying by a strap or cover.* Al-Qurtubi states it cleanly: "Malik said: one who is not pure does not carry it by a strap nor on a cushion. Abu Hanifa said: there is no harm in that — and he did not forbid carrying it by a strap or touching it with a barrier between" [12]. So on the Hanafi position, a cover or a cloth resolves it; on the Maliki position it does not.

*Carrying it inside something else.* Even Malik allows this: "Malik said he does not carry it by its strap nor on a cushion unless he is pure. He said: and there is no harm in one who is not in a state of *wudu* carrying it in a chest, a saddlebag or a sack" [13]. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr explains the reasoning, which is about intention: "Malik permitted one not in *wudu* to carry the *mushaf* in the chest and the sack only because the aim was not to carry the *mushaf*; he aimed only to carry the chest and whatever was in it, of a *mushaf* and other things" [14]. Carrying a bag that has one in it is not the act being regulated.

*Children.* Al-Qurtubi reports two positions and the argument that decides it in practice: "concerning children touching it there are two views: the first, prevention, by analogy with the adult; the second, permission — **because if he were prevented, the Qur'an would not be memorised, since learning it is in childhood**" [15].

*Menstruation.* Not uniform either. Ibn 'Abd al-Barr: "it is narrated from 'Ata' that he said there is no harm in a menstruating woman carrying the *mushaf* by its strap" [16].

**And the point most likely to be useful today**, because it is what people actually need at the moment they ask: **reciting is not touching.** Al-Jassas reports Salman's practice as the illustration of the verse: "it is narrated from Salman that he said *none touches the Qur'an but the purified* — and he recited the Qur'an and did not touch the *mushaf*, when he was not in a state of *wudu*" [17]. Nothing in the majority position stops you reciting from memory, or listening, or reading aloud. What it regulates is the physical handling of the codex.

**In short.** The great majority say don't touch the *mushaf* without *wudu*; that is the safe practice and it is what the schools teach. The basis is a hadith rather than the verse, which the jurists themselves were careful about. A minority — Dawud and the Zahiris — held otherwise on an argument that is not silly. And within the mainstream there is real latitude: through a cover on the Hanafi view, inside a bag on every view, for children learning, and for reciting from memory at any time.
