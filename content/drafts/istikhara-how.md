# How do I pray istikhara and what happens after?

## Answer

**Two rak'ahs, then a du'a, when a decision is in front of you. Afterwards you
decide the ordinary way — there is no dream to wait for.**

**The hadith it rests on.**

Jabir's report in al-Bukhari is the whole basis: **the Prophet used to teach us
istikhara in all our affairs as he taught us a sura of the Qur'an** [1]. The
instruction is short: **when one of you has resolved on a matter, let him pray
two rak'ahs, then say: O God, I seek Your choosing by Your knowledge, and I seek
Your decreeing by Your power** [2]. It ends with the line that names its subject:
**and decree good for me wherever it may be, then make me content with it — and
he names his need** [3].

**How it is prayed.**

Al-Nawawi states it as a sunna: **the istikhara prayer is a sunna, and it is that
whoever intends some matter prays two rak'ahs with the intention of the istikhara
prayer, then makes the du'a** [4]. He adds a recommendation on the recitation:
**it is recommended that he recite in the first rak'ah after al-Fatiha "Say: O
disbelievers", and in the second "Say: He is God, One" — then he proceeds after
the istikhara to whatever his heart is opened to** [5].

On where the du'a goes, Ibn Taymiyya was asked and answered: **the du'a is
permitted in the istikhara prayer and others, before the salam and after it, and
before the salam is better** [6]. Ibn Hajar notes why the prayer comes first at
all: **the wisdom in putting the prayer before the du'a is that what is intended
by istikhara is obtaining both the good of this world and of the next** [7] — you
knock on the door before you ask.

**It is not obligatory, and the timing matters.**

Ibn Hajar draws from the words *other than the obligatory prayer* that **the
command to pray the two rak'ahs of istikhara is not binding** [8]. He also holds
that **the prayer and the du'a should occur after the intention toward the matter
has formed** [9] — istikhara is for a decision you are actually facing.

**What happens after.**

Al-Nawawi's sentence above is the one to hold onto: *then he proceeds to whatever
his heart is opened to.* Not a dream, not a sign, not a colour, not a feeling
that arrives overnight. None of the passages here mention dreams at all, or
describe waiting for one.

You pray, you ask, and then you decide the ordinary way — weigh it, take advice,
and go with what settles. If it goes well, that was the answer. If it does not,
that was also the answer, and the closing words of the du'a were a request to be
made content with it. Nothing here forbids repeating the istikhara, and nothing
here sets a number.

**One clarification**, since it worries people. On the phrase *I seek Your
decreeing*, Ibn Hajar explains: **I ask You to give me power over it — and what
is meant by the decreeing is the making-easy** [10]. You are not asking to be
shown the future. You are asking for the good outcome to be made possible and
made smooth.
