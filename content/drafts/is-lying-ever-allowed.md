# Is lying ever allowed?

## Answer

Yes, in narrow cases — and the scholars disagreed sharply about how narrow.

**The hadith everything turns on.**

Umm Kulthum bint 'Uqba heard the Prophet ﷺ say: "He is not a liar who reconciles between people, and says good, and conveys good." Ibn Shihab, who transmits it, adds: "I did not hear any dispensation given in anything of what people call lying except in three: war, reconciling between people, and a man's speech to his wife and a woman's speech to her husband" [1].

Note the structure. The Prophet's ﷺ own words are the first sentence; the list of three is the transmitter's summary of what he heard — though other routes of the hadith carry the list within the report itself [2], and Asma' bint Yazid's hadith at al-Tirmidhi gives it as marfu': "Lying is not lawful except in three: a man's speech to his wife to please her, lying in war, and in reconciling between people" [3].

**The disagreement: does "lying" here mean actual lying?**

This is the live question and both answers have serious backing.

*Position one — real lying is permitted in these three.* Al-Qadi 'Iyad: "there is no disagreement on the permissibility of lying in these cases, but they differed as to what the permitted lying in them is. One group said it is on its unrestricted sense, and they permitted saying what was not the case in these places for a benefit; and they said the blameworthy lie is the one containing harm" [4]. Their evidence is prophetic precedent: "they argued with Ibrahim's words *rather, the biggest of them did it* and *I am sick*, and his words *she is my sister*, and the words of Yusuf's herald *O caravan, you are surely thieves*. They said: and there is no disagreement that if an oppressor intends to kill a man who is hidden with him, lying that he does not know where he is becomes *obligatory* upon him" [5].

*Position two — only *tawriya*, never outright falsehood.* "Others, among them al-Tabari, said lying is not permissible in anything at all. They said: what has come of permission in this — what is meant by it is *tawriya* and the use of ambiguous speech, not explicit lying" [6]. Al-Nawawi shows how that works in practice: "that he promise his wife that he will treat her well and clothe her with such-and-such, intending *if Allah decrees that*. The upshot is that he brings words that bear more than one meaning, from which the addressee understands what settles his heart" [7].

Al-Nawawi's own verdict, and Ibn Hajar quotes it: "the apparent view is the permissibility of actual lying in the three matters — but indirect speech is better" [8]. Ibn 'Abidin comes down the other way: "al-Tahawi and others said it is carried onto ambiguous speech, because outright lying is forbidden. I say: and that is the truth" [9].

So: you have a real choice of school here. What both agree on is that *tawriya* is safer and better, and that the three headings are the outer limit.

**The rule that makes this usable.**

Ibn 'Abidin transmits al-Ghazali's criterion, and it is the most practical sentence in this whole subject: "know that lying may be permitted and may be obligatory. The criterion in it… is that every praiseworthy objective that can be reached by truthfulness and lying together — lying in it is forbidden; and if it can be reached by lying alone, then it is permitted if attaining that objective is permitted, and obligatory if attaining it is obligatory — as when he sees a protected person hidden from an oppressor who intends to kill him or harm him: lying here is obligatory" [10].

Read the first clause again, because it disposes of almost every case people actually ask about. **If the truth would also have worked, the lie is forbidden.** Lying to avoid mild embarrassment, to get out of an invitation, to smooth over lateness — the truth was available. That is not one of the three.

Ibn 'Abidin continues: "and likewise if he asks him about a deposit he intends to seize, denying it is obligatory. And whenever the objective of war, or reconciling between estranged parties, or winning over the heart of one wronged, cannot be completed except by lying — it is permitted" [11].

And the weighing rule, with a clear preference for taking the hit yourself: "one should set the harm of lying against the harm that follows from truthfulness. If the harm of truthfulness is greater, he may lie; and if the reverse, or if he doubts, it is forbidden. If it concerns himself, it is recommended that he not lie; and if it concerns another, indulgence is not permitted at the expense of another's right — and the prudent course is to leave it wherever it is permitted" [12].

*Leave it wherever it is permitted.* The dispensation exists; taking it is not the default.

**The limits inside the three.**

Between spouses, the licence covers warmth and generosity of expression, not deception about rights. Al-Nawawi: "as for his lying to his wife and hers to him, what is meant is in showing affection and promising what is not binding and the like. But as for deceiving in withholding what is owed by him or by her, or taking what is not his or hers — that is forbidden by the consensus of the Muslims" [13].

That line does a great deal of work. "I love this dinner" is inside it. Hiding money, concealing an affair, or lying about what you spent is not — it is on the far side of a consensus.

On war, al-Nawawi records al-Tabari again: "what is permitted of lying in war is ambiguous speech, not real lying, for that is not lawful" — then gives his own view: "the apparent view is the permissibility of the very substance of lying, but confining oneself to indirect speech is better" [14]. Ibn al-'Arabi notes that this exception is purely scriptural: "lying in war is among the excepted cases permitted by the text, as a mercy to the Muslims for their need of it — and reason has no scope in it; had the prohibition of lying been by reason, it would never have turned lawful" [15].

**Two things that are not lying at all.**

Ordinary exaggeration: "customary hyperbole is not of the category of lying — like *I have come to you a thousand times* — because the intent is to convey abundance, not the number of times. But if he has come only once, then he is a liar" [16]. So idiom and emphasis are fine; a specific false claim dressed as emphasis is not.

Poetry, by the same reasoning, since the audience knows it is craft.

**One category with no exception at all.**

Lying about the Prophet ﷺ. Al-Nawawi: "the enormity of the prohibition of lying about him ﷺ — it is a grave obscenity and a destroying major sin" [17], and the consequence for a transmitter is total: "whoever lies about the Messenger of Allah ﷺ deliberately in a single hadith becomes a *fasiq*, all his narrations are rejected, and argument by all of them is void" [18].

Which reaches further than it looks. Forwarding a "hadith" you have not checked, and attaching *the Prophet said* to something warm and plausible you saw online, is the modern shape of this. If you do not know it is authentic, do not attribute it to him.

**In short.** The presumption is truth, and it is heavy. Three narrow exceptions exist. Before reaching for one, ask al-Ghazali's question: *could the truth have achieved this too?* If yes, you are not in an exception. If no, prefer wording that is true but incomplete over wording that is false. And if the lie protects only your own comfort rather than someone else's life, safety or reconciliation, the better course is to tell the truth and bear it.
