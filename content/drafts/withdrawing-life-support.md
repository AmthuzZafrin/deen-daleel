# Can life support be withdrawn?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

> Reviewer note: Turns on a medical determination the corpus has no notion of. What is citable is the duty to seek treatment and its limits, and the material on accepting a decree. Explain what it turns on; route to a scholar and the treating doctors. Do not rule.


## Answer

**This turns on a medical judgement the corpus has no notion of, and this page will not rule on it.** No author here had ventilators, so none of them faced the question that makes this hard: whether a machine is treating a person or postponing a death that has already begun. What they do give is a framework — a duty to seek treatment, a limit on that duty, and a firm view that the moment of death is not ours to set — and the shape of that framework is genuinely useful for the conversation you are about to have.

**Seeking treatment is commanded, not merely tolerated.**

`قالت الأعراب: يا رسول الله ألا نتداوى؟ قال: نعم، يا عباد الله تداووا،` [1] — the bedouin asked, shall we not seek treatment? He said: yes, servants of God, seek treatment.

That is worth stating first, because families are sometimes told that accepting treatment shows weak trust. This tradition does not say so; it says the opposite.

**But the same report marks a limit.**

Another wording adds an exception: `فإن الله لم يضع داء إلا وضع له شفاء، أو قال: دواء إلا داء واحدا. قالوا: يا رسول الله وما هو؟ قال: الهرم.` [2] — for God has placed no illness without placing a cure for it, save one illness. They said: and what is it? He said: old age. Al-Tirmidhi: `وهذا حديث حسن صحيح` [3].

A category of decline for which there is no cure, named inside the very hadith that commands treatment. The duty to treat is a duty to treat what can be treated. It was never a duty to prevent death.

**And death itself is not the thing being decided.**

`وما كان لنفس أن تموت إلا بإذن الله كتابا مؤجلا` [4] — no soul dies except by God's leave, at a term appointed.

Read it carefully in both directions, because families read it in only one. It does not mean that a machine may be switched off casually, since actions have consequences and intention matters. But it also means that nobody in the room is holding a person in life. The term is not being set by the ventilator, and it will not be set by you.

**Why this page cannot go further.**

The question that decides your case is whether continuing treatment is *treating* — whether there is a person to be restored, and a realistic prospect of it — or whether the intervention is now only prolonging the process of dying. That is a factual, medical determination. These texts do not contain it and cannot supply it, and the answer differs between two patients on identical machines.

There is also a distinction the tradition takes very seriously that gets blurred here: between acting to end a life and ceasing an intervention that is achieving nothing. The material on the first is severe and is set out in the separate answer on assisted dying. The second is a different question, and the severity of the first does not automatically transfer to it.

**What these books do not reach.**

Nothing here addresses brain death, or how death is to be determined at all when circulation is maintained mechanically. Nothing addresses the difference between withholding an intervention and withdrawing one already begun, which many jurists and doctors treat as significant. Nothing addresses artificial nutrition and hydration, often argued separately from ventilation. Nothing addresses who decides — patient, next of kin, doctors, a court — nor advance directives, nor how to weigh cost and the needs of other patients. Nothing addresses organ donation, which has its own answer here.

**What to do, concretely.**

Get the two conversations into one room rather than holding them separately:

- Ask the medical team, plainly: is there any realistic prospect of recovery, or is this now prolonging dying? Ask what they would expect over days and weeks, not in principle.
- Ask whether the proposal is to stop something futile, or to do something that causes death. These are different acts and the answer differs.
- Ask a scholar who follows your school, and give them the medical answer to the first question, because their ruling depends on it.
- Ask what will be done for the person's comfort either way. Relief of suffering is not in question in any of this and should not be traded against anything.

Two things to say to whoever is carrying this. Wanting the suffering to end is not wanting the person to die, and the tradition nowhere treats that wish as a fault. And on [4], the decision in front of you is smaller than it feels — you are deciding about a machine, not about a term that was appointed before any of this began.
