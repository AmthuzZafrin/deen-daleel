# Is an AI girlfriend or companion app haram?

> **This question is marked `expect: decline`.** The answer must explain
> why it needs a person — a mufti, a doctor, a lawyer — and must not give
> a ruling. Cite only to show what the question touches on.

> Reviewer note: No author here imagined it. What is citable is the material on where desire is directed and on what a marriage is for; none of it addresses a correspondent who does not exist. Explain what it turns on; do not rule.


## Answer

**No author in these books considered a correspondent who does not exist, and this page will not pretend one did.** There is no ruling here to report on AI companions, and manufacturing one out of texts written about people would be the thing this whole site exists to avoid. What the corpus does have is a body of material about where attention and desire are allowed to go — and it turns out that some of it transfers and some of it very clearly does not. Knowing which is which is what makes the question askable.

**What does not transfer, and it is most of what people reach for.**

The obvious texts are about *persons*, and they stop working when there is no person. `لا يخلون رجل بامرأة فان ثالثهما الشيطان` [1] — no man is alone with a woman but that Satan is the third of them. That is a rule about seclusion, and seclusion has two parties. An app has one.

The same is true of everything built on rights and consequences between people — the harm of a woman's reputation, the claims of a spouse, the parentage of a child, the possibility of zina at all. Every one of those presupposes someone on the other side. Quoting them at a chatbot is a category error, and it would be an easy way to sound decisive while saying nothing true.

**What does transfer, because it was never about the other person.**

The gaze material is framed around what looking does to *you*. `Tell the believing men to lower their gaze and be modest. That is purer for them. Lo! Allah is aware of what they do.` [2]

Ibn Kathir reads it as a command about what is looked at, full stop: `هذا أمر من الله تعالى لعباده المؤمنين أن يغضوا من أبصارهم عما حرم عليهم، فلا ينظروا إلا إلى ما أباح لهم النظر إليه` [3]. The reason given in the verse is `ذلك أزكى لهم` — *purer for them* — not that the person looked at was wronged. That is an argument about the state of the one looking, and it does not obviously need a real subject on the other end.

The famous guidance about the accidental glance points the same way: `لا تتبع النظرة النظرة، فإن لك الأولى وليس لك الآخرة` [4] — do not follow a look with a second look; the first is yours and the second is not. And `فإن اتفق أن وقع البصر على محرم من غير قصد، فليصرف بصره عنه سريعا` [5] — if the eye falls on something forbidden unintentionally, turn it away quickly. Both are about a habit of attention, not about an injured party.

Qatada's gloss on `خائنة الأعين` — `من النظر إلى ما نهي عنه` [6], looking at what one was forbidden — describes something happening inside a person, and al-Hasan's advice on unavoidable sights was simply `اصرف بصرك عنهن` [7].

And the six-fold undertaking joins the eyes to the tongue and the hands as a single discipline: `اكفلوا لي بست أكفل لكم بالجنة: إذا حدث أحدكم فلا يكذب، وإذا اؤتمن فلا يخن، وإذا وعد فلا يخلف. وغضوا أبصاركم، وكفوا أيديكم، واحفظوا فروجكم` [8].

**So what is the actual question.**

It is this: is the concern in these texts the *wrong done to another person*, or the *formation of the one who looks and wants*? If the first, an AI companion escapes much of it. If the second, it does not escape any of it, and the absence of a real person may make it worse rather than better, since nothing external limits it.

The gaze verses, on their own stated reason, lean towards the second. But nobody in this corpus was asked, and there is a genuine difference between a text leaning somewhere and a text ruling on something. A qualified scholar can carry that reasoning; this page should not.

**What these books also do not reach.**

Nothing here addresses whether an entirely fictional persona is different from a generated likeness of a real person — the latter raises the separate questions covered by the answers on generated images and on impersonation. Nothing addresses a companion app used for loneliness or practice with no romantic or sexual content. Nothing addresses one used by someone married, which raises questions of the spouse's rights that these texts *do* speak to but were not asked in this form. Nothing addresses the design of the products themselves, which are built to be difficult to stop using.

**What to ask.**

Bring a scholar the facts, not the label:

- Is there sexual or romantic content, or is it conversation?
- Does it depict or imitate a real, identifiable person?
- Are you married, and would your spouse recognise this as a betrayal if they saw it?
- Is it replacing the pursuit of an actual marriage, or filling a gap while you pursue one?
- Would you be able to stop this week if you decided to?

That last question is not a legal test and no book here poses it. But if the answer is no, you have learned the useful thing about your situation before any ruling arrives, and the material on guarding the eyes at [4] and [8] is describing a discipline you already know you have lost.
