# Do I pay zakat on shares or investments?

## Answer

Usually yes — but how much you pay depends on why you hold them, and that distinction is older than the stock market.

The classical category that shares fall into is *'urud al-tijara*, trade goods: things you own in order to sell at a profit rather than to use. Al-Fatawa al-Hindiyya states the rule plainly: **zakat is obligatory on trade goods, whatever they may be, once their value reaches a nisab of silver or gold — thus in al-Hidaya** [1]. Note what is being taxed. Not the object, but its **value**: **the value is reckoned at the turning of the year** [2]. Al-Nawawi puts it the same way: **when the year turns over trade goods, valuing them in order to pay zakat becomes obligatory** [3].

**This is why shares are not an exotic case.** You do not ask what a share *is*. You ask what it was worth on your zakat date, and pay 2.5% of that.

**The distinction that matters: the trader and the holder.**

Malik draws a line the classical texts return to repeatedly — between the man who turns his capital over and the man who buys and sits. For the trader: **the settled practice with us regarding trade goods that are turned over is that when a man has paid zakat on his wealth and then buys with it goods, cloth or slaves or the like, and sells it before a year has passed over it, he does not pay zakat on that wealth until a year has passed from the day he paid zakat on it** [4] — his stock is valued at his annual date, whatever it happens to be sitting in.

For the holder, Ibn 'Abd al-Barr reports al-Layth agreeing with Malik: **if he buys goods for trade and they stay with him for years and then he sells them, he owes only a single zakat — exactly the same as Malik's view** [5]. One payment on the proceeds, not one for every year the asset sat there.

**Applied to a portfolio:**

- **Shares bought to trade** — you buy and sell, you watch the price, you intend to exit at a profit: this is the merchant's stock. Value the holding at market price on your zakat date and pay 2.5% of the whole thing.
- **Shares held long-term for dividends,** never intended for resale: this is the holder's case, and the classical reasoning gives you a lighter position — zakat on the dividends as they arrive and join your cash, with the majority contemporary view still asking for an annual payment on the underlying. Both readings are defensible from the texts above.

**Everything counts together.** Al-Jassas records the Shafi'i rule for a mixed pool: **al-Shafi'i said of a man who has a hundred dirhams and trade goods worth a hundred dirhams, that zakat is obligatory on him — so he joined the goods to the hundred despite the difference of the two kinds** [6]. Cash, gold, business stock and shares are one pool tested against one nisab, not four separate tests each of which you might fall below.

**On a gain between valuing and paying:** al-Nawawi notes the schools split. **If he valued it and then sold it for more than its value before paying the zakat, there are two views** [7] — one that the increase came after the obligation attached and is not itself zakatable, one that it is. Either answer is within the tradition; do not agonise over it.

Where the classical texts genuinely run out: a share is not a sack of cloth. It is a fractional claim on a company that itself holds cash, machinery, debts and property in some mix, and the widespread contemporary method — paying on your proportionate share of the company's *zakatable* assets rather than the market price — is a modern derivation from the reasoning above rather than something the sources state. It is a reasonable derivation. But if you hold a large portfolio, or index funds where you cannot see the underlying, that calculation is worth putting to a living scholar rather than doing from first principles.
