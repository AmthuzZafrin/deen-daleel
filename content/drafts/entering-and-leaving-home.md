# What do I say entering and leaving the house?

## Answer

**Leaving:** *Bismillah, tawakkaltu 'ala Allah, la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah* — in the name of Allah, I rely upon Allah, there is no power and no strength except by Allah.

**Entering:** say *bismillah*, and greet whoever is home — and if nobody is home, greet anyway.

**Going out.**

Anas reported the Prophet ﷺ saying: "Whoever says — meaning when he leaves his house — *Bismillah, tawakkaltu 'ala Allah, la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah*, it is said to him: You are sufficed, you are protected, and Shaytan withdraws from him. This is a *hasan gharib* hadith; we know it only from this route" [1].

There is a fuller version explaining the three phrases. Abu Hurayra: "When a man goes out of the door of his house — or the door of his dwelling — two angels are appointed with him" [2]; and then, phrase by phrase: "they say: *You are guided*. And when he says *la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah*, they say: *You are protected*. And when he says *tawakkaltu 'ala Allah*, they say: *You are sufficed*" [3]; "then his two companions [from among the devils] meet him and say: What do you two want from a man who has been guided, sufficed and protected?" [4].

Be aware of the grading. Ibn Majah's editor notes of that chain: "in its chain is Harun b. Harun b. 'Abdullah, and he is weak" [5]. The Tirmidhi version above is *hasan gharib* [1]. So the practice is well enough established; the vivid detail about the angels rests on a weaker chain.

**A second supplication for leaving**, from Umm Salama — and here al-Nasa'i works out the chain in front of you, rejecting the first route and giving the sound one: the Prophet ﷺ "when he went out of his house would say: *O Allah, I seek refuge in You from slipping or being led astray, from wronging or being wronged, from behaving ignorantly or being treated ignorantly*" [6]. Another route combines both: "when he went out of his house he would say: *Bismillah, tawakkaltu 'ala Allah. O Allah, we seek refuge in You from slipping or being led astray, from wronging or being wronged, from behaving ignorantly or being treated ignorantly*" [7].

Notice what that one asks for. Not safety from traffic — safety from being in the wrong, and from being wronged, in the day you are walking into.

**Coming in.**

Jabir reported: "When a man enters his house and mentions Allah on entering and at his meal, Shaytan says: *No lodging for you and no dinner.* And when he enters and does not mention Allah on entering, Shaytan says: *You have got your lodging.* And when he does not mention Allah at his meal, he says: *You have got your lodging and your dinner*" [8].

Ibn Hajar reads it as a real exclusion — "the mention of Allah's name comes between him and doing these things" — while recording Ibn Daqiq al-'Id's caution that the report shows the barring of the Shaytan *outside*, and does not by itself say anything about one already within: "so that would be a lessening of the harm, not its removal" [9].

Abu Malik al-Ash'ari transmits a supplication that covers both directions at once: "When a man enters his house let him say: *O Allah, I ask You for the good of the entering and the good of the going out. In the name of Allah we have entered and in the name of Allah we have gone out, and upon Allah our Lord we have relied.* Then let him greet his household" [10].

Al-Qurtubi also passes on Ibn al-'Arabi's simpler recommendation: "when you enter your house it is recommended for you to remember Allah, by saying: *ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah*" [11].

**Greet your own family.**

This is the part most often skipped. Al-Qurtubi: "all these rulings are only about a house that is not yours. As for your house, which you live in — if your wife is in it, there is no seeking permission upon her, except that you greet when you enter. Qatada said: when you enter your house, greet your family, for they have the most right of those you greet" [12].

*They have the most right of those you greet.* If you say salam to strangers at the mosque and walk past your own household in silence, that is exactly backwards.

**And if the house is empty?**

Al-Qurtubi reports Qatada: "when you enter a house in which there is nobody, say *as-salamu 'alayna wa 'ala 'ibad Allah al-salihin* — for that is commanded. He said: and it has been mentioned to us that the angels return it to them" [13].

Al-Qushayri gives the tidiest rule, covering all three cases: "the more evident view is that this is general for entering every house. If there is a Muslim resident in it, he says *as-salamu 'alaykum wa rahmatullahi wa barakatuh*; and if there is no resident in it, he says *as-salamu 'alayna wa 'ala 'ibad Allah al-salihin*" [14] — and where the household is not Muslim, *as-salamu 'ala man ittaba'a al-huda*, peace be upon whoever follows guidance.

Not everyone agreed on the empty-house case; Ibn al-'Arabi held that greeting is not required there. Al-Qurtubi's own verdict is short: "Qatada's position is good."

**Other people's houses: ask first.**

"O ye who believe! Enter not houses other than your own without first announcing your presence and invoking peace upon the folk thereof. That is better for you, that ye may be heedful" (24:27) [15].

And this extends further into the family than people expect. Al-Qurtubi: "Ibn al-Qasim said: Malik said — a man seeks permission of his mother and his sister when he wishes to enter upon them. And 'Ata' b. Yasar related that a man said to the Prophet ﷺ: Shall I seek permission of my mother? He said: Yes. He said: But I serve her. He said: Seek permission of her. He came back to him three times. He said: Would you like to see her naked? He said: No. He said: Then seek permission of her" [16].

For the household you live with, al-Qurtubi passes on the practical version: "clear your throat and stamp your foot so that they notice you coming in" [12]. A knock, or saying salam loudly from the hallway, does the same job.

**In short.**

Going out: *Bismillah, tawakkaltu 'ala Allah, la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah.*
Coming in: *Bismillah* — then *as-salamu 'alaykum* to whoever is there, or *as-salamu 'alayna wa 'ala 'ibad Allah al-salihin* to an empty flat.
Someone else's door: knock, and don't walk in until you're asked to.

None of this is obligatory, and none of it takes more than a few seconds. What it does is mark the two moments when your day changes shape — walking out into what you cannot control, and coming back to the people who are yours.
